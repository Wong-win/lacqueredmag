The verdict, stated early, because a review that withholds its conclusion is an advertisement. Four of the five steps of this face survived a full working day; the fifth, the lip, did not. That is not a verdict on the products but on a category, and the category is the lipstick — the only one of the five steps engineered to be noticed, and therefore the only one engineered to fail in public. We spent a Tuesday with a $46 tinted moisturizer, a $38 cream bronzer, a $24 blush, a $19.50 brow pencil and a $42 lipstick, and photographed the result at the first hour, the fifth and the ninth: office light, a commute, a lunch, and finally a video call with the camera on. A face that survives a bathroom mirror and a selfie has not survived a working day.

The test subject is a 34-year-old editor with combination skin: an oilier T-zone, drier cheeks, no actives beyond a nightly retinol. The camera is a phone at 1x, unfiltered, with no beauty mode — beauty mode is where most of the faces on the internet live, and it is not a face. Photographs were taken at 8:41, 1:40 and 5:36, and again under a borrowed flash unit at the end of the day, because the flash is where the truth lives. Nothing was retouched, and the lip was reapplied once, at the seventh hour.

## the five steps

The five steps are the base, the complexion, the brow and eye, the cheek, and the lip. The order is the order in which a face fails when you get it wrong: skin first, because everything else sits on it; warmth second, because a face without warmth photographs as a mask; structure third, because the brow does the work of expression; the mouth last, because it is the only step finished by other people — by cups, by napkins, and the phone pressed to a cheek during a call.

**Step one — the base.** A $46 tinted moisturizer: Merit's Skin Tinted SPF 50, applied with the fingers. The base is the tint and the sunscreen in one layer — the most useful idea in the modern makeup bag: coverage that evens without masking is what lets every step above it be light. The failure mode is not wear; it is physics. Pilling began the moment a second, heavier sunscreen went over the tint within two minutes — the mineral filters catch on the film the first layer leaves and lift in flakes beside the nose. Under flash, the titanium dioxide in a 50 SPF turns the centre of the face faintly ashen.

**Step two — the complexion.** A $38 cream bronzer: NARS, two shades deeper than the neck. Cream rather than powder, and warm rather than cool, because warmth is lighting and lighting is what a camera is looking for. The failure mode is separation. A cream sitting on a base that has begun to break up migrates — off the temples, along the hairline, into the creases beside the nose — and by hour five the face carries a grey cast at the jaw and a warm patch above the ear. Under flash it reads dustier than the eye expects, because a photographed face reflects forty per cent more than a real one.

**Step three — the brow and the eye.** A $19.50 brow pencil: Merit's, waterproof, one shade deeper than the natural brow. A pencil rather than a gel, because the brow carries expression on camera and a gel needs a spoolie, a clean hand and ninety seconds a commuting face does not have. The eye stays unlined, unmascaraed and unshadowed: this is a five-step face, and a separate eye step is over budget. The failure mode is the tail. The pencil does not smudge, which is its virtue, but the brow fades from the inside outward, so by hour nine the tail is gone and the brow reads short and faintly surprised.

**Step four — the cheek.** A $24 blush: Glossier's Cloud Paint, tapped onto the apples with the fingers. Cream rather than powder, because over a thin base cream melts into the skin and reads as blood under it rather than colour on top, while powder over a cream base goes patchy and sits. The failure mode is duration. The blush was the first thing to leave the face and the last to be missed, and by hour nine the cheek was the colour it began, which is to say the natural colour was doing all the work.

**Step five — the lip.** A $42 lipstick: Charlotte Tilbury's Pillow Talk, one full coat, blotted once against a tissue. A bullet rather than a gloss or a stain, because a satin bullet is the fastest route to a face that reads as finished, and the lip is what a camera studies when it decides whether the person is paying attention. The failure mode is transfer, and it is the whole story of the lipstick. It printed onto a paper cup within the first hour; by hour nine the colour had retreated to the vermillion border — the specific and unflattering shape of a lipstick that has been used up.

## hour one

At 8:41 the face is at its best, and the photograph proves nothing, because every one of these five products was designed to look exactly like this for the first ten minutes of its life. The base is seamless apart from one patch of pilling beside the nose, where the second sunscreen went on too quickly. The cream bronzer is invisible as a product and visible as a purpose: warmth across the upper cheekbone. The brow is crisp, a shade deeper than the natural brow, because a brow on camera needs two shades of something. The blush reads as a flush rather than as a colour. The lip is opaque at the centre and sheer at the edges — what a satin bullet does at hour one, and the reason so many people believe the lipstick is holding. It is not holding. It is simply new.

## hour five

At 1:40, after the underground and a sandwich eaten at a desk, the face has begun to have an opinion about the day. The base has moved: a faint shadow along the nasolabial fold where the mouth has been all morning, and the pilling has spread into dry texture beside the nose, repaired with a single blot — no powder, because powder is a commitment to a face you did not plan on. The cream bronzer has separated, the grey at the jaw now legible in the office photograph, which is 4000 kelvin and overhead and, in most cases, forgiving of nothing. The brow is losing its tail. The blush is where it should be, muted by the oil the face has produced, and it has left a print on the phone. The lip is where lipstick goes at hour five: a ring of colour at the vermillion border, a bare centre, and a mark on the inside of the coffee cup. That mark is the moment a lipstick stops being a product and becomes a decision.

## what survived

The ninth hour decides it. At 5:36 the face has been through a screen call with the camera on, and the flash photograph taken afterwards is the harshest evidence we collected. The base survives, with the caveat that the flash turns the centre of the face faintly ash — the price of buying a 50 SPF and a tint in one tube. The blush is gone. The brow is a memory. The lip is a ring and a photograph of a lip.

The scoreboard, plainly: the base at eighty per cent in daylight and seventy under flash; the complexion at forty, degraded into a stain rather than a contour; the brow at sixty, if you accept a shorter brow than you started with; the cheek at thirty, which is to say the natural colour of the skin is doing the work; the lip at fifteen, and no amount of blotting will take it higher. Four steps survive, one does not, and the one that fails is the one the industry spends the most money telling us is permanent.

What we would change is straightforward. Apply the sunscreen first, wait two minutes, and the pilling never starts. Skip the cream bronzer if a flash is anywhere in your day: less warmth, no grey at the jaw, no flashback. Keep the pencil, carry it, and expect to redraw the tail — at $19.50 it is the cheapest step and the second most useful. And be honest about the lip: a $42 bullet is an event product, and the industry will never say so, because it sells the fiction that a lipstick lasts until it is removed. A working-day lip is a stain and a bullet together, with the bullet reapplied around the seventh hour. The routine as tested costs $169.50 and eleven minutes: the most expensive step does the most work, the cheapest does the second-most, and the most expensive thing you own is the least likely to still be on your face at lunch. That is, in our view, a fair summary of the industry.

## the verdict

We would keep this face. Four steps out of five is a better record than the industry implies is available; the failure belongs not to the products but to the one category that refuses to admit it is a category of occasion rather than of day. The five-step face survives contact with real light, which is more than the six-step face and the entire skinification of the industry manage. What the nine hours taught us is that a working day does not ask a face to be finished; it asks it to be consistent, and consistency is bought with fewer steps, thinner formulas and a willingness to carry one pencil. A face that lasts is not a face that tries harder. It is a face that does less, more reliably — and, nine hours in, still owes you a lip.
