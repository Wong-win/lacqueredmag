There is an object that has been with us, in some form, for at least three centuries. It is not the lipstick — the lipstick is a recent invention, a twentieth-century moveable feast. It is not the compact mirror — that, too, is comparatively recent. It is the *counter*, or the *vanity*, or the *dressing table*, or, in older forms, the *lacquer box*.

What unites all of these forms is a surface, set off from the rest of the room, dedicated to the woman's act of assembling herself before she was seen. We thought it might be useful, in this first issue, to take a long view of that surface — to ask where it came from, how it has changed, and what its architectural history tells us about the practice we are documenting.

## The 18th century: the toilette

The word *toilette* referred, in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, not to a bathroom in any modern sense but to the small cloth that was spread over a dressing table before the act of dressing the head and face began. By extension, the *toilette* came to mean the dressing ritual itself — the slow morning assembly of a woman of a certain class, in front of a mirror, before the day's business began.

The most luxurious toilettes were public events. Madame de Pompadour received, on certain mornings, while her hairdresser worked. The surface on which all of this occurred was a long, low table, often marble or scagliola, sometimes inlaid with mother-of-pearl, set with mirrors of various sizes (the magnifying mirror being a particular luxury), and surrounded by a vocabulary of small objects: pomanders, cosmetic boxes, ribbon boxes, powder boxes.

Of these, the cosmetic box — small, often portable, sometimes inlaid, usually lacquered on the outside and velvet on the inside — is the direct ancestor of the modern vanity. It is an object built for the *carrying* of the small instruments of beauty, and the survival rate among cosmetic boxes from this period is much higher than among full dressing tables; the table was furniture, replaceable, but the box was a private thing, often kept for life and passed down.

## The 19th century: the dressing case

The cosmetically elaborate lady of the early nineteenth century — Regency through early Victorian — travelled with a *dressing case*. These were fitted boxes, often leather-bound, often with brass hardware, containing everything a woman might need to re-do her face on the road: combs, scissors, small mirrors, pots for cream, small vials for scent, sometimes even a small trough for mixing powder.

The dressing case marks the moment in which beauty practice began to be *mobile* — not a private ritual at home but a public act in a hotel room, on a train, in a friend's parlour. The Victorian case is the great-grandparent of the modern train-case vanity; if you have ever seen a velvet-lined box with a swing-out mirror and a fitted tray, you have seen the inheritance.

The cases are also where the *lacquer* tradition in cosmetics first becomes explicit. Many surviving cases carry small pots and compacts whose lids are japanned or painted with flowers, landscapes, or chinoiserie; the inside of a lady's case, opened, was meant to be a small festival of surface. The lacquered cosmetic is older than its twentieth-century industry; the industry has simply rediscovered it.

## The 20th century: the dressing table

The dressing table in the modern sense — a low table with a single mirror, often skirted, often with a velvet bench or a vanity stool — is a creation of the late nineteenth century and reaches its mature form in the 1920s and 1930s. This is the era in which the dressing table acquires its most clearly *theatrical* character: the great glass-topped vanity of the 1930s film star, with its three-panel mirror, its ring of bulbs, its catchment tray for pins, is a piece of dressing-room furniture that has been elevated, almost literally, to a stage.

It is this vanity that we see in the films of the period — the *it* vanity, with its small pots and compacts arranged just so. The vanity is a stage set because the woman who uses it has become, in this period, a *performer*; her face is part of her work, and the stage on which it is assembled has been engineered, for the first time, by professionals.

## The post-war: the bathroom counter

The 1950s and 1960s are the era in which the vanity begins its slow retreat. The bathroom becomes, for the first time in the modern house, the primary site of cosmetic application — a fact that was not true before the war, when the bedroom, the boudoir, and the dressing room were the primary sites. The vanity does not disappear in this period; it migrates. The modern vanity is a bathroom shelf with a mirror and a row of identical pots. The lacquered tradition continues, but in commercial form — Guerlain's *Météorites*, Revlon's compacts with painted lids, Estée Lauder's *Cinnabar* jars.

This is the period that shaped the cosmetic industry as we know it. The dressing table has been displaced, but its logic has been carried forward in the layout of the retail shelf — a long surface, lit from above, dedicated to the face.

## The 21st century: the studio

In the most recent fifteen years, the vanity has fragmented further. The single dressing table has been replaced, for many of us, by a constellation of surfaces: a shelf in the bathroom, a tray on a kitchen counter, a clutch for travel, a small acrylic stand for the most-used brushes. The dedicated dressing room is, statistically, the property of perhaps one in twenty households in the post-industrial west.

What remains, however, is the *logic* of the vanity: a surface set off from the rest of the room, with a mirror, lit in a particular way, dedicated to the act of the face. The Instagram flat-lay is, structurally, a vanity laid out on a background; the YouTube beauty tutorial is filmed at a vanity with three-point lighting. The dressing table has become the *frame*.

## The lacquer box today

We are publishing this magazine from a small room with a single counter, a three-panel mirror, and a vintage mahogany box in which we keep the few instruments that matter most. The lacquered object has not disappeared; it has only become harder to see, in plain sight among the bathroom shelf's many pots.

What we are proposing, in this magazine, is a return to the long view. The vanity is older and stranger than we are taught to think. It is also more central than the industry currently makes room for. The lacquered box, on the counter, is a permanent object — handed down, repaired, repainted. It is the ancestor of the magazine you are now reading.

We hope you will keep us on the counter.
