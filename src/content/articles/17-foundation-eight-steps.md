A foundation counter is a poor place to answer a question about colour. The light is warm and overhead, the mirror is close, and the person holding the bottle is working to a target. Everything a customer needs to know about her own skin she has carried into the shop, and none of it is printed on the box.

The assumption most shoppers bring is that the problem is brand — that somewhere a label has solved the puzzle and the task is to find it. The evidence points elsewhere. Depth is a number; undertone is a hue, and the Fitzpatrick scale, the industry's favourite instrument, was never built to measure either. The two axes are independent, and coverage, finish and texture are format decisions that change neither.

Set that pair correctly and the brand becomes the last decision, not the first. Someone who can name her undertone and her depth can walk into any shop and leave with the right bottle in ninety seconds. The rest of the counter — numbered cards, rituals, laminated charts — exists largely to obscure how small the problem is.

## Step one — the undertone

Dermatology practice defines undertone as the colour below the skin's surface — a hue that will not change if you tan or freckle, and that has nothing to do with how pale or dark you are. That clause makes undertone and depth separate variables.

The scale the industry reaches for instead is Fitzpatrick, and its origin is not flattering. Thomas B. Fitzpatrick built it in 1975, with Pathak, to estimate UV sensitivity and set starting UVA doses for PUVA therapy in psoriasis patients. The original subjects were described as persons with white skin; four categories covered them.

Types V and VI, for brown and black skin, were added later and have been called an afterthought. The scale does not measure melanin: a 2024 review found correlations with measured skin colour from |R| 0.23 to 0.90, with only about 28 percent of studies above 0.75, and called the result a coarse categorisation for a continuum of skin tones.

Clinicians, the same review found, predominantly assign non-white individuals to types IV, V and VI by ethnicity — a practice it calls unreliable — and colour changes in dark-pigmented skin are generally overlooked by visual assessment. A scale built to dose a lamp is being used to sort people into shade families.

The wrist vein test, repeated in every guide, is a parlour heuristic with no validation behind it: blue or purple for cool, green for warm, indeterminate for neutral, offered on a clinic page with no citation and no named clinician. The jawline stripe test replaced it in practice — two or three adjacent shades blended onto the neck, judged against both face and neck.

## Step two — the depth

There is no industry-standard eight-point depth scale. No trade body defines one, and the numbering on the counter is each brand's house dialect — folklore hardened into something shoppers mistake for a measurement. Retail names are just as loose: opal and alabaster sit lighter than a general light, chestnut darker than a general dark.

MAC is the clearest documented system, and it inverts intuition. Its shade finder states that numbers represent depth from 5 to 65, lower lighter and higher deeper, while letters carry undertone. NC reads warm and NW reads cool, with C for cool, W for warm and N for a balance of both.

Because the number is depth and the letter is undertone, the same person can be NC15 in one formula and a different code in another; codes do not travel between brands. Fenty's Pro Filt'r went on sale on 8 September 2017 with forty shades and added ten more in January 2019. Darker shades sold out; Harvey Nichols reported selling one foundation per minute that September.

## Step three — the coverage

Coverage is a pigment load, not a shade. A formulation reference sets sheer below 5 percent pigment, light at 5 to 10, medium at 10 to 15, and full or high above 15. Titanium dioxide, in anatase or rutile form, does most of the work, with black, umber or russet iron oxides for shading.

A second trade source gives different figures, and the two do not reconcile; any published percentage is a house convention rather than a standard. Full coverage is described in the trade literature as corrective or camouflage makeup, formulated for vitiligo, birthmarks and scars rather than everyday wear.

Coverage changes opacity, not match. A sheer and a full-coverage formula in the same depth still suit the same skin, because coverage is a format decision and depth is not. Choosing full coverage does not change your undertone; it makes a mismatch more visible.

## Step four — the finish

Finish names are the loosest vocabulary on the counter. A professional breakdown lists five — matte, velvet, satin, luminous, dewy — with wear windows of ten to fourteen hours for matte, six to ten for velvet, eight to twelve for satin, six to nine for luminous, and two to four good hours for dewy before it reads oily.

Mecca's consumer guide uses four finishes and does not treat luminous as a category, using the word only as a descriptor. The professional breakdown is blunter about natural matte, which it calls marketing language for a velvet finish: oil-absorbing but not aggressively flat. Most modern matte foundations sit closer to velvet than to a true matte.

The mechanisms differ by ingredient. Matte works by absorption — silica, kaolin clay, talc and silica silylate act like tiny sponges, physically absorbing sebum. Dewy works by reflection, with mica's crystalline structure bouncing light off the surface. Satin is defined by dimethicone, which lays down a smooth film and blurs imperfections, with enough mica to glow and silica to stay.

Finish is matched to oiliness, not to taste. Oily skin is steered to matte or velvet and away from dewy; dry skin the reverse; combination skin to satin with zone-by-zone powdering. Matte exaggerates wrinkles on mature skin, and dewy on oily skin blurs into oily by hour four. Sebum mixing also darkens colour, so matte formulas resist a shift that dewy ones invite.

## Step five — the texture

Texture is the variable most often mistaken for a shade problem. Liquid and serum formats carry more water or volatile silicone, produce the thinnest film and largely follow the skin beneath them, which makes them honest to a fault on dry or textured skin: flakes show, patches catch. Cream and stick formats carry more wax, oil and pigment — the heaviest film, the most coverage per pass, the worst creasing, because wax does not reflow.

Powder relies on talc or another absorbent with mica for diffusion, and on deeper skin a pale, cool base can sit on top of the pigment rather than in it, reading ashy. The one texture with a datable origin is the cushion compact: AmorePacific began in January 2007, ran more than 3,600 trials across 200 sponge types, launched the IOPE Air Cushion in March 2008, and by January 2015 had filed 114 patents.

## Step six — the undertone, again

The second pass at undertone is about light, not skin. Metamerism is the phenomenon in which two colours match under one lighting condition and stop matching under another, because their spectral reflectance curves differ even where they appear alike. A match approved in a lab can fail in a retail store, in daylight or under office lighting — the conditions in which foundation is bought and worn.

Codes do not rescue this. Letters are not standardised: C usually means cool, W warm, N neutral, with G for golden and P for pink in some ranges, while MAC's NC reads warm. Daylight checking remains the correction, and no counter can supply it.

## Step seven — the oxidation

Oxidation is a permanent colour shift, caused when pigments — iron oxides especially — react with air and skin oils and move orange, red or deeper brown. It is not the same as wet-to-dry change. A wet swatch usually settles back toward the bottled shade as volatiles evaporate; true oxidation moves away from the bottle and stays there.

The working standard is fifteen full minutes, by which point most formulas have oxidised and settled into their wear colour; very emollient ones may keep deepening for another five to ten. A visible change then signals that the formula is unstable on that skin. The protocol is 0/5/15: a thin unbuffed streak along jaw and neck, photographed at each interval with locked exposure and a strip of white paper for balance.

The popular workaround — buy a shade lighter and let oxidation warm it into place — is attributed advice, not consensus. Makeup artist Lerma told Makeup.com in May 2021 to choose a foundation slightly lighter than the skin tone, because when the product warms to the face and oxidises it will be your perfect shade.

## Step eight — the setting

The order is foundation, then powder, then spray. Powder absorbs oil and locks creams in place; spray binds the layers and reduces a powdery finish. Both, not either — powder for shine control, spray for hold. Application is targeted: T-zone, under the eyes, nose, pressed in with a puff for maximum hold or dusted with a fluffy brush for a lighter veil.

Primer is where the routine fails before it starts. Water-based primer goes under water-based foundation and silicone under silicone; the water formula will pill and separate over the silicone barrier. The ingredients to look for are dimethicone, cyclomethicone, amodimethicone and methicone, and letting each layer set before the next reduces pilling.

The SPF in the bottle is not sunscreen. The test standard is 2 mg/cm², about a quarter-teaspoon for the face, which cosmetic chemist Michelle Wong calculated as thirteen pumps of foundation against the one and a half she normally uses. Dermatologist Lauren Moy is flat about it: you cannot rely on the SPF in foundations.

The counter can test colour and finish. It cannot reproduce your daylight, your skincare layering or your eight-hour sebum output. The fifteen-minute wait and the daylight check are things you do yourself, after you leave, with a phone and a strip of white paper.

Eight steps sound like a system, and a system sounds like something you buy. It is closer to a vocabulary: two variables you can name and six decisions that follow from them, expressed in codes that are shop dialect rather than measurement. Learn the dialect and the brand stops being the first question.
