The foundation is the most counter-intuitive cosmetic in the modern bag — the cosmetic that the buyer spends the most time choosing, and the one she is most likely to get wrong. The woman who has found her foundation is, statistically, the exception.

The foundation depends on more variables than any other cosmetic: undertone, depth, coverage, finish, texture, foundation-undertone, oxidation, and setting. Eight variables. One product.

The eight-step process below is, in our view, the order in which the variables are most usefully tackled — not the order the counter will use. The counter starts with depth. Depth is, in fact, the *last* thing to settle, since it depends on undertone, coverage, and oxidation, none of which the counter is usually testing.

We are proposing a different order.

## Step one — the undertone

The undertone is the colour *under* the skin, not the surface colour. The surface colour changes with the season, with the sun, with the cold, with the foundation you are currently wearing. The undertone does not change. The undertone is, in most cases, the same colour your great-grandmother had.

There are three undertones: warm (yellow or golden), cool (pink or red), and neutral (both, in roughly equal measure). The undertone is best read at the jawline, in natural light, on a bare face. The undertone is best read by someone other than yourself — the self, looking in the mirror, is biased by years of having looked at the surface.

The undertone is, in our view, the most important variable — and the one the counter is, in most cases, getting wrong. Once the undertone is wrong, every other variable becomes impossible to fix.

## Step two — the depth

The depth is the *darkness* or *lightness* of the skin. The depth is what the counter, in most cases, starts with. The depth is, in our view, the variable that should be settled *after* the undertone.

The depth is measured on a scale. There are several scales. The most common, in the contemporary foundation market, is a numerical scale from 1 (very fair) to 50 (very deep). The scale is not standardised across brands. The depth 20 in one brand is not, in most cases, the depth 20 in another. The depth, in our view, is best settled by *swatching* — by testing the foundation on the jaw, in natural light, on a day when the rest of the face is bare.

The depth is, also, in our view, the variable the wearer is most likely to *want to be wrong about*. The wearer will, in most cases, want to be one shade lighter than she is. The wearer will, in most cases, be wrong. The depth that matches the undertone at the jawline is the depth that will, in the actual lighting of the day, look right. The depth one shade lighter will, in the actual lighting of the day, look grey.

## Step three — the coverage

The coverage is the *amount* of the skin the foundation is expected to hide. The coverage is, in most cases, described on the packaging in words — *sheer*, *light*, *medium*, *full* — but more usefully described by what the wearer wants the foundation to *do*.

There are, in our view, three coverages that are worth considering. The first is coverage that *evens out* — that reduces the redness of the cheek, the discolouration under the eye, the old spot on the chin, to the same surface colour as the surrounding skin. The second is coverage that *replaces* — that covers the skin to the point that the skin beneath is, in the photograph, invisible. The third is coverage that *highlights* — that uses the foundation to bring out specific features, in a way that requires the rest of the skin to be visible.

The first is, in most cases, the right coverage. The second is, in our view, almost always wrong. The third is, in our view, almost always more effort than it is worth.

## Step four — the finish

The finish is the *surface quality* of the foundation once it has set. The finish is, in most cases, described on the packaging as one of four options: matte, satin, natural, or luminous.

The matte finish is, in our view, best reserved for the camera. On actual skin, in actual light, the matte finish often looks heavy or *powdered*. The matte foundation is also, in many cases, the finish that ages the worst through the day — settling into the lines it was meant to hide by mid-afternoon.

The satin finish is, in our view, the most useful finish for everyday wear. The satin has a slight sheen, without being a *glow*. The satin photographs well. The satin ages well. The satin is, in most cases, the finish that suits the widest range of skin.

The natural finish is, in our view, a finish that should be approached with caution. In many formulations, the natural finish is the finish that wants to be a satin but has been over-formulated — it looks, on the skin, like a foundation that has been *marketed* to look natural.

The luminous finish is, in our view, best reserved for the evening or for the camera. The luminous finish is, on the everyday face, often a finish that looks *wet*, and in many formulations requires a powder to set, which makes the powder the *real* finish.

## Step five — the texture

The texture is the *physical form* of the foundation: in most cases, liquid, cream, stick, or powder.

The liquid is the most versatile and the easiest to apply. The liquid is also, in most cases, the easiest to over-apply. The cream is more forgiving than the liquid but more difficult to blend — the texture that suits the drier skin best. The stick is the most portable but also the most likely to be applied too heavily — the texture that suits the oily skin best.

The powder is, in our view, almost never the right texture. The powder sits on top of the skin rather than blending into it, and by the end of the day has settled into a different surface than the rest of the face. The powder should be reserved for the *setting* of another foundation, not for the foundation itself.

## Step six — the undertone, again

The undertone of the *foundation* is not the same as the undertone of the skin. The undertone of the foundation is what the foundation *adds* to the skin — warm, cool, or neutral, with further variation toward pink, yellow, olive, or red.

The undertone of the foundation is, in our view, the most over-considered variable in the foundation market. Contemporary foundations are marketed with *warm-neutral*, *cool-neutral*, or *true-neutral* on the label. The labels are, in most cases, more confusing than helpful. The undertone is best settled by swatching on the jaw and walking to a window.

The undertone of the foundation is, in our view, almost always *cooler* than the wearer thinks. The market is, on the whole, biased toward warm undertones — partly because the market has been told, for twenty years, that warm is more flattering. The bias is, in our view, often wrong, and easy to correct.

## Step seven — the oxidation

The oxidation is the *colour the foundation becomes after it has been on the skin for ten minutes*. It is, in our view, the most overlooked variable in the foundation market, and the difference between a foundation that looks right at the counter and one that looks right at home.

The oxidation is tested by swatching the foundation on the jaw, walking to a window, and waiting ten minutes. The oxidation is, in most cases, a *darkening* of the foundation. In some formulations, the oxidation is a *colour shift* — the foundation becomes more yellow, more pink, or more orange.

The oxidation is, in our view, the variable that determines whether the foundation is, finally, right. A foundation that matches the depth at the counter but oxidises too dark will look wrong by lunchtime. A foundation that matches the undertone but oxidises too warm will, by lunchtime, look orange. The counter is least likely to test this — the counter wants the sale to close. The oxidation is, in most cases, the variable that has to be tested at home.

## Step eight — the setting

The setting is the *product applied over the foundation to make it last*: in most cases, a powder, a spray, or a balm. The setting is, in our view, the most over-considered variable in the foundation market, and the one most likely to undo all the work of the previous seven steps.

The powder is applied, in most cases, with a brush, in a thin layer, only on the parts of the face that are most likely to shine — the T-zone, around the nose, occasionally the chin. The powder is almost never the right product for the full face: applied to the full face, it undoes the finish of the foundation and, in most cases, requires re-application by the end of the day.

The spray is applied, in most cases, as a mist, after the foundation has set. The spray is, in our view, the most useful setting product for everyday wear — the one that does the least to *change* the finish. The balm is applied to the cheekbones, after the foundation has set, and is the most useful setting product for the drier skin.

The setting is, in sum, the *seal*. The seal is what makes the foundation survive the day. The seal is, in our view, best chosen to match the finish of the foundation, not to *change* the finish. The seal that changes the finish is, in our view, the wrong seal.

---

The eight steps, in order, are: undertone, depth, coverage, finish, texture, undertone-again, oxidation, setting. The order is, we believe, the order that produces, in the end, a foundation that is, finally, right for the skin.

The foundation is, in many ways, the most counter-intuitive cosmetic in the modern bag. The foundation is also, when it is right, the most useful. The right foundation is, in our view, the foundation that has been chosen by all eight steps, in the right order. The right foundation is the foundation that is, finally, the foundation you forgot you were wearing.

The foundation you forgot you were wearing is, in 2026, the foundation most worth finding. The foundation is, finally, the cosmetic that is meant to disappear. The disappearance is, when it works, the work.
