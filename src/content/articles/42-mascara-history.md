Take the wand away and the cosmetic is still there: a black pigment bound in a fat, applied to the eye for five thousand years. The brush is the newcomer. It arrived in two steps, in 1957 and 1958, and it is the youngest object in its own history by an order of magnitude.

The popular version has the relationship backwards: it treats the eye as untouched until a clever house launched mascara in the 1910s, and the modern tube as the finished point of refinement. The evidence runs the other way. Kohl was worn as protection against eye ailments from c. 3100 BCE, the Victorians cooked their own on a plate, and what comes out of the bathroom cupboard today is a sixty-five-year-old delivery system wrapped around a pigment already old in Hatshepsut's court.

## Antiquity — the kohl and the antimony

The oldest hard date in the story is Egyptian. Kohl has been worn traditionally since the Naqada III era, around 3100 BCE, by Egyptians of all social classes, and it was not intended as decoration but as protection against eye ailments. The looser popular version starts around 4000 BC. Either way, the practice begins as medicine.

The system had two colours, and the recipe was not improvised. Upper lids were painted black with galena, lead sulphide; lower lids green with malachite, copper carbonate. Galena use runs from the Badarian culture through to the Greco-Roman era, with finds from the prehistoric Tasian culture and imports from Western Asia, Coptos and Punt.

Antimony sat in the mixture too. Stibnite, Sb₂S₃, appears alongside galena, and modern blue kohl may still contain galena, laurionite, phosgenite, cerussite and stibnite. The word for the category comes from the Arabic *kuḥl*, originally the term for finely ground antimony powder; it acquired its modern definition in the 18th century.

It was doing medical work as well. The Ebers Papyrus, dated around 1550 BCE, discusses kohl's compounds as eye-protective; lead compounds in Egyptian kohl could trigger nitric-oxide production and an enhanced immune response at submicromolar concentrations, and the mixture inhibited *Streptococcus pyogenes*, mildly inhibiting *Proteus vulgaris* and *Staphylococcus aureus*.

Hatshepsut, in the 18th Dynasty, would grind charred frankincense into her eyeliner — the first recorded use of that resin, brought back from the Punt expedition around 1500 BCE. Nor was Egypt the only market: lead-isotope analysis of kohl from the Nubian cemeteries at Debeira and Ashkeit, dated between about 2300 and 1500 BCE, points to Nubians using and possibly producing it themselves.

## The Middle Ages — the loss and the Islamic recovery

Every account of this history has a middle chapter in which Europe loses the eye and the Islamic world keeps it — and the record is tidier on the keeping than on the losing. The survivor is a different mineral: *ithmid*, the Arabic term for stibnite-based kohl, made by grinding stibnite fine and binding it with oil, ghee or rose water.

It was a professional trade, and the evidence survives in an unexpected place — a law book. Ibn Abi Shaybah, in a legal compilation, described how to apply kohl to the eye.

The continuity shows in how it is still used. Berber and Semitic-speaking women across North Africa and the Middle East applied kohl to the face, including a vertical line running from the lower lip to the chin and along the nose — a style that originated on the Arabian Peninsula and reached North Africa in the seventh century.

## The Victorian era — the at-home cosmetic

Before there was a mascara industry there was a recipe. Victorian women made mascara at home by heating a mixture of ash or lampblack and elderberry juice on a plate.

As a formula it is the modern one in embryo: pigment — soot, iron oxide, carbon black — bound in a fat: elderberry juice on the plate, castor, lanolin, sesame or eucalyptus oil in the tube. The Victorian kitchen was running a small-batch version of a recipe still in use; the only thing missing was the factory.

Industrialisation took it out of the kitchen. After the Industrial Revolution cosmetics were generally manufactured from raw materials on a large scale, and two nineteenth-century developments stand out: Vaseline and lanolin. In 1882 Lillie Langtry became the first celebrity to endorse a commercial product, for face powders and skin products. The factory brought its own hazards: nineteenth-century lead poisoning, eye swelling and inflammation.

## 1913 — the Rimmel cake mascara

The year most retellings give for the birth of modern mascara is 1913, and it is a retelling rather than a record. What the sources support is narrower: Eugène Rimmel developed a petroleum-jelly cosmetic in the nineteenth century, a mixture of petroleum jelly and coal in a set ratio, credited as the development of modern mascara.

The format mattered more than the date. A cake mascara is a solid block of pigment and fat: you wet the cake and transfer the colour with a separate brush, hardware you own and clean. It was the honest container for a waxy, oily formula: no solvent to evaporate, no polymer to form a film, nothing to spoil.

The cake was still the mass-market form half a century later: a Max Factor cake mascara with brush is photographed from 1960, a refill tube with Max Factor Mascara Wand cream from 1961. In 1915 Thomas Lyle Williams created a similar substance for his sister Mabel, and by 1917 it was a mail-order business that grew into Maybelline.

The prestige half of the story is Elizabeth Arden and Helena Rubinstein, who launched mascara brands after World War I and gave the product respectability in America. The faces of the era's screen mascara — Theda Bara, Clara Bow, Greta Garbo, Jean Harlow — did more for its standing than any laboratory.

## 1957 — the Helena Rubinstein wand

The most repeated date in this history is also the least complete. What Helena Rubinstein did in 1957 was make the thing liquid: her formula evolved mascara from a hard cake into a lotion-based cream. She packaged it in a tube to be sold with a brush, the cream squeezed onto the brush and applied to the lashes.

The brush was a separate piece of hardware. The wand — the object everybody pictures when they picture mascara — came one year later. In 1958 Rubinstein created the first refillable mascara in a tube with an integrated brush for easy application, and called it Mascara Matic.

That is a small correction to a large legend: the popular version says Rubinstein invented the wand in 1957. What the record shows is a two-step invention — a cream in a tube first, an integrated applicator second — and the applicator then took a slow course of its own. Soon a grooved rod was patented, then altered to the brush used today; mascara became easier to use, and its popularity rose.

The real change was not the brush but the state of the product. Before 1957 a mascara was a solid you owned — a cake, or a dried tube rewetted each morning. After 1957 it was a liquid you dispensed, and the hazards that follow arrive with it: shelf life, bacterial risk, the rule about not sharing the wand.

Rubinstein's waterproof creme was licensed rather than original: it came from Helene Winterstein-Kambersky's patent of 1935, licensed to her four years later, still sold as La Bella Nussy. The wax-and-oil formula inside the tube had been settled for centuries. What the wand changed was the terms on which the old pigment was allowed to behave.

## 2020s — the modern formulation

The formula inside the tube is still the ancient one. The pigments are usually iron oxides and titanium dioxide, with ultramarine blue in some formulas; the oils run to linseed, castor, eucalyptus, lanolin, turpentine and sesame; the waxes are paraffin, carnauba and beeswax. The Victorian plate and the modern tube are the same dish.

What the last sixty-five years added is a coating. Tubing mascaras use film-forming polymers that coat individual lashes, and lengthening or curling formulas may contain nylon or rayon microfibers. Water resistance is a formulation choice, not a promise: water-resistant versions use hydrophobic ingredients such as dodecane, non-resistant versions a water-soluble base.

And the modern chemistry brought new dangers to an old cosmetic. A 2021 study of 231 products found organic fluorine in more than half, highest in waterproof mascara at 82% of brands tested, with up to 13 individual PFAS compounds in one product; they can enter through skin and tear ducts.

A liquid formula also incubates: styes and swollen eyelids are classed as allergic reactions, improper cleaning can leave mascara under the conjunctiva as black lumps, and the product may incubate lash microbes. The reason any of it is regulated is an accident of the twentieth century.

In 1933 a woman known on the court record as Mrs Brown consented to have her eyelashes permanently dyed with Lash Lure, which contained para-phenylenediamine; it caused severe burning, ulceration and swelling, blindness in 16 women, and one further death from bacterial infection. Congress gave the FDA authority over cosmetics in 1938.

The regimes built on that are recent: in Europe it is Regulation EC 1223/2009, in force since 11 July 2013, covering the EU, Iceland, Norway and Switzerland. Minnesota banned intentionally added mercury in cosmetics in 2008. Which brings the oldest thing in the story back to the newest: kohl is not an FDA-approved colour additive, and importing galena kohl into the United States is banned outright because of its lead content, though modern lead-free cosmetics may still sell "kohl" as a shade name.

Lead levels in commercial kohl preparations were as high as 84%. In Pakistan, surma is a major lead-exposure source: a 2016 study of pregnant women's nails found 13 of 84 samples above the cited fatal-case concentration of 13.6 μg/g. The regulatory problem and the ancient cosmetic are the same substance.

Which leaves the wand looking like the newcomer it is. The pigment was old enough to be a prescription in Hatshepsut's court and a recipe in a Victorian kitchen, regulated in a medieval law book and kept alive by a North African line drawn from the lower lip to the chin.

The applicator everybody photographs — the brush, the tube, the twist of the wrist — is about sixty-five years old, and it arrived after the eye had already been dressed for five thousand years. Mascara is the one product in the bathroom in which the thing you are buying is the newest part.
