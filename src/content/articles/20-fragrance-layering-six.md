Two bottles, one wrist, and an instruction that arrives without an argument attached: heavier scent first, wait, lighter over it. The advice is on counter cards, in video captions, on the box that sells a cologne beside a body cream. Almost no counter omits it.

Evidence is harder to find. There is no peer-reviewed study of two finished fragrances worn together, no house method for combining scents, no regulatory category for the result. The sceptical case is not that layering smells bad — that is taste. It is that layering is a dosage problem wearing a taste problem's clothes.

## 1. The perfumer's objection

Francis Kurkdjian has been the practice's most quoted opponent. Asked directly by *The Style Sheet*, the Pacific Place magazine in Hong Kong, he said: "I personally don't like mixing fragrances and I strongly disagree with that trend of layering." The refusal is categorical.

His reason is compositional. "As a perfumer, I create a perfume which is completely finished, no more no less," he said in the same interview. To *W* he put it shorter: "It's like saying you can layer perfumes. It's my job to create a perfume." A formula, on this account, is a whole with an intended arc.

He is not absolute. In the same interview he conceded that once a bottle is sold it "becomes theirs," and endorsed layering one scent across formats — cream, shower gel — which he said "is meant to be done that way." The line runs between one scent in several formats and two different scents.

Frédéric Malle, at a Barneys event in San Francisco in June 2011, took the same position, as a blogger present recounted it. He recommends layering only "if the person doing the layering is very skilled"; in his experience "they're never very skilled. It always smells awful." His conclusion: "I'm very opposed to it."

His house says it in retail copy: layering across scents "is not recommended as these perfumes are each an olfactive work of art, crafted to stand alone and apart." Composition is an officially distinct craft — UNESCO's 2018 inscription of the skills of Grasse names "the art of perfume composition" separately from growing and extraction. It has institutional weight. It is not testable.

## 2. What volatility decides

The structure the craft is taught on is younger than it looks. Jean Carles (1892–1966) worked at Roure-Bertrand-Dupont in Grasse and founded its perfumery school in 1946; a teaching text that circulated from around 1961 is credited with systematising the pyramid. Its best-known ratio, roughly 25% top, 20% heart and 55% base, is a convention. Modern fragrances ignore it.

The tiers are heuristic zones along a continuous volatility gradient, not airtight categories. Notes overlap rather than switching discretely, and a material of medium vapour pressure can read as heart or base depending on dosage and on what is fixing it. Volatility governs: an odorant must enter the gas phase before it can be smelled.

Molecular weight predicts the timetable. Limonene is about 136 g/mol and linalool 154; ambroxan is 236.39, hedione 226.31, Galaxolide 258.4, the principal Iso E Super isomer 234.38. Citrus is gone within the hour. The heavy materials are still detectable the next morning.

A fixative is a low-volatility material that reduces the evaporation rate of the more volatile materials beside it, by vapour-pressure depression and physical retention rather than chemical bonding. Ambroxan, made from sclareol extracted from clary sage, boils above 300 °C and works as both fixative and diffuser. Putting the heavy scent down first is physically coherent. No source measures whether it changes the headspace of a pair.

## 3. The market's scent wardrobe

The market has already decided. Circana's 2024 tracking found fragrance the fastest-growing beauty segment in both channels — up 12% in prestige, 9% in mass, with prestige at 28% of category sales. Nearly 40% of annual category sales land in the fourth quarter, and brands work to an 80/20 rule in which about 80% of revenue comes from 20% of SKUs.

Most of it is still bought in a shop. Only about 15% of fragrance sales happen online, against roughly 40% for beauty overall; Maesa's chief business officer, Oshiya Savur, said digital penetration was "less than 10%" before Covid.

The industry's phrase is not layering but *scent wardrobe*. Faye Harris of Eauso Vert told Beauty Independent: "People are building out their scent wardrobes now and wearing different fragrances based on moods," and credited combinations circulated on PerfumeTok. That is an executive's observation. No published dataset measures how many people layer two fragrances; sales research tracks ownership and wear frequency.

Japan is the outlier. Roughly 14% of more than 11,000 people surveyed by MyVoice had worn perfume in the past year; Statista's survey is reported at about 16%, the lowest of the countries surveyed. Fine fragrance was worth ¥57.5 billion in 2024, under 2% of a ¥3.2 trillion cosmetics market. The Japanese term for the friction of strong scent is *kōgai*.

## 4. The house that designed for it

Jo Malone built the commercial version before the word existed. Malone founded the brand in London in 1994, with a first shop at 154 Walton Street in Chelsea; the business grew out of a Nutmeg & Ginger bath oil she gave to facial clients, and the shop is reported to have sold out its stock in about six weeks.

Combining was the architecture, not a later campaign. The colognes were built on few notes so they could be worn alone or together, and the house called it "the Art of Fragrance Combining." Its global head of fragrance, Céline Roux, explains the effect as minimalism: few, high-quality ingredients give each cologne "clarity" so two can be read together.

Estée Lauder Companies bought the brand in 1999; Malone stayed as creative director until 2006 and founded Jo Loves in 2011. Three designated combiners — Grapefruit, Peony & Blush Suede, English Oak & Hazelnut — now do the work for people who would rather not experiment. It also layers formats: cologne over a Body Crème or Bath Oil, cream as base, cologne as top.

Diptyque, as reported by Mr Porter, suggests two scents sprayed simultaneously for equilibrium, or the favourite sprayed last and more liberally: Do Son with Olene, L'Ombre dans l'Eau with Eau Rose. Le Labo pairs Santal 33 with Baie 19, and with Another 13, an ambroxan-heavy amplifier rather than a scent. Pixi entered perfume in November 2025 with a Fig, Rose and Mimosa trio built to "blend, mix and play": $18 each, $36 the trio.

## 5. The practice, as advised

The published advice converges on one order. The heavier, deeper scent — amber, oud, musk, wood, vanilla, resin — goes down first as an anchor; the lighter, fresher one goes on top as the lift, on the reasoning that heavy materials persist while light ones would be buried underneath. It is coherent, and untested against the reverse.

The wait is not standardised. Sources give about 30 seconds, 30 to 60, 60 to 90, or "until the alcohol has evaporated," wet-on-dry rather than wet-on-wet. There is no industry specification. The alternative is zoning rather than stacking: different fragrances on different pulse points — wrists, neck, behind the knees — so the scents are met separately.

Dosage advice is restraint: one to four sprays in total, a 2:1 anchor-to-accent ratio as a starting point, no more than two or three fragrances at once. The reliable pairing rule is a shared note — two vanillas amplify each other, a heavy amber lightens with citrus — stated as principles by the fragrance expert Thomas Dunckley. Alice du Parcq calls skin scents "the makeup primer to your foundation."

Concentration is convention, not law: cologne commonly 2–8% fragrance oil, eau de toilette 5–15%, eau de parfum 15–20%, parfum 20–40%, with no ISO standard fixing the bands. The guides rarely mention them. That is the quiet error in the genre. Two sprays of an 18% eau de parfum deposit roughly twice the oil of one, and the advice is written as though all sprays were equal.

## 6. The dosage objection

Two instruments get confused here. IFRA Standards are a voluntary, self-regulatory industry framework restricting how much of a given material may appear in a given product category. EU allergen labelling is a legal obligation under Cosmetics Regulation 1223/2009, Annex III, governing what must be printed on the pack. IFRA limits apply to individual restricted ingredients, not to total fragrance-oil percentage; there is no blanket "safe up to X% perfume" rule.

Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/1545, adopted on 26 July 2023 and published the next day, replaced the original 26-substance list with more than 80 declarable substances, including natural complex substances such as bergamot, sandalwood and ylang-ylang extracts. The thresholds did not move: 0.001% in leave-on products, 0.01% in rinse-off. Placing on the market is prohibited from 31 July 2026; sell-through runs to 31 July 2028.

Exposure science long ago stopped thinking in single products. The Creme RIFM aggregate model used in IFRA's QRA2 draws on diaries from 42,000 panellists across 71 products and 18 non-overlapping skin sites, aggregating by body site rather than by product. And yet no regulation, IFRA standard or SCCS guidance assesses two finished fragrances used together on the same patch of skin. Compliance is assessed product by product.

The labelling consequence is a blind spot. Two products each carrying an allergen below the 10 ppm declaration threshold can together deliver more than 10 ppm, and neither label would name it. Allergy is common but not universal: Fragrance Mix I positivity runs from 0.5% in Italian military recruits to 11.4% in a German MONICA subgroup. Patch testing detects delayed type IV allergy only, not irritation.

## What we take from this

People are poor at decomposing mixtures. In the standard review of odour-mixture perception, untrained subjects correctly identify no more than about four familiar odours in mixtures of up to eight; for four-component mixtures, correct identification falls below 20%.

Mixtures are usually subject to *hypo-addition*: the response is often weaker than the response to the strongest component, through suppression and compromise at the receptor and mucus level. Ratio matters as much as content. A 30/70 ethyl isobutyrate and ethyl maltol blend was processed by newborn rabbits as a new whole; at the far end, 30 equally intense odorants produce *olfactory white*.

Enough components stop reading as components. A layered pair arrives as one unstable third thing, and the wearer cannot take it apart. What is left is arithmetic — two loads of the same allergens on one skin site, two evaporation schedules, no label that adds them up. The perfumer's objection is worth taking seriously as a claim about authorship. Only the chemist's can be tested.
