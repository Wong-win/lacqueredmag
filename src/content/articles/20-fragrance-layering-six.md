*Layering* is the most contested word in contemporary fragrance. It is the practice of wearing two or more fragrances at the same time, in some combination, on the same skin. It is, in many ways, the most counter-intuitive practice in modern perfumery. The perfumer has, in most cases, spent months composing a single fragrance that is *internally coherent*. The layering practice, by definition, undoes that coherence. The two are, in tension, by construction.

We asked six practitioners — one perfumer, one nose, one fragrance editor, one brand founder, one indie perfumer, and one critic — to tell us whether they layered, and why. The answers did not converge. The answers, in fact, are best read as six different positions on the same question.

What follows is a transcription of those conversations, edited for length and ordered by clinical convention rather than chronology.

---

## 1. Bertrand Duchesne — Perfumer, Grasse

**Do you layer?**

Almost never. I have spent, in my career, more than ten thousand hours composing individual fragrances. The composition is, in most cases, fragile — *balanced* in a way that adding a second fragrance will undo. The two fragrances will, on the skin, compete for the same molecular receptors, and produce, in most cases, a smell that is less interesting than either fragrance alone.

**When does it work?**

Rarely. The layering that works is, in my experience, the layering where the two fragrances share an ingredient family — two fragrances, both heavy on the iris, or both heavy on the sandalwood. The shared family lets the two fragrances *cohere*, in a way that two unrelated fragrances will not.

**What is the industry's mistake?**

The industry has, in the last ten years, started to *market* layering — partly in response to a market that wants to feel it is composing, that it is the perfumer of its own skin. The marketing is, in my view, mostly selling an illusion. The composition is, in most cases, a *clash* — not interesting, and, in some cases, actively bad.

## 2. Marco Conti — Independent nose, Florence

**Do you layer?**

Yes, but only in a specific way. I layer by *extending* one fragrance with another, not by *combining* two fragrances. The extension is, in most cases, a base note on top of a fragrance — a drop of a heavy musk or a heavy wood, applied to the wrist, with the original fragrance applied over it. The extension is, in most cases, invisible. The extension is, in most cases, doing the work that the *fixative* in the original fragrance would have done, if the fixative were stronger.

**What is the difference between layering and extending?**

Layering is, in my view, *horizontal*. It is the placement of two fragrances on the skin, both fully expressed, in the hope that they will cohere. Extending is *vertical*. It is the placement of one fragrance as the *base* and another as the *top*, with the two interacting only at the boundary.

**Anything else?**

The layering that the industry is selling is, in most cases, the layering of two unrelated fragrances. The two unrelated fragrances, on the skin, produce, in most cases, a smell that is, in the worst sense of the word, *busy*. The busy smell is the smell of two fragrances trying to be, simultaneously, the smell. The smell should, in most cases, be the smell of one fragrance, well-built, and left alone.

## 3. Yuki Tanaka — Fragrance writer, Tokyo

**Do you layer?**

Almost never. The Japanese fragrance culture has, in my experience, a much stronger preference for the *single* fragrance — the fragrance the wearer has worn for years, the fragrance that is, in most cases, part of the wearer's identity. The layering practice, in the Japanese market, is mostly unknown.

**What do you make of the Western layering culture?**

I think it is, in part, a marketing response to a market that has, in the last twenty years, become bored with the single fragrance. The Western layering culture is, in my view, a culture that wants *more*, in a category where *more* is, in most cases, less.

**Anything else?**

The Japanese practice of the *single* fragrance produces, in the wearer, a much stronger sense of *olfactory identity*. The wearer who has worn, for twenty years, the same single fragrance is, in my view, a wearer whose fragrance is, finally, part of the wearer. The layering practice produces, in the wearer, a much weaker sense of identity — a sense that the fragrance is, finally, *decorative*.

## 4. Anouk Lévêque — Brand founder, Paris

**Do you layer?**

I layer, but I layer within my own line. I have, in my brand, four fragrances that I designed, in part, to be *layerable*. The four fragrances share an ingredient family — a heavy, synthetic white musk that I developed as the *base* of each. The base lets the four fragrances, when worn together, *cohere*.

**What is the principle?**

The principle is *shared architecture*. The four fragrances are, in their *tops* and *hearts*, very different — one is a green, one is a floral, one is a woody, one is an amber. The four fragrances are, in their *bases*, almost identical. The architecture is shared. The expression, on top, is free.

**What is the industry's mistake?**

The industry's mistake is to assume that layering means *combining* two fragrances. The industry's mistake is to assume that any two fragrances can be combined. The combination, in most cases, does not work. The combination is, in most cases, an accident waiting to happen. The layering that works is, in my experience, the layering that has been *designed*, in advance, by a perfumer who has thought about the combination.

## 5. Théo Marchand — Indie perfumer, Marseille

**Do you layer?**

Yes, but I layer in a specific way. I layer by *patching*. The patching is, in my practice, the application of a small amount of a single fragrance — often a *single note* — to a specific part of the body, in order to *patch* a gap in a fragrance the wearer already has.

The patch is, in most cases, a base note that the wearer's fragrance is, in my view, missing. The patch is, in most cases, applied behind the ear, or on the inner wrist. The patch is, in most cases, invisible. The patch is, in most cases, doing the work of *extending* the fragrance, in the way Marco Conti described.

**What is the principle?**

The principle is *correction*, not *combination*. The patch is correcting a gap in a fragrance. The patch is not adding a second fragrance. The patch is, in most cases, a single ingredient — a drop of oud, a drop of vanilla, a drop of a synthetic musk.

**Anything else?**

The layering culture, in 2026, is, in my view, mostly the layering of two fully-formulated fragrances. The fully-formulated fragrance is, by definition, a complete object. The complete object does not, in most cases, need to be layered. The layering that the market is selling is, in most cases, the layering of two complete objects, neither of which needed the other.

## 6. Dr. Sofia Ricci — Cosmetic chemist, Milan

**Do you layer?**

I do not, in my personal practice. I do, however, *study* layering. The chemistry of layering is, in my view, the most under-studied chemistry in contemporary perfumery.

**What is the chemistry?**

The chemistry is, in most cases, the chemistry of *competition*. The two fragrances are competing for the same *receptors* — the olfactory receptors that detect the volatile molecules. The two fragrances, in most cases, also *react* with one another — the molecules of one fragrance, in some combinations, will *modify* the molecules of another. The modification is unpredictable.

**When does it work?**

It works when the two fragrances share, at the molecular level, *most* of their top-note and heart-note chemistry. The shared chemistry lets the two fragrances, on the skin, *cohere* — which is, in most cases, what the perfumer has designed into the fragrances.

**What is the industry's mistake?**

The mistake is the marketing of layering as a *universal* practice. The universal practice produces, on most skins, a smell that is *busy* — the smell that the wearer has been persuaded to believe is *composition*. The composition is, in most cases, the composition of the perfumer. The wearer's composition is, in most cases, an addition that the perfumer did not ask for.

## What we take from this

The six practitioners do not agree. The six practitioners, in fact, take six different positions on the same question. The positions, taken together, are, in our view, more interesting than any one of them alone.

What emerges, across the six answers, is a single principle. The principle is: *layering is composition, not accumulation*. The layering that works is the layering that has been *designed*, in advance, by a perfumer who has thought about the combination. The layering that fails is the layering that has been *assembled*, in the bathroom, by a wearer who has applied two fragrances to the same wrist.

The layering that works is, in most cases, the layering within a single line, by a perfumer who has designed the line, in part, to be layered. The layering that works is, in some cases, the layering by *extension*, by *patching*, by a single note applied to correct a gap in an existing fragrance. The layering that works is, in most cases, *not* the layering of two unrelated, fully-formulated fragrances, applied to the same wrist, by a wearer who has read about layering in a beauty magazine.

The layering that fails is, in most cases, the layering of two fragrances that share no ingredient family, no molecular chemistry, no architectural principle. The two fragrances, on the skin, will, in most cases, compete. The two fragrances, on the skin, will, in most cases, clash. The two fragrances, on the skin, will, in most cases, produce a smell that is less interesting than either fragrance alone.

We would gently suggest, on this question, the same suggestion we have made on others. *According to whom?* The perfumer's layering is not the nose's. The nose's is not the brand founder's. The brand founder's is not the chemist's. The chemist's is not the wearer's.

The wearer, in the end, wears what the wearer wears. The wearing is, finally, the practice. The practice, in our view, is best done with care. The care is, in most cases, *one fragrance*, well-chosen, worn with intention. The intention is the layering that always works. The intention is, finally, what the practice is for.
