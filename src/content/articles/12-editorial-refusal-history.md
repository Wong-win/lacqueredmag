Every magazine is, finally, a collection of refusals. The pages you turn past — the article that was assigned and never filed, the photograph that was shot and never used, the product that was tested and never mentioned, the cover that was considered and rejected — are as constitutive of the magazine as the ones that made it into print. The reader sees the second category. The editor lives with the first.

This is an essay about the first category. It is also an argument that a magazine, in 2026, can be judged more usefully by what it refuses than by what it publishes.

The argument is not new. It is, however, increasingly unfashionable. The 2026 magazine, like the 2026 podcast and the 2026 newsletter, lives in a media economy that rewards publication. The algorithm rewards publication. The advertising model rewards publication. The metric of *releases per month* is, in many editorial offices, the only metric that matters. The result is a publication culture in which the cost of publishing is low and the cost of refusing is high.

We would like to push back, gently, on that economy.

## 1968 — *Cosmopolitan* and the surgery feature

In 1968, *Cosmopolitan* published a feature on cosmetic surgery. The feature was, by the standards of its day, frank. It described, in clinical detail, what a facelift entailed. It quoted prices. It named surgeons. It included before-and-after photographs of women who had undergone the procedure.

The feature was, in retrospect, a turning point. It was, in effect, the first mass-market American magazine to treat cosmetic surgery as a normal consumer choice rather than as a fringe practice. *Cosmopolitan* did not invent cosmetic surgery. It did, however, in 1968, give it a cover.

The magazine industry in the decades since has, mostly, declined to repeat this gesture. The contemporary beauty magazine will, on the whole, not run before-and-after photographs of cosmetic surgery. The contemporary beauty magazine will, on the whole, not quote surgical prices. The contemporary beauty magazine will, in most cases, not name a particular surgeon as a recommendation.

The refusal has not been made loudly. It has been made, mostly, by not running the feature. The refusal is, in other words, a refusal by *omission* — the magazine's silence on the subject is, itself, an editorial position.

## 1985 — the celebrity cover

The celebrity cover is older than the contemporary beauty magazine, but it became, in the 1980s, the dominant cover format. By the late 1980s, it was unusual for a major American beauty magazine to publish a cover that was not, in some sense, a celebrity. The editorial logic was simple: the celebrity sold the magazine at the newsstand. The celebrity also, in the same gesture, displaced any other editorial logic the cover might have had.

The indie magazine movement of the 2010s was, in part, a refusal of the celebrity cover. *Cherry Pickle*, *The Establishment*, *Coveteur* in its print run, and a handful of others, returned to the model of the *concept* cover — a model that was, in fact, the dominant cover model of the 1950s and 1960s. The concept cover is the cover that is built around an idea (the lacquered face, the spring skin, the wet hair) rather than a face the reader is asked to recognise.

The refusal of the celebrity cover is, in editorial terms, a refusal to outsource the cover's argument to a third party. The celebrity arrives on the cover with her own argument already made. The concept cover requires the magazine to make its own argument, in the image and in the cover lines, without help.

## 1995 — the before-and-after photo

The before-and-after photograph — two images of the same face, side by side, the *before* usually imperfect, the *after* usually perfected — has been a recurring feature of beauty advertising since the early twentieth century. The before-and-after, in its commercial form, has been mostly uncontroversial. The before-and-after, in its editorial form, has been more fraught.

In the early 1990s, a small number of beauty magazines ran editorial before-and-after features, often in connection with skin or makeup stories. The features were, in most cases, run on consenting subjects, with the subjects photographed under identical conditions. The features were also, in many cases, somewhat misleading — the *before* was photographed without makeup, the *after* with, and the comparison was presented as evidence of the *product* rather than as evidence of the makeup.

By the late 1990s, most major beauty magazines had stopped running editorial before-and-after features. The editorial before-and-after had been, in effect, refused.

The refusal was not announced. The refusal was made, mostly, by not publishing the feature. But the refusal was, by the mid-2000s, an industry position. The major American beauty magazine of 2006 did not, in its editorial pages, publish before-and-after photographs. The major American beauty magazine of 2026 still does not.

## 2008 — the sponsored review

The sponsored review — an editorial piece that has been paid for by a brand, in some form, and presented to the reader as an editorial recommendation — has existed, in some form, since the early twentieth century. The 1920s beauty magazine ran, with no shame, advertorial sections: pages that were paid for by a brand, written in the magazine's editorial voice, and presented, with or without disclaimer, as advice.

The contemporary beauty magazine, in most of its serious editorial manifestations, refuses the sponsored review. The refusal is, in 2010s and 2020s industry language, a matter of *separation of church and state*: the editorial page is paid for by subscriptions; the advertising page is paid for by the brand. The two do not mix.

The refusal has been tested, repeatedly, by brands willing to pay large sums for the appearance of editorial endorsement. The refusal has, mostly, held. The 2026 beauty magazine, in its serious editorial manifestations, still does not run paid editorial as editorial. The reader can, in most cases, tell which page is which.

## 2018 — the AI face

In the late 2010s, several beauty magazines ran covers or features whose central image was not, in any meaningful sense, a photograph of a person. The image was generated. The image was, in some cases, an AI composite. The image was, in other cases, a heavily retouched photograph that had been altered past the point of recognisability.

The reaction, in 2018 and 2019, was sharp. Several major magazines issued statements. *Allure* announced, in 2018, that it would not, in its editorial pages, use significantly altered photographs. *Refinery29* announced a similar policy. The policies were not always honoured in practice. They were, however, made.

The AI-face refusal is, in 2026, still in flux. The technology has moved faster than the policy. The contemporary beauty magazine is, in many cases, still working out what the policy should be. The working-out is, in itself, an editorial act. The magazine is being defined, in 2026, by what it does with the technology.

## 2024 — the ethics statement

The contemporary beauty magazine, in many of its serious editorial manifestations, publishes an *ethics statement* — a public document that states what the magazine will and will not do. The ethics statement is, in effect, a public version of the editor's running list of refusals. It is the part of the magazine's refusals that the reader is allowed to see.

The ethics statement is not new. The *British Medical Journal* has published its ethics statement for over a century. The contemporary beauty magazine has, however, only in the last ten years, adopted the practice with any seriousness. The ethics statement is, in 2026, a marker of editorial seriousness. The magazine that has one is, in most cases, a magazine that has thought about its refusals.

## The economy of refusal

The refusal costs money. The magazine that refuses the celebrity cover sells fewer copies at the newsstand. The magazine that refuses the sponsored review takes less advertising. The magazine that refuses the AI face pays for more photography, more models, more production.

The refusal also, in the long run, earns something. The reader who notices the refusal — and more readers notice than the editors sometimes realise — comes to trust the magazine that has made the refusal. The trust is the asset. The asset is the thing the magazine has, finally, to sell.

This is, in the end, the argument for the refusal. The magazine is defined, finally, by the work it will not do. The work it will not do is, in many cases, the work that would be most profitable in the short term. The refusal is, in this sense, the long-term business decision masquerading as the moral one.

We are, in this magazine, attempting to refuse several things. We will not run sponsored reviews. We will not, in our editorial pages, use significantly altered photographs. We will not, in our editorial pages, run before-and-after photographs of cosmetic surgery. We will not run celebrity covers that displace the magazine's argument.

We have not made a public ethics statement. The refusal is, we believe, more credible when it is acted out than when it is announced. We are acting it out. You are reading it.

The magazine is, finally, the refusals it has made. We hope ours are worth making.
