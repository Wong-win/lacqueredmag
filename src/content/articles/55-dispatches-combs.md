A comb is a small instrument most people use badly, and the reason is a story about materials that has outlived its facts. The story says wood is gentle and anti-static, metal is harsh, boar bristle spreads oil, and a detangler is what long hair deserves. Six weeks with four implements confirmed none of it. What the evidence leaves in place is less quotable: pressure, and the direction the tool travels.

The mechanism is documented, and wood is not in it. Taylor, Barton, Duffy, Enea-Casse, Marty, Teeling and Santoprete set it out in *Interface Focus* in 2024: hair breaks while it is being combed or brushed through a tangle, not because it is pulled along its own axis. Roughly 1 N of axial tension would pull the strand out of the follicle first. What breaks hair is a strand bent around another strand or a tooth, loaded repeatedly until it fatigues — a claim about how a tool moves, not what it is made of.

## The wooden comb

In the Pays d'Olmes the horn-comb craft survives at industrial scale: sacks of raw buffalo horn arrive from the southern hemisphere, get sawn into sections and flattened into slabs by a specialist called the *biscayeur*, the elite of the profession.

Teeth are cut, polished and packed through roughly 15 stages, documented at the Musée du textile et du peigne en corne in Lavelanet. Boxwood and peachwood were the other staples: low-density woods that will not sink in water and cannot take a high gloss.

The dermatology consensus is narrower than the folk version, and Dr. Aanchal Panth, in the *Indian Express*, is blunt about the gap. A wooden comb reduces static electricity and slightly reduces frizz; it does not reduce hair fall, improve scalp circulation or reduce dandruff.

Her caveat matters: wood is porous and can trap oils, bacteria and fungi, so an oily scalp or heavy dandruff argues against one, and damp hair is best combed at around 70% dry, once a day.

The anti-static claim needs rewiring; it is usually stated backwards. Conductive materials — steel, carbon fibre — work as anti-static combs precisely because they neutralise the positive charge left on hair, while plastic is an insulator that strips electrons off the keratin and leaves the strand positively charged.

Wood's quieter claim is the absence of an injection-moulding seam to catch at the scalp. Geometry is what a wooden comb is specified by: teeth a quarter to three-eighths of an inch wide for a wide-tooth detangler, spacing under a sixteenth of an inch for a fine-tooth comb.

## The metal comb

The metal comb has a documented birth, older than the electric age. Nathaniel Bushnell of Middletown, Connecticut took out a patent on 31 October 1835 for "a new & useful improvement in the manufacture of Ladies Hair Comb" — a tin top, iron wire teeth painted to imitate tortoise shell, and at least three coats of varnish.

He claimed lightness, strength, an even curve and teeth that would not injure hair. His shop ran from about 1825 to 1850; the 1850 census lists him as a maker of pen holders.

The metal comb lost its reign to plastic's patents and dates: celluloid from about 1880, Bakelite patented in 1907, cellulose acetate replacing celluloid in the mid-1920s, and stainless steel invented in 1913, too late. The salon metal comb is usually a hybrid: carbon-fibre or carbon-infused plastic teeth on a stainless steel tail, sold on heat-resistance claims of up to 220°C.

A truly all-metal comb is machined from 304 or 440C stainless, where the quality indicator is not the grade of steel but the tooth tip — rounded, smooth, free of burrs and stamping seam lines.

The metal argument that survives into the modern salon is chemical rather than cosmetic.

Stainless withstands salon sanitisers and developer solutions in a way ABS plastic does not, and California's rules, 16 CCR § 979, require salons to disinfect combs between clients — which a metal comb survives. A full-metal luxury line exists in Italy under Janeke, sold as *Cromium metal*. None of that makes metal gentle: period medical opinion was hostile on scalp-injury grounds.

## The boar-bristle brush

Boar bristle was the material of choice from the Middle Ages onward, set by hand into wood or bone handles, and the reason is physical rather than sentimental. Bristle is keratin-based and structurally close to human hair, which is why it grips without generating static and redistributes scalp sebum down the shaft.

The industrial brush arrived in the nineteenth century: Mason Pearson invented the automatic brush-boring machine in 1885, and Hugh Rock patented cushioned wire bristles as US 11,157 in 1854.

At the top of the market the boar brush is still finished by hand, trimmed and stitched into cherry or beech handles. Boar's limits are as informative as its virtues: nylon tolerates roughly 210°C and boar cannot take that heat, which is why boar belongs to dry grooming and nylon to wet detangling and heat styling.

Two claims also get conflated in every product page — natural bristles mainly address static, tapered nylon and wide-tooth spacing mainly address breakage.

Boar bristle travels as its own tariff line, HS 0502.10, "Pigs', hogs' or boars' bristles and hair; and waste thereof", covering brushmaking hair: an animal-origin import commodity with a customs category of its own.

A boar brush also has a short life: replace a natural-bristle brush at 6 to 8 months, a daily-use brush at 6 to 12, and any brush that starts pulling, or one whose ball tips have worn off, since the exposed nylon underneath abrades the scalp.

## The modern detangler

The detangler is the newest of the four and the only one with a patent-defence history. Shaun Pulfrey, a British hair colourist and stylist, began developing it in 2003; the UK Intellectual Property Office case study calls the brush the "world's first detangling hairbrush" and describes it as inspired by a technique he used with his fingers while doing hair colour. His 2007 pitch to the Dragons was 15% of Tangle Teezer for £80,000, and the investors declined.

The launch date is one of the few things the record cannot settle: Wikipedia, checked directly, gives 2007 for *The Original* with Boots stocking from 2008, while other summaries say 2008 throughout.

Scale followed quickly: turnover of £2.2 million in 2011 and £28.6 million by March 2016, and a place in the Sunday Times Fast Track 100 in 2013 and 2016. In July 2021 Pulfrey sold a majority stake to Mayfair Equity Partners for about £70 million.

The intellectual property was defended throughout: by 2015 the brush was patented in more than 30 countries, each design protected by design rights, and the first counterfeit had turned up in 2011.

What is sold is a geometry rather than a material — long, flexible teeth that flex over a tangle while shorter, firmer teeth smooth the cuticle — though that description comes from the brand's marketing, not from the Wikipedia article, which does not contain it.

The patent record tells a stranger story: the detangling-adjacent US 5,502,860, granted to John Franke in 1996, covers a brush with a limited-excursion rotating head and variable rotational resistance to minimise tugging, and US 11,369,190 of 2019 specifies differential bristle diameters of 0.4 to 1.8 mm.

Prices are third-party and in dollars — $11.89 for the Ultimate Detangler, $18.99 for the Zodiac — and there is no waterproof Tangle Teezer, whatever the listings imply.

## The honest take

Start with the laboratory's own disclaimer: it sits where the argument rests. Low-quality hair failed after 207 cycles on average against 531 for control hair at 14 g; at 26 g the figures were 85 and 255.

Low-quality strands developed centre splits extending more than 5 mm; control hair produced edge splits generally under 1 mm. Then the authors admit their rig moved strands in both directions, unlike normal brushing toward the tip, and never systematically tested tooth shape, brush stiffness, pressure or one-way against bidirectional movement.

The strongest case is a safety case, with nothing to do with the implement. *Traction alopecia* is defined by prolonged or repeated tension on the follicles, from tight braids, cornrows, ponytails and extensions.

StatPearls, updated 4 May 2025, puts hair changes at up to 31.7% of adult women against 2.3% of men in one South African dataset, and finds signs in 18% of African American girls aged 5.4 to 14.3 years. Diagnosis is clinical: the fringe sign, a band of miniaturised vellus hair at the margin. Caught early it regrows fully; left alone it becomes irreversible cicatricial alopecia.

The dermatology guidance is narrower than either camp would like. The American Academy of Dermatology says that "brushing your hair 100 strokes each day can cause split ends" and advises keeping brushing to a minimum — it does not call the 100-stroke rule a myth, it warns against that amount.

Wet hair breaks more easily when combed or brushed, and a comb is specifically discouraged after strong-hold styling products. The technique literature is more specific still: *ends to roots* for detangling, roots to ends for conditioning, working in sections of two to six, starting at the last inch or two and holding the hair above the knot.

The evidence is thinner than the marketing on either side. There are no head-to-head trials comparing combs with brushes on wet hair for breakage, and no peer-reviewed trials comparing plastic with wooden combs on scalp irritation; the evidence is expert opinion.

The comb-versus-brush distinction that does exist rests on a single 2007 paper in a paywalled trade journal; the nearest actual trial, Koyama in 2016, measured hair thickness after scalp massage, not combing. Hygiene is the documented exception: a dirty brush, Dr. Julia Tzu warns, is "similar to using an unwashed makeup brush" and may contribute to folliculitis and tinea capitis; the CDC confirms that lice spread via combs.

So what did six weeks establish? Less than the material argument wanted, more than the folklore offered. Wood and horn are gentler than plastic and metal; the boar is chemically kin to hair; metal's surviving argument is sterilisation; the detangler's is tooth geometry. None of that is the variable the evidence points to.

What breaks hair is a strand bent around another and loaded again and again, which means pressure and direction: a wide-toothed wooden comb on wet hair, ends first, section by section; a boar brush for dry hair; a detangler only when you need the speed; and a tool whose teeth are smooth and unburred. The implement matters. It just matters less than the way you move it.
