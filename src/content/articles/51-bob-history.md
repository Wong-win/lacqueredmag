A bob is decided by where it stops. Wikipedia's named bob techniques are structural rather than trendy: an inverted bob is "similar to an A-line bob, but with stacked layers in the back," a shingle bob is "tapered very short in the back, exposing the hairline at the neck," a buzz-cut bob is "shoulder-length in the front and close-cropped at the back." One question, several answers.

We meet the bob as a trend — short in the 1920s, sharper in the 1960s, French in 2024 — a cycle of surfaces returning every twenty years. The record describes something else: a few shapes answering a fixed problem about the jaw and the neck. The pendulum between the blunt cut and the graduated one is not a fashion cycle but a standing argument between two claims about what a face is for.

## 1909 — the first bob

The English-language record starts in Paris in 1909, with a Polish hairdresser, Antoni Cierplikowski, trading as Antoine de Paris, whom Wikipedia credits with starting the fashion. What prompted him was a report, not a haircut: accounts of Joan of Arc.

How short that first bob was remains disputed — The National has him cutting a woman's hair to chin length, Chinese-language encyclopaedias describe an ear-length cut — and no English account names the woman. The oldest photograph usually cited is Alice Liddell at seven, in 1860, photographed by Lewis Carroll with a bob and fringe — half a century before the supposed invention.

That precedent carried real charge. Joan of Arc cut her hair and dressed as a man so that she would not be recognised; an English court convicted her of heresy and burned her at the stake at 19, in 1431 at Rouen, the short hair part of the case. The first bob imported a martyr's haircut into a Paris salon, and the scandal was not that hair had become fashionable but that it had become legible as something other than conventionally female.

## 1920s — the flapper bob

The haircut was legal before it was fashionable. In 1921 the New York Times reported that women hairdressers in Connecticut who wished to bob hair "would have to obtain a barber's licence," and Wikipedia is explicit that no law ever required or forbade bobbed hair itself.

A California school board refused to hire a teacher over her bob in 1928, and a commissioner who had backed the purge then conceded: "If all the bobbed-haired teachers were fired, there would not be enough long-haired applicants to go around." On 21 August 1927 a Minsk rabbi ruled that a boyishly bobbed sheitel was not grounds for divorce.

Respectability came from the war: women driving ambulances in Romania in 1916 and British land girls in 1918 were noted for short hair. Irene Castle's bob had "become rapidly fashionable" by 1920, and Lady Diana Cooper, Colleen Moore and Louise Brooks carried it onward. The press declared it dead before it won — The Times called bobbed hair passé in 1922, yet by the mid-1920s it was the dominant Western women's hairstyle.

The shape that made the 1920s bob distinct was Antoine's, and it was structural: the *shingle cut*, introduced in the 1920s, popular with the flappers, tapered very short in the back, exposing the hairline at the neck, and finished with one curl or point per cheek. Mass removed from below the jaw, the neck shown, the face left to do the work. Every dispute since — Sassoon's points at the cheeks, the French bob's jawline — is a variation on that decision.

The Marcel wave was worked with a hot iron — the US patent for a "Curling-Iron" is No. 806386, published in 1905, with an electric "Hair-Waving Iron" in 1918 — and the finger wave needed no heat, associated both with Black hair culture and with Monsieur Antoine, Josephine Baker's hairdresser.

The mundane reason the bob became a daily cut is a hairdryer, credited to the Frenchman Alexandre-Ferdinand Godefroy in the 1880s and to John James Brisben for the home model in the 1920s: the technology that made the bob practical arrived just before the bob did.

The bill was modest — $5.00 for the initial cut, then $2.00 a week — and a Washington Post piece of 1925 headed "Economic Effects of Bobbing" reported hairdressing shops up from 5,000 in 1920 to 21,000 by the end of 1924. In *Pictorial Review* in 1927, the actress Mary Gordon put the argument plainly — "I consider getting rid of our long hair one of the many little shackles that women have cast aside in their passage to freedom."

## 1960s — the Vidal Sassoon bob

By the middle of the 1960s the bob had been rewritten by Vidal Sassoon. His five-point cut, created for Peggy Moffitt and worn by Mary Quant, is described as "an asymmetrical bowl cut" — "a simple, close-cut geometric hairstyle." The irony is worth keeping: the cut that broke with salon orthodoxy was a bowl cut.

The literature dates it to 1963–64; the encyclopedia gives no year at all. Sassoon's claim was a change in method: "eliminate the superfluous and get down to the basic angles of cut and shape." His 1968 book *Sorry I Kept You Waiting, Madam* puts it more plainly still — "To me hair dressing means shape. It's very important that the foundations should be right."

He did not invent the hair dryer but popularised hand-held blow-drying, and the Associated Press obituary of 2012 reports that rollers and helmet-style dryers became all but obsolete once he used a blow dryer to make his cuts.

A first London salon in 1954, the first American one on Madison Avenue in 1965, and then, in 1968, Roman Polanski paying $5,000 to bring him from London to Hollywood to cut Mia Farrow's pixie for *Rosemary's Baby* — a sum the encyclopedia renders as $35,000 in 2025. The bob went pop in the same decade, alongside the Beatles' "mop top," and it was prominent among Diana Ross & The Supremes and The Marvelettes.

## 1990s — the supermodel bob

The 1990s held two opposite bobs at once. Mia Wallace's bob in *Pulp Fiction*, 1994, became inseparable from Uma Thurman, and it was very short, blunt and straight-edged — the opposite of the graduated blowout bob running at the same time.

The shape that carried the decade's advertising and its salons was the *flob*, the flipped bob, which Glamour describes as combining "the texture and volume of Cindy Crawford's signature bombshell blowout with the length, movement, and low-maintenance of Christy Turlington's and Eva Herzigová's bobs." The rule that separates the two poles: a flip needs super-light, barely visible layers — if the top is too heavy, it will not flip — the direct opposite of the blunt bob's premise.

Warmth came from the technique rather than the cut: maximum root volume, lifted roots and inward-facing waves, achieved by rough-drying 70–80 per cent upside down, setting in large rollers and brushing out with the head flipped. The bob had a power seat too: Anna Wintour took the severe bob in 1988, the year she became editor-in-chief of *US Vogue*, with a 2006 report claiming she trimmed it daily.

It was also read as evidence. Monica Lewinsky's 1995–98 look is, in one account, "the perfect example of what preppy style in the 1990s looked like — high necklines, classic patterns, and a very voluminous blowout were daily staples"; when the story broke, decision-makers made remarks about her hair and her body, which Leora Tanenbaum calls "a textbook case of the sexual double standard." The woman whose short hair could cost her a licence and a marriage had been replaced by one whose blowout was read as a fact about her morals.

## 2020s — the modern bob

What follows is a family, not a shape. GE Magazine's 2025 anatomy of the modern bob sets out seven iterations, organised by where the weight line sits and how blunt the edge is. The French bob is shorter, chin-length or higher, less structured, with a soft piecey fringe grazing the brow.

The Italian bob is longer, grazing the neck or shoulders, defined by its heavy weight line. The micro bob is a sharp crop at or just above the jawline. The box bob is cut all one length. The bixie keeps the bob's body, with shorter, textured layers at the nape and the ears. The layered bob holds the foundational weight, softened. The luxe bob, or lob, falls just above the collarbone; because its weight line sits lower, the grow-out is far more graceful.

The French bob has been redefined as well as renamed. Luke Hersheson, for Vogue, puts the change neatly: "Historically, what made a bob French was its chin, jaw or lip length… now the thought of Parisian or French hair is just about it feeling effortless."

Ryan Forsythe of Trevor Sorbie keeps it structural — "A French bob is similar to a one-length bob… no heavy layering… always hits the jawline or just below." George Northwood, London's "king of bobs," cuts with a razor for "a slightly jagged, textured edge… less harsh… more modern" — the shingle cut's logic arriving through a different instrument. In 2025 the "French Girl Bob" shortened to ear-to-chin length.

Then the pendulum came back. The blowout power bob, the shape being promoted for 2026, has sharp lines, clean edges and a high shine, usually cut just below the jaw or grazing the collarbone. What it is built on is graduated layering at the ends rather than harsh horizontal lines, and that is what enables a lift standard bobs cannot achieve.

The price is the short cut's convenience: a 2.5-inch round brush where a standard bob takes 1.5, about 18 minutes of work — 12 blow-drying, 3 for product, 3 for finish — and 10 inches or more of starting length. Julianne Hough wore one to the Tony Awards; Meghan Markle wore one in 2025.

The seven modern names and the 1909 anecdote describe one long argument about what a face is for. The 1909 version made a woman legible as a boy; the 1920s version put the face behind a helmet, Louise Brooks's black helmet; and the 2020s version, with a fringe grazing the brow and a clean graphic line, frames the face and takes the frame away again.

The shape is older than its inventor: Alice Liddell had it in 1860, and the earliest English account of the invention is thin enough to look retrofitted. The structure, though, is not thin.
