There is no word more used, in skin care marketing, than *barrier*. It is on every product brief. It is in every consultation. It anchors half the editorial copy on the internet about skin. And yet, when we asked six practitioners to define it for us this autumn, we received six answers that did not quite overlap.

What follows is a transcription of those conversations, edited for length and ordered by clinical convention rather than chronology.

---

## 1. Dr. Inès Ferrand — Dermatologist, Paris

**What is the barrier?**

The barrier, in the strict dermatological sense, is the stratum corneum and the way it maintains itself. It is a physical structure: fifteen to twenty layers of dead keratinocytes, embedded in a lipid matrix of ceramides, cholesterol, and free fatty acids. It is the body's most exterior surface, and its job is to prevent transepidermal water loss and to prevent the entry of pathogens, allergens, and irritants.

**Where does the popular use diverge?**

In the popular use, *barrier* has become a metaphor for *everything that is going wrong* on the skin. Rashes, redness, dehydration, sensitivity — all of these are now spoken of as *barrier damage*, regardless of whether the stratum corneum itself is compromised. Sometimes it is. Often it is not. The metaphor has expanded to absorb the word's clinical specificity, and the word has lost diagnostic value.

**How do you treat a barrier?**

If the barrier is truly compromised — and we can measure this with a Tewameter, looking at transepidermal water loss above 25g/m²/h in a healthy adult — the treatment is simple. We stop everything. We give the patient a bland, ceramides-containing emollient. We instruct them to avoid surfactants, retinoids, and any active for two to four weeks. The barrier repairs itself; it does not need much from us.

**What do you make of the industry?**

The industry has built an entire category — *barrier repair* — on a clinical situation that mostly resolves itself. The category is profitable because patients are afraid, and fear is easier to sell to than patience.

## 2. Mathilde Aubin — Facialist, Lyon

**What is the barrier?**

For me, the barrier is the *conversation* the skin is having with the environment. When I look at a face, I am reading what the skin has been doing — how it has responded to heat, cold, products, sleep, food. The barrier is the skin's pattern of response. A compromised barrier, in my practice, is a skin that is responding chaotically — flaring, breaking, peeling — to inputs that it should be able to absorb.

**How do you read it?**

Touch, mostly. I have been doing this for eighteen years. I can feel a barrier that is over-processed within the first fifteen seconds — it is reactive in a particular way, almost bristly. I can feel a barrier that is dehydrated, which is different: it is soft but empty, like a piece of fabric that has not been sufficiently wetted. There are about seven textures I can differentiate without thinking about it.

**What is the most common problem you see?**

Over-exfoliation. By an order of magnitude over any other complaint. Women, mostly, who have layered acids, retinoids, and physical exfoliants in pursuit of a glow that the barrier was already producing on its own.

**What is your prescription?**

I do not give one. I give the face what the face is asking for. If the barrier is reactive, I calm it. If it is dull, I enliven it. The industry wants to sell a fixed prescription. I have never had a fixed prescription work.

## 3. Dr. Hana Yamazaki — Cosmetic Chemist, Tokyo

**What is the barrier?**

In formulation, *barrier* usually means *the lipid lamellae* — the structured layers of ceramide, cholesterol, and fatty acids between the corneocytes. When we formulate a "barrier cream," we are trying to deliver these three classes of molecule in something close to the ratio the skin already uses.

**What does the formulation industry get wrong?**

We get the marketing of it wrong. In our labs, the barrier is a clean technical object. In the marketing copy, it becomes a fairytale about *strength* and *fortification*, as though the skin were a medieval castle we needed to defend. This is not how it works. The skin is more like a reef than a castle; you do not fortify a reef, you keep it healthy.

**Anything you would ban?**

The phrase "barrier repair" in product marketing, when the product has been tested only on an emulsified petrolatum base with no ceramides. Most "barrier repair" products on the market do not contain ceramides in bioavailable ratios. They are barrier*feel* products — they feel protective, and the user feels she is doing something. The actual barrier may not be touched at all.

## 4. Linnea Søndergaard — TCM-Trained Herbalist, Copenhagen

**What is the barrier?**

In my tradition, there is not a single barrier. There is the *wei qi*, the defensive qi at the surface of the body, and it has a different function than the western stratum corneum — it is dynamic, it circulates, it is part of the lung system, and it is influenced by the emotional state. To talk about the barrier outside the emotional context is, in my tradition, a category error.

**How do you treat it?**

First by listening to the patient for a long time. Often the skin issue is downstream of something else — a sleep pattern, a grief, a working life that the person can no longer sustain. I almost never begin with a topical. I begin with food and rest.

**What do you make of the western market?**

I think it has rediscovered something that has been in my tradition for a long time: that the skin and the rest of the body are one system. I am glad of that. I am less glad of how reductive the formulation is. We do not have *the barrier*; we have *a defensive system*, in conversation with all the others.

## 5. Sara Bekele — Former Dermatology Nurse, Now Formulator, Brooklyn

**What is the barrier?**

I came up in a dermatology clinic, and I saw a lot of barrier damage. Almost all of it, I realised after I left, was iatrogenic — caused by the prescribed treatments themselves. Steroids, retinoids, the overuse of acids. The barrier was strong, and we made it weak. Then we sold the patient the thing to fix the thing we broke.

**What do you do differently, in your own line?**

I do not sell actives. I sell the things you use *between* actives. A cleanser that does not strip. A ceramide serum. A bland balm. The patient still uses her retinol at night, if she wants to. But the rest of the routine is built around *not making her condition worse*.

**What is the industry mistake?**

The marketing of *strength*. The skin does not need to be strong. The skin needs to be intact. There is a difference. Strong is fortresses; intact is for *whole*. We have built an entire narrative around the metaphor of warfare, and it has produced a generation of skin that is over-treated and under-cared-for.

## 6. Olivia Chen — Esthetician, Los Angeles

**What is the barrier?**

In my practice, the barrier is what the client has between her and her triggers. For some clients, the barrier is environmental. For some, it is emotional. For some, it is the difference between a skin that tolerates a long-haul flight and a skin that erupts after a long-haul flight. I do not distinguish among these as rigorously as perhaps I should.

**What is the most common worry your clients come in with?**

"I think my barrier is damaged" — this phrase, verbatim, three or four times a week. Almost always, when I look, the barrier is not what the client thinks. The client thinks the barrier is a thin film that has been *removed*. In fact, the barrier has been *disrupted* — the lipid structure is uneven, the corneocytes are clumping, the microbiome has shifted. The metaphor has gone viral; the underlying anatomy has not kept up with the metaphor.

**What do you tell them?**

I tell them that the barrier is more capable than they think. I tell them to look at the skin of any older relative who never used anything on her face — that skin has a barrier, too, and it has held for seventy years without anyone's help.

---

## What we take from this

The word *barrier* is overused and underexamined. Six practitioners, in six different professional traditions, gave us six different definitions of it. None of them were wrong. None of them were complete.

What we would gently suggest is that the next time you see a product called a *barrier repair*, you ask: *according to whom?* The dermatologist's barrier is not the facialist's. The herbalist's is not the chemist's. And the skin, sitting there under your hand, is doing its work without needing you to name the work, let alone to *fix* it.

Be skeptical. Be patient. Use fewer things.
