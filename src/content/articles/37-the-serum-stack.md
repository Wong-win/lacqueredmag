The serum stack is, in our view, the routine most often got wrong. The getting wrong is, in our view, mostly a matter of *order*. The order is, in our view, also the only thing about the stack the consumer most often thinks does not matter. The order is, in our view, finally, the only thing that matters.

The order is, in our view, also the easiest thing to get right. The order is, in formulation terms, *thinnest first, thickest last.* The order is, in our view, also *water-based first, oil-based last.* The two rules are, in most cases, the same rule said twice. The rule is, in our view, finally, the rule the consumer should memorise before memorising any ingredient.

The seven steps below are, in our view, the practical application of the rule. The steps are, in our view, also the steps the modern skin care shelf, in 2026, most often does not teach. The teaching is, in our view, finally, the work of this guide.

## Step one — the cleanse

The first step is, in our view, the only step the consumer most often does not skip. The cleansing is, in our view, also the step most often done badly. The doing badly is, in our view, mostly a matter of *product*. The product is, in most cases, a cleanser that is, in formulation terms, too aggressive for the skin the consumer has. The cleanser is, in our view, also the product the consumer most often over-pays for. The over-paying is, in our view, finally, the loss the modern market has, in the last twenty years, been most unwilling to name.

The cleansing is, in our view, also the step most often rushed. The rushing is, in our view, mostly a function of the morning. The consumer has, in most cases, fifteen minutes, and the cleanser has, in most cases, been allotted ninety seconds. The ninety seconds are, in our view, finally, the difference between the morning's routine being effective and the morning's routine being, in effect, wasted.

The cleanser we would, in our view, use is a *low-pH gel cleanser*. The low pH is, in formulation terms, close to the skin's own pH. The low pH is, in our view, the pH that does not strip the barrier. The gel texture is, in our view, also the texture that rinses cleanly, without leaving a film that, in most cases, interferes with the next step.

## Step two — the hydrating toner

The second step is, in our view, the step the modern skin care shelf, in 2026, most often calls the *essence*. The essence is, in our view, also the product most often mis-named. The naming is, in our view, mostly a matter of marketing — the brand wants the consumer to believe the essence is, in formulation terms, a unique category. The essence is, in formulation terms, finally, a *hydrating toner* — a watery liquid that delivers humectants to the skin in a form the skin can absorb.

The hydrating toner is, in our view, the step that prepares the skin for the serum that follows. The preparing is, in formulation terms, mostly a matter of *hydration*. The hydrated skin is, in our view, the skin that absorbs the next layer more efficiently. The skin that is not hydrated is, in our view, the skin that, in most cases, *drinks* the next layer — the drinking is, in our view, the loss of product the consumer most often does not realise she is incurring.

The toner we would, in our view, use is a *humectant toner* — glycerin, hyaluronic acid at a low molecular weight, sometimes a touch of panthenol. The humectants are, in formulation terms, the ingredients that pull water into the skin. The pulling is, in our view, also the work the toner is doing for the next layer.

## Step three — the first serum

The third step is, in our view, the first serum in the stack. The first serum is, in our view, also the serum the consumer has, in most cases, decided the routine is, in our view, actually about. The first serum is, in our view, the *active serum* — the one with the active ingredient the consumer is, in formulation terms, trying to deliver.

The active serum is, in our view, the step most often over-loaded. The over-loading is, in our view, mostly a matter of *marketing* — the brand wants the consumer to believe the active serum is, in formulation terms, a single product that will solve the consumer's concerns. The single product is, in our view, finally, the lie the modern market most often tells. The truth is, in our view, that an active serum can, in most cases, address *one* concern well. The other concerns require other products.

The serum we would, in our view, use depends on the concern. For *photoageing*, a retinoid serum at a concentration the consumer's barrier can tolerate. For *hyperpigmentation*, a vitamin C serum at a concentration and pH that, in formulation terms, has been shown to deliver the active. For *dehydration*, a hyaluronic acid serum at multiple molecular weights. The serum is, in our view, finally, the one active the consumer has, in most cases, decided to prioritise.

## Step four — the second serum

The fourth step is, in our view, the second serum in the stack. The second serum is, in our view, also the serum most often *skipped*. The skipping is, in our view, mostly a matter of *time* — the consumer has, in most cases, decided the routine is, in our view, already too long, and the second serum is, in our view, the step that gets cut when the consumer is rushing.

The second serum is, in our view, the *supporting serum* — the serum that, in our view, does not have an active ingredient at a high concentration, and that is, in formulation terms, mostly there to *support* the first serum's work. The supporting is, in our view, mostly a matter of *hydration*, of *barrier support*, of *soothing*. The supporting is, in our view, also the work the second serum is doing while the first serum is doing its work.

The serum we would, in our view, use is a *niacinamide serum* — a low concentration, a simple formula, a serum that, in our view, plays well with almost everything. The niacinamide is, in formulation terms, an ingredient that supports the barrier, that regulates sebum, that, in our view, does a number of small things well without, in most cases, doing anything that *interferes* with the first serum. The not-interfering is, in our view, finally, the work.

## Step five — the moisturiser

The fifth step is, in our view, the moisturiser. The moisturiser is, in our view, also the step the consumer most often thinks is, in our view, redundant. The redundancy is, in our view, mostly a matter of the serum stack — the consumer has, in most cases, decided that the serums are doing the work, and the moisturiser is, in our view, finally, the product that is, in most cases, sold at the highest margin and delivers the least.

The moisturiser is, in our view, the step that *seals* the stack. The sealing is, in formulation terms, mostly a matter of *occlusion*. The occlusive layer is, in our view, the layer that prevents the water in the layers below from evaporating. The evaporation is, in our view, the loss the consumer most often does not realise she is incurring. The loss is, in our view, finally, the loss of the active ingredient the first serum delivered.

The moisturiser we would, in our view, use is a *ceramide moisturiser* — a cream that, in formulation terms, contains ceramides, cholesterol, and fatty acids in the ratio the skin's barrier naturally uses. The ratio is, in our view, finally, the work the moisturiser is doing for the barrier.

## Step six — the SPF (morning only)

The sixth step is, in our view, the SPF — and the SPF is, in our view, the only step in the routine that is, in our view, not optional. The SPF is, in our view, also the step the consumer most often applies *too little*. The too little is, in our view, mostly a matter of *texture* — the consumer has, in most cases, decided the SPF is, in our view, the step that pills under makeup, that sits on the skin, that feels, in most cases, like the routine is finally over.

The SPF is, in our view, the step that, in formulation terms, prevents the photoageing the first serum was, in our view, trying to address. The SPF is, in our view, also the step that, in formulation terms, prevents the hyperpigmentation the second serum was, in our view, trying to address. The SPF is, in our view, finally, the step that, in formulation terms, does the *most* work in the entire routine — and the work is, in our view, mostly invisible.

The SPF we would, in our view, use is an *SPF 50 PA++++* — a high-protection, broad-spectrum sunscreen, applied at a quarter teaspoon for the face, applied as the final step of the morning routine, applied *every morning*, including the mornings when the consumer is staying indoors. The indoor is, in our view, finally, the indoor the SPF most often gets skipped.

The order is, in our view, also water-based first, oil-based last. The order is, in our view, finally, the rule the consumer should memorise before memorising any ingredient.

The ingredients are, in our view, mostly interchangeable. The consumer could, in our view, swap the hyaluronic acid for a polyglutamic acid, the niacinamide for a beta-glucan, the ceramide cream for a squalane cream, and the routine would, in our view, in most cases, work just as well. The products are, in our view, finally, interchangeable in a way the order is not.

The order is, in our view, finally, the only thing the consumer needs to get right. The getting right is, in our view, finally, the work. The work is, in our view, finally, what the serum stack is for.
