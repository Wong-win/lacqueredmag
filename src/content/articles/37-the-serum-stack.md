The dropper is the whole marketing problem in one object: a pipette, a bead of clear liquid, the underside of the glass holding the light, and behind it a shelf of amber bottles each with a claim and a price. The industry sells layering as a menu to be ordered correctly, as though a list existed that experts had and you did not. There is no such list. The beauty desk hands over a mnemonic — *thinnest first, thickest last* — and treats it as the answer; it is folk wisdom repeated for decades without anyone identifying who said it first, and it is roughly right for the wrong reasons.

What follows the drop lands is physics, and physics is less interested in your choices than the shelf is. A 2026 review in *Skin Pharmacology and Physiology* of twelve studies on sequential application of topical medicines found absorption governed by the state of the barrier and by vehicle interactions, not by whether the bottle says serum or cream.

The effects run both ways: a urea emollient roughly doubled steroid delivery, petrolatum applied first reduced it, salicylic acid pretreatment increased betamethasone penetration. No single rule emerges, and no controlled trial on cosmetic application order exists. Two things survive: whether a layer is occluding what sits beneath it, and whether the next layer needs a dry surface or a particular pH.

## Step one — the cleanse

The stratum corneum is 10–20 micrometres thick and its lipids make up only about 10% of its weight, yet those lipids perform essentially the entire barrier function. The brick-and-mortar picture proposed by Elias and Menen in 1983 still holds: anucleate corneocytes, roughly 95% keratin, wrapped in a cross-linked envelope of involucrin and loricrin, the mortar being stacked intercellular lipid bilayers. By mass that mortar runs ceramides at about 40–50%, cholesterol at 20–25%, free fatty acids at 10–15%, the three near a 1:1:1 molar ratio in healthy skin.

The lipids arrive from lamellar bodies, secretory organelles about 0.3 micrometres across that sit in the granular layer carrying the enzymes that mature them, which is why the cleanser matters more than its price suggests.

Healthy skin sits at pH 4.5–5.5 and recommended cleansers run 4.0–6.0, while a soap bar sits at 9–10 and skin pH takes hours to recover. The surfactant mix decides more than pH does: sodium lauryl sulfate, then sodium laureth sulfate, other anionics, amphoterics such as cocamidopropyl betaine, then nonionics such as alkyl polyglucosides.

Technique is simpler than the ingredient list — lukewarm water at 32–34 °C, 30–60 seconds of contact, one or two washes a day, no scrubbing, moisturiser within three minutes — and recovery depends enormously on location.

After tape-stripping or acetone, surface pH normalised within 96 hours either way, yet forearm skin had recovered only about 12% of barrier function by 24 hours against more than 40% for facial skin. On *pilling*: a formulation phenomenon rather than a skin one, arriving when a thin layer is over-worked or over-dried before the next goes on.

## Step two — the hydrating toner

A toner is not a required step. Modern cleansers are already pH-balanced and skin self-corrects after washing, which is why the industry's first toner claim — that it restores pH after cleansing — is unsupported by independent studies.

Pore size is set by genetics, oil production and sun damage, and astringents tighten only briefly; that a toner helps later products absorb better has no evidence either. What a hydrating toner does is humectancy, binding water in the upper epidermis, which makes it a lightweight first coat of moisturiser rather than a category of its own.

Gloria Lu, a cosmetic chemist, told The Cut that the toner is never going to be the step doing the heavy lifting, and the ingredient list is where judgment happens. Alcohol denat. at 20–60% strips protective lipids and denatures epidermal proteins; the *squeaky clean* feeling it produced came from oxidised skin oils lifted onto the cotton pad rather than from grime.

Witch hazel and essential oils can irritate or sensitise. AHAs and BHAs belong to the exfoliant category, and applying to damp skin may work against the actives that follow, since surface water inhibits retinoid penetration.

## Step three — the first serum

The strongest single-ingredient evidence in skincare belongs to niacinamide. Bissett and colleagues in 2004 ran 50 Caucasian women aged 40 to 60 for 12 weeks in a double-blind, placebo-controlled, split-face randomised design, 5% on one side and vehicle moisturiser on the other, and found significant improvements in fine lines, hyperpigmented spots, texture, red blotchiness and yellowing.

A follow-up dose–response study in *Dermatologic Surgery* in 2005 tested 2% and 5% twice daily for eight weeks: both beat vehicle, the difference between them was modest, and there was no 10% arm.

The mechanism is direct, which makes niacinamide one of the few actives that both penetrates efficiently and has a defined endpoint at the barrier: a 2021 randomised trial of 48 people using 3% twice daily found improved corneocyte envelope maturation and hydrophobicity at multiple facial sites.

A 2024 review in the International Journal of Molecular Sciences concluded that topical nicotinamide at around 4–5% is generally well tolerated and consistently improves photoaging parameters, increases hydration and reduces water loss, and inhibits melanosome transfer, while flagging small samples and short follow-up.

Vitamin C is governed by a harder constraint. Pinnell's 2001 rule holds that L-ascorbic acid enters skin only when formulated below pH 3.5, below its pKa of roughly 4.1–4.2, where it is uncharged and diffuses passively; absorption rose with concentration to about 20% and then plateaued, tissue saturating in three days with a half-life near four days.

The stabilisation data are just as specific: in Lin and colleagues' 2005 *Burns* paper, 0.5% ferulic acid held more than 90% of the L-ascorbic acid after a month at 45 °C, and a 15% vitamin C, 1% α-tocopherol, 0.5% ferulic acid stack doubled photoprotection from four-fold to eight-fold. The counter-evidence deserves equal space: a 2020 study found a commercial 15% vitamin C, E and ferulic formulation reduced skin explant viability against saline control.

## Step four — the second serum

The retinoid ladder shows how far marketing outruns data. The line that retinal is 11 times stronger than retinol comes from laboratory conversion work, not clean head-to-head human trials, and no trial has titrated retinaldehyde against retinol to find an equivalence point.

The direct tolerance data say otherwise: Fluhr and colleagues' 1999 study found retinol and retinaldehyde equally low in irritation potential under 14-day patch testing, with retinoic acid significantly worse. Creidi's 1998 trial of 125 people with photodamage found no significant efficacy difference between 0.05% retinaldehyde and 0.05% retinoic acid. What does scale cleanly is concentration: erythema and flaking ran at about 18% in a 0.05% retinal group against roughly 32% at 0.1%.

Azelaic acid is the best-evidenced brightening and acne active that is a cosmetic rather than a drug in most markets, with a regulatory wrinkle: in the United States every FDA-approved gel and foam is 15%, the approved 20% cream is for mild-to-moderate inflammatory acne, and melasma is off-label for all of them.

The 2020 Cochrane review pooled 49 trials and about 3,880 participants, finding it probably slightly less effective than benzoyl peroxide but probably little or no different from tretinoin, which is why the 2024 American Academy of Dermatology guideline gives it only a conditional recommendation. For rosacea the evidence is stronger still: Cochrane pooled 106 trials and 13,631 participants and found azelaic acid superior to placebo with high-quality evidence.

Exfoliation is where the percentage on the label is least meaningful, because pH matters as much as concentration — a 10% mandelic solution at pH 4.0 barely penetrated while 40% at pH 1.5 exfoliated significantly — and AHAs do not neutralise themselves, so inadequate rinsing risks delayed burns.

Hyaluronic acid turns on molecular weight rather than concentration: 50 and 130 kDa fragments significantly improved wrinkle depth in a 76-woman trial, while 1,000–1,400 kDa is essentially impermeable. Peptides are weakest of all, resting on small, industry-funded, vehicle-controlled trials.

## Step five — the moisturiser

Three functional classes do three jobs, and most confusion in the moisturiser aisle comes from treating them as one. Humectants attract and bind water; emollients fill the intercellular lipid gaps and soften; occlusives form a physical film that reduces water loss without adding any.

The occlusive numbers are stark: petrolatum cuts transepidermal water loss by roughly 98–99%, against about 30% for mineral oil and 60–70% for beeswax. Squalane is the elegant member, hydrogenated squalene with six double bonds removed and so no oxidation pathway; at 410.7 g/mol it works purely at the surface, softening dehydration lines rather than synthesising collagen.

The layering rule lives here. Bucks and colleagues' 1988 crossover study, five subjects, found that occlusion applied after a product raised absorption of lipophilic steroids by up to sixfold but did nothing for hydrophilic hydrocortisone; Pellanda's 2007 work found post-occlusion doubled triamcinolone penetration while pre-occlusion had no significant effect.

That asymmetry is the whole of the retinol-sandwiching logic, and it is why a heavy *occlusive* over a retinoid is not a neutral act. Humectants need sealing for the same reason: they pull only from ambient air above about 70% relative humidity.

The barrier-repair evidence for a lipid-complete moisturiser is real. Man and colleagues' 1996 work set the physiological target — ceramide, cholesterol and free fatty acid near 3:1:1 molar — and showed that in disrupted skin all three are required, because applying one or two delays recovery, while raising any one up to threefold accelerates it.

Recent randomised trials agree: Spada's 2021 trial of 100 adults with moderate eczema improved water loss and hydration over four weeks, and Andrew's 2025 lipidomic study cut post-tape-strip loss from 38.02 to 29.79 g/m²/h while a lipid-free control showed no change. Tables claiming a 2–5% ceramide threshold come from brand content with unverifiable citations.

## Step six — the SPF (morning only)

In the United States, sunscreens are over-the-counter drugs rather than cosmetics, and any SPF claim must comply with 21 CFR Part 201 and Monograph M020, in rulemaking since a proposed rule in 2019. The number on the bottle is a laboratory measurement with a fixed protocol: under ISO 24444, SPF is determined by phototesting at an application thickness of 2 mg/cm², and the figure is the protected minimal erythema dose divided by the unprotected.

Here is the failure. People apply 0.5–1.3 mg/cm², a quarter to a half of the tested dose, and because protection does not scale linearly an SPF 30 applied thinly often behaves like SPF 10 or less.

The best independent field measurement found a median 21% of accessible skin across 17 volunteers in swimwear completely uncovered, and covered skin receiving 1.12 mg/cm² rather than 2. Taylor and Diffey's 2002 letter in the *BMJ* derived the two-finger rule from the medical rule of nines, then conceded the volume was unrealistic.

Practical quantities are 1.2–2 mL for face and neck; Diffey's modelling put the most valuable reapplication 15–30 minutes after sun exposure, and a 2022 study found 78% of outdoor workers lost more than half their UV protection within 90 minutes.

Composition matters as much as quantity: avobenzone photodegrades, octocrylene stabilises it, octinoxate accelerates the decay. In the European Union a relative SPF needs a UVA protection factor of at least a third of the labelled SPF and a critical wavelength of 370 nm; the United States has no such ratio.

And products fail: the Hong Kong Consumer Council found only four of fourteen SPF 30–50 samples met EU criteria, and a 2023 Dutch investigation found 36 of 54 products below their labelled claim. None of which makes sunscreen a waste of time.

The anchor is Green and colleagues' randomised trial of 1,689 Australian adults followed for five years, in which daily SPF 50+ use reduced the risk of invasive melanoma by about half and squamous cell carcinoma by roughly 18%. That is the honest version of the argument: your bottle is not performing as labelled, because nobody applies the dose the label assumes, and the label is a laboratory claim about an application rate nobody achieves. Daily use still halves melanoma risk.

Which returns us to the dropper. It is not a decision; it is a quantity. The order in which layers go on is not a rule handed down by experts but a question of whether what you put on last changes the surface, and whether what sits under the film needs to get in.

Water-based first, oil-based last, occlusive last of all, and never seal an active you wanted to absorb. Everything else — the toner, the second serum, the extra step — is detail, and most people running eight products are running the same three ingredients several times.
