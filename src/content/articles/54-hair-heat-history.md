Two flat irons, heated and pressed together: that is the arrangement described in Isaac K. Shero's U.S. patent 943321, issued 14 December 1909. It was the first hair straightener built that way and the direct ancestor of the object now sold as a flat iron, filed ninety years before the boom that made the thing ordinary.

Hair damage is usually filed the other way round: a modern affliction, manufactured by the modern flat iron, with a century of salon curling written out of the record. By the 1870s a Parisian hairdresser was already regulating four variables at once on a hot iron worked against a client's hair — temperature, pressure, timing, movement — with no thermostat anywhere, so the metal was judged by eye and by a scrap of paper. If the paper burned, the iron was too hot.

The operator at the start of this story and the one at the end are making the same guess about the same variable, and the argument the trade has been having with itself ever since is whether the answer to a hot tool is a cooler tool or a better promise. On the evidence it has overwhelmingly been the promise.

## The nineteenth century — the first heated iron

Start with the implement. François Marcel Grateau, who lived from 18 October 1852 to 31 May 1936, worked in Paris through the Belle Époque and is credited with the heated iron as a hairdressing instrument: two curved metal rods joined by a spring mechanism and heated externally, over gas flames. What came out of it took his name — a *Marcel wave*, a thing to marcel — and it did not last: the waves disappeared after the first washing or brushing.

The date is not settled, and anyone who offers one is taking a side. The year most often repeated is 1872, and 1875 is also in circulation; other accounts put the work in the 1870s and 1880s, the perfected technique in the mid-1880s, the modern curling iron around 1890. The patent record complicates the picture instead of settling it: Grateau's U.S. 806386, "Curling-Iron", was published in 1905, and his electric "Hair-Waving Iron" followed in 1918 as U.S. 1277739. Neither is the early French filing the 1872 story implies.

With no thermostat on the market, irons were heated over open fires, stoves, chimney stoves and alcohol or spirit lamps, then tested on a scrap of paper; if the paper burned, the metal was too hot. An 1895 Montgomery Ward & Co. catalogue offered both chimney-stove and alcohol-lamp curling-iron heaters. Soot was the second hazard, transferred into the hair with the smell to prove it; the trade answered with gloves, wooden handles, and nickel-plated or floral fittings on the luxury models.

It was a manufactured product with a supply chain, easy to lose in a history told as a story about one man's genius: R. Timmins & Sons of Birmingham, steel makers established in 1790, made nineteenth-century barber's curling tongs in steel with heavy flat ends; L. Pelleray of Paris exported Marcel-style tongs, heated over a gas burner, worldwide.

## 1906 — the Nessler perm

The 1906 in that heading is the year the trade attaches to a German hairdresser, Karl Ludwig Nessler, who lived from 1872 to 1951, worked in Switzerland and London, and built the first practical permanent-wave system. The date deserves a footnote: the fullest account of his method dates the system to around 1905 and carries no demonstration record, no patent number and no treatment length. A London demonstration in October 1906 appears in some tellings, and nothing in the surviving record settles it.

What is well documented is the method. Hair wound tightly, a chemical preparation, heat under electrical control and mechanical tension — together these altered the internal structure of the hair so a curl could last for months.

The contrast with Grateau is the point of the era: an iron that made a wave lasting a wash, against a machine that made one lasting months. His machine was large and complex, with multiple heated rods suspended above the client's head, every strand processed individually; even, whole-head heat was not possible until later machines.

Out of it came an industry. Nessler established that hair could be permanently reshaped, which helped turn hairdressing into a trained, equipment-based trade, while the iron carried on patenting: Grateau's U.S. "Curling-Iron" in 1905, Shero's two-plate straightener in 1909, Grateau's electric "Hair-Waving Iron" in 1918. One caution, since the date travels badly: 1906 is regularly filed as the date of the first electric curling iron, credited to a Charles Nestlé. That is the wrong story.

## 1960s — the geometric cut, the iron-set style

The plainest fact of the decade is also the driest: women in the 1960s sometimes used clothing irons to straighten their hair. The domestic clothes iron and the beauty iron were the same object, sold in the same shops, and only the intention differed.

The dominant cut was geometric, and it was Vidal Sassoon's — first London salon in 1954, after training with Raymond Bessone; a short, angular bob cut on a horizontal plane in 1964; the asymmetrical bowl he created for Peggy Moffitt, known as the five point; and Mia Farrow's pixie cut for *Rosemary's Baby* in 1968, after Roman Polanski brought him to Hollywood.

His own account of the method is a sentence about subtraction — eliminate the superfluous and get down to the basic angles of cut and shape. A cut defined by angles and planes is a cut built to hold its shape. The tool that carried the decade was air, not contact heat: better motors and improved plastics made plastic-bodied hair dryers practical and lighter through the 1960s, with the bonnet dryer reaching consumers in 1951 and GEC moving the motor inside the casing in 1954.

The dryer came with a safety record the iron has never had. Handhelds in the 1920s weighed about 1 kg (2 lb) and ran at 100 W against up to 2,000 W in modern dryers; the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission set safety-manufacturing guidelines in the 1970s, since 1991 U.S. dryers have been required to use a ground-fault circuit interrupter, and by 2000 reported annual deaths involving blow dryers had fallen from under ten to fewer than two.

The hot comb stayed in the picture, Madame C.J. Walker popularising wider-toothed combs and Annie Malone sometimes said to have patented it. By the time the geometric cut arrived the older technique had become a line of dialogue: in *Some Like It Hot* (1959) Tony Curtis's character mentions a girlfriend who has just had her hair marcelled.

## 2000s — the flat iron boom

The flat iron is not a 2000s invention, and this is where the popular version goes wrong. Its direct ancestor is Shero's 1909 straightener — two flat irons heated and pressed together, issued 14 December 1909 — ninety years earlier, in an age of gas flames and paper tests.

What the 2000s brought was the plate rather than the tool: ceramic and electrically heated straighteners appeared later, enabling adjustable heat and different tool sizes, and ceramic plates dominate today, a single layer at the low end and multiple layers or entirely ceramic at the top.

Adjustable heat is the mechanical fact sitting underneath the advertising, and it arrives alongside automatic shut-off on some straighteners, offered for one plain purpose: to prevent fire accidents. That is the innovation. A century of guesswork about temperature became a dial, and the dial became a category.

The science underneath is worth stating precisely. Straightening irons break down positive hydrogen bonds in the hair cortex, allowing the hair to open and bend, and those bonds can reform when the hair is exposed to moisture. Heat damage is therefore partly a reversible bond-breaking problem, which is exactly what the no-damage claims deny. The safety language in the sources is modest: overuse of these tools can cause severe damage to hair.

The boom's tail end is documented — a ceramic straightening-brush patent published on 21 November 2013 and Sharon Rabi's first straightening brush in 2015 — while the record contains no launch year, no manufacturer, no brand and no price for the 2000s flat iron. The decade can only be described through the hardware it made.

## 2020s — the modern iron

A modern iron's bill of materials is short. Ceramic plates, multilayer or solid ceramic at the top of the market; adjustable heat settings; automatic shut-off on many models; a barrel palette of ceramic, metal, Teflon, titanium and tourmaline in sizes from 0.5 in (1.3 cm) to 2 in (5.1 cm); three handle types, clipless, Marcel and spring-loaded, with spring-loaded the most popular, and Kevlar gloves shipped with many clipless wands to reduce burn risk.

Set that beside the nineteenth-century list — temperature, pressure, timing, movement — and the pitch is unchanged in a century. The modern tool sells the first of those four as a digital feature.

The physiological claim needs the same care. Hair damage is real, and the literature describes it as severe with overuse; the mechanism is hydrogen-bond breakage in the cortex that moisture can partly reverse.

The framing that exists in the sources is do not overuse, not this temperature is safe, and no source consulted for this piece puts a number on the temperature at which hair is damaged or counts the passes that do it. The number on the display is a claim about the tool, not a guarantee about the head.

The regulation went to the dryer rather than the iron. The dryer's clever features are the closest analogue to an iron's digital controls, and one ships with a caveat. The cool shot disables the heater and blows room-temperature air to set a style, while ionic operation is meant to reduce static buildup, though its effectiveness is disputed.

The diffuser exists for the same reason the dial does — it disperses the airflow so hair is less disturbed and dries more slowly at a cooler temperature, which is the physics of lowering an iron's temperature sold as an attachment.

The last word belongs to the paper. In the 1870s an operator judged the temperature of an iron by whether a scrap of paper burned; in the 2020s the operator reads a number on a display. Both are guesses about the same variable, and the paper is the more honest instrument, because it shows you what it measures.

Heat damage has been a marketing problem far longer than an engineering one, and the physical injury — a burnt scalp, a singed strand — has been constant since the first flame. The professional protocol survives as habit rather than instruction, because it was never printed: less heat, more often.
