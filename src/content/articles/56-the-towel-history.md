There is a number the towel trade uses to measure quality: GSM, grams per square metre, checked with a cutter that punches a 100 cm² square from the cloth and weighs it. Convention rather than regulation — no ISO or EN standard obliges a towel to carry a figure — but it governs the category. The bands run from 300–400 for gym, pool and beach towels through 500–600, the retail standard, to 600–700 for the plush and five-star benchmark, and 700 upwards for what the trade calls ultra-luxury.

On that scale the towel looks thoroughly improved and almost entirely unconsidered, and there is a reason for it. The category was industrialised later than nearly any other household textile: linen was a documented organised industry in the Linear B tablets of Pylos, and by 1846 Britain was legislating public baths that issued towels with the ticket.

The terry pile arrived in 1850 and 1851, which means the object on your rail this morning is roughly 170 years old in its current form. That is not an argument for nostalgia. It is an argument for a question the trade never asks, which is not how soft a towel is but how long it takes to dry.

## Antiquity — the linen

No towel is documented from ancient Egypt, which is worth sitting with: most of the pages explaining the history of towels open on tomb paintings of people drying themselves on woven flax towels, a claim that traces to uncited commercial pages rather than to any document.

The record does hold linen — dyed flax fibres some 36,000 years old from a cave in the Southern Caucasus, flax of about 8,000 BC in the Swiss lake dwellings, a woven flax textile at Çatalhöyük around 7,000 BC — and it holds cotton far later, the Mehrgarh fibres being the earliest known cotton in the Old World (Moulherat et al., 2002). The towel and the cotton are not contemporaries.

The written record is nearly as old. Mesopotamia domesticated flax for the wealthy, priests above all; in Egypt the fibre went into mummification, and mummy wrappings worked as a symbol of light and purity and a display of wealth at once. The Tarkhan dress, dated between 3482 and 3102 BC, is one of the oldest woven garments in the world.

The closest thing to a picture shows work rather than a towel: in the Beni Hasan tombs of Amenemhat (BH2) and Khnumhotep II (BH3), of the 12th Dynasty, men wash cloth, wring it, beat it and stretch it on sand to dry before bleaching, in painted facsimiles made in situ by Norman de Garis Davies for the Met's 1931 Graphic Expedition.

The earliest specific reference to a cloth called a towel is stranger: Strabo, in the late 1st century BCE, on asbestos combed and woven into towels, cleaned by burning rather than washing.

What linen does is why the rest of the story hangs together. The fibre resists dirt and stains, has no lint or pilling tendency, withstands high temperatures, shrinks only moderately at first, and is up to three times stronger than cotton. Wet cloth gains strength rather than losing it, the tensility increasing by 20%.

It biodegrades in a few weeks when buried in soil. No source states how long anyone kept a towel, and 'a piece of linen kept for a lifetime' is an inference rather than a documented figure — but it follows from everything else here. For most of the object's history a towel was a length of linen laundered with the rest of the household's washing, expected to outlast the decade.

## The Middle Ages — the hemp

By the late Middle Ages the word *linens* had become shorthand for household goods — bedding, tablecloths, napkins and towels — so the medieval towel was not a bathroom object but a category of household linen, washed with everything else.

The word is Germanic, not Latin, and unrelated to English 'toil': Middle English towayle and its variants, in use from about 1250, came through Old French toaille from Medieval Latin *toallia*, and ultimately from a Proto-Germanic root meaning 'to wash'. The tidy version, in which 'towel' is a corruption of a Latin verb meaning 'to cleanse', is folk etymology.

Nor did Europe stop bathing: public baths remained common in larger cities through the 12th century — Paris reportedly had 26 of them in 1272 — and Southwark's medieval bagnios were closed under Henry VIII in 1546, a fact about religious feeling rather than about hygiene vanishing.

Hemp and nettle stood in for flax when supplies ran short, particularly among poorer communities, and hemp — tougher than flax — got the heavy work, including a coarse Russian cloth called *motely* for undergarments. The scale of the trade is not a domestic detail.

Flax was a primary Russian commercial commodity from the 11th century; through the 14th and 15th centuries the Hanseatic League exported thousands of flax bales annually from its kontor offices in Novgorod and Livonia, and flax revenues ranked below only the fur trade.

Heidi Sherman, writing on 'From Flax to Linen in the Medieval Rus Lands' in Medieval Clothing and Textiles vol. 4 (2008), notes that Novgorod's Peterhof kontor shipped flax and hemp for rope-making in shipyards. Production was dispersed: textiles, primarily linen and wool, were made in decentralised home weaving mills, and the linen trade reached Germany by the 9th century and Flanders and Brabant by the 11th.

The medieval evidence also warns against reading the modern noun backwards. The medieval Russian *ubrus* was a woman's headdress: chronicles record the *povoy*, a towel-like veil wound around the head to cover all the hair, the ends falling over the shoulders and chest, and a 12th-century Novgorod legal code punished tearing a married woman's povoy with a fine twice as high as for damaging a cloak.

Archaeologists have found such headdresses in 10th- and 11th-century peasant graves in the Vologda region and in a 12th-century burial at Povorovka near Moscow, sometimes weighted at the ends and embroidered with glass beads.

A second object carried the same name, a long band of cloth hung over an icon with the ends falling to either side, made in five-piece versions for Easter and Christmas and decorated with silk embroidery, bracteates, pearls and silver tassels, surviving from the 16th century onward. A towel, in other words, has always been priced as a unit of bodily respect.

## The eighteenth century — the damask

The modern towel's technical ancestor is Turkish rather than European, and it turns up in the seventeenth century. Bursa is the place: early Turkish towels were flat woven cotton or linen textiles called *peshtemal*, often hand-embroidered, long enough to wrap the body and absorbent when wet, used in the hammam. Then the pile arrived.

By the eighteenth century towels had loops rising from it, called havly; the term later became *havlu*, the Turkish word for towel, meaning 'with loops'. A 17th-century Bursa workshop produced early looped-terry towels on a two-warp-beam loom — a ground warp for a stable base, a slack pile warp pushed into loops by the weft — and that loom is the direct technical ancestor of the terry towel.

The eighteenth-century towel was also a damask, which is a more technical thing than it sounds. Damask is a reversible figured weave whose figure is warp-faced satin and whose ground is weft-faced sateen, structurally opposite fabrics, the pattern visible only as a shift in light reflection, the long satin floats catching the light differently depending on where the viewer stands.

The name comes from Damascus, but the city was a conduit rather than an origin: Chinese drawlooms may have produced damasks as early as the Tang dynasty (618–907), crusaders carried the fabric out of Damascus in the 11th century, and the word first appears in a Western European language in mid-14th-century French records. Italian weavers spent a week or more warping the loom and held the patterns in their heads until the Jacquard loom's punched cards replaced memory and made the work faster and cheaper.

Then cotton broke the material monopoly, and the law fought it for a century. Britain's Calico Acts ran from a 10% tariff on East Indian goods in 1685 through the 1700 Act, which locked up printed calicoes until re-export, to the pivotal 1721 Act banning the sale and use of printed, painted or stained callicoes in apparel, in 'household stuff' and in furniture — and even in warehouses for re-export.

Repeal came in 1774 and triggered investment in mill-based cotton spinning: raw cotton demand doubled within two years and doubled again every decade through the 1840s. France's ban ran 73 years from 1686, with penalties reaching the galleys and death by 1726, and was lifted in 1759 with a 25% tariff. One correction to the period's fashion: it was *lampas*, not damask, that the mid-to-late eighteenth century treated as the expensive patterned fabric.

## The twentieth century — the terry

The industrial terry towel is English, and the date is 1850–1851 rather than a single event in 1847. Samuel Holt's first machine ran in 1848; by 1851 he had a design of two warps on a loom, one of which formed the looped surface, and he patented it on 24 March and again on 24 September that year as an improvement in the manufacture of textile fabrics.

Holt had joined W M Christy & Co in 1834, and Christy began industrial production in 1850 on his machine, based on handwoven terrycloth seen in Turkey, under the name Queen Victoria approved: 'Royal Turkish Towels'. The 1847 date deserves a note, because secondary sources keep attaching it to Peter Foxcroft of Bury, Lancashire.

His patents run 1853, 1856 and 1857 — for doubling cotton, for roving and spinning, and for processing cotton wool — and no 1847 terry patent, no patent number and no loops-per-inch figure can be found for him anywhere.

The pivot was the Great Exhibition of 1851. The towels were shown in London, the exhibition awarded Holt a gold medal, and on 14 April 1851 Queen Victoria ordered six-dozen towels; the firm then began mass production and took over Holt's patents in 1860 for a yearly annuity of £30. It is the argument in miniature: the towel was sold as exotic heritage, Turkish and royal, at the moment it stopped being a curiosity and became a commodity.

The towel was also becoming a public institution. The Public Baths and Wash-houses Act received royal assent on 26 August 1846, and London's first municipal baths opened at Goulston Square in 1847, advertising a cold bath at 5d. and a warm one at 6d. for the first class with two towels included, against 1d. and 2d. for the second class with one.

Soap was the extra. The 1846 Act was ignored for decades because the baths were sited in the slums and popularly equated with the workhouse; an 1879 medical officer put it that one man can bring a horse to water but a dun cannot make it drink — a reported quotation whose source could not be opened — and the real surge came in the 1890s. A towel priced into the ticket was never a luxury object in the way a silk damask was.

Here is the physics, which the marketing has never been interested in. Terrycloth is woven with many protruding loops of thread which can absorb large amounts of water, on special looms with two beams of longitudinal warp through which the weft is fired laterally, and what determines how much fluid the cloth takes is the length of the loops — not the thread count, and not the softness.

GSM is therefore a proxy for a physical fact rather than a luxury scale: each additional 100 grams per square metre adds roughly 15 to 20 minutes of natural drying time. A heavier towel is a slower towel. Britain learned that under wartime conditions, where the Board of Trade's Utility scheme began in February 1942 with a CC41 mark designed by Reginald Shipp, fixing minimum specifications of material, size and weight at controlled prices, and by 1943 some 80% of British clothing output was utility.

## The 2020s — the modern towel

The industry has moved east. Denizli is the capital of the Turkish towel trade, and the export figures its chamber DENİB published for 2024 give total Denizli exports of $4.4 billion, up 4.7% on the year, with textile and apparel at $1.4 billion — 32% of the total — and 1,700 active exporters, the United States among the top five markets.

The branding dates from 2015 and 2016, when 'Turkish Towels' and its Turquality project began attaching the country to the product; Buldan, in Denizli province, is the historic peshtemal centre. The category is governed as well as made: the hotel towel runs on a ritual, on the rack meaning reuse and on the floor meaning replace, a programme that began in the 1990s as a cost and environmental measure and is now standard across the chains.

The fibre question is a water question. The 2,700-litre figure that circulates is the global average water footprint of a cotton T-shirt, from Mekonnen and Hoekstra's work for the Water Footprint Network (UNESCO-IHE Value of Water Report No. 18, 2006), and about 2,300 litres of it is green water — rainfall the plant absorbs anyway — with only 500 to 800 litres of blue water, the part that competes for irrigation.

UNEP puts the textile industry's water use at around 79 billion cubic metres a year. Against that, flax is rain-fed in Normandy, Flanders and the Netherlands, with more than 80% of European flax grown without irrigation and without synthetic pesticides; rain-fed linen comes to roughly 500 litres per kilogram of fabric against 2,700 for cotton, about 15 kilograms of CO₂e per kilogram against 22 to 28, and a lifespan of 8 to 15 years against 3 to 5. Bamboo, the material everyone is now sold, is usually rayon: almost all bamboo towels are viscose, at around 1,800 litres per kilogram, or lyocell at around 1,400.

None of that survives the mill unchanged: heavy wet processing, chemical softening and long-distance shipping erode the crop-stage advantage, and for a long-lived item the use phase dominates — cold washing, line drying, washing every three or four uses, replacing towels every 18 to 24 months.

The cheap modern substitute is a short-loop synthetic microfibre, nonabsorbent but wicking, which dries in about an hour against up to a day for cotton terry while trapping water between the fibres rather than absorbing it, and which sheds microplastics: around 700,000 fibres per 6 kg acrylic wash load, on a 2016 count we have not opened, found in human blood and lung tissue.

Which leaves the question the trade has avoided for half a century. A wet towel is a drying problem. The industry's answer has been to sell a heavier object — and the object it has been selling since 1851 is 170 years old.
