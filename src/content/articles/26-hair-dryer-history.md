US Patent 389,803 describes no hand crank. Alexandre F. Godefroy, a French hairdresser in St. Louis, filed it on 13 April 1888; it was granted on 18 September. What the claims describe is a head case for shampooing and drying, fed by a furnace through a pipe, with a flue at the top and an apron at the rear edge to carry water and suds away.

No fan appears in the claims, and the gas-stove chimney with a hand-driven fan, repeated in every history, is a later retelling. Even the year is contested: Wikipedia and ABC News say 1888, while a 2016 New York Times slideshow and Mashable say 1890. Only the filing and grant dates are primary. Patents precede products here, category names get back-dated by advertising, and the shape of the appliance is set less by the motor than by the cord.

## 1900 — the bonnet dryer

The round number in the section title is doing a lot of work. The first electric dryer tied to a maker and a museum record is an AEG prototype of 1899, built under chief engineer Michał Doliwo-Dobrowolski: motor inside the handle, 2 kg, air up to 90 °C. It is a bench object, not a product, but the architecture is there — weight in the hand, heat in the stream.

Berlin supplied the name. Sanitas launched the Foen in 1908 and registered the trademark on 22 January 1909; the word comes from the warm Alpine wind, the *Föhn*. A 1911 model weighed just under a kilogram and sold for 45 Marks, advertised as "Sehr leicht. Kein Ermüden der Hand" — very light, no tiring of the hand.

The hazards were chemical as much as thermal. Gas-heated and early electric units could give off carbon monoxide, so salons needed ventilation; hoods without a steam-release valve risked burns and, in the period phrase, a "gently cooked" head. A surviving Powerhouse Collection example, probably Australian and dated c.1920–1930, is a 240-volt machine with a wooden handle and a nickel-plated body.

Hooded dryers were in salons by the 1930s, suited to the weekly "hair set" that middle-class clients expected: heat applied evenly, for a long time, over a head already wound in rollers. That is the bonnet's argument, and it has not changed. The handheld would win the home; the salon kept the hood for decades.

## 1920 — the handheld

The first handheld is a patent, not a launch. Gabriel M. Kazanjian filed US 994,259 on 20 November 1908 and received it on 6 June 1911; the claims describe a handle secured to the casing, a hand-operated lever driving a screw-bladed fan through a gear train, and an electric heating coil inside a detachable spout. "A lady can conveniently operate the device."

Wikipedia contradicts itself — the lead says 1912, the history section 1911 — and the patent settles it. What 1920 marks is the market. Handhelds began to be sold that year by National Stamping and Electric Works, under its White Cross brand, by the Racine Universal Motor Company and by Hamilton Beach.

The 1920s machine was heavy and weak: about 2 lb, or 0.9 to 1 kg, and roughly 100 watts, against up to 2,000 W for a modern dryer. It overheated and it electrocuted people; ABC News records metal bodies, faulty wiring and several deaths from bathroom use. Wattage could not rise until the wiring behind the wall could carry it.

Demand came from a haircut. The early-1920s shift from the Gibson Girl updo to the bob and the flapper cut exposed limp or dirty hair that a pompadour had hidden, and the dryer made the new shape practical at home. "Having clean, shiny, fluffy hair — that's a 20th-century thing," the Cornell historian Rachel Maines told The Atlantic.

## 1950 — the salon hood

1951 is the standard date for the consumer soft-bonnet dryer: a portable box connected by a tube to a perforated plastic bonnet, which gave more even heat than a handheld. Histories differ on 1950 or 1951, and the distinction matters less than what the object did. It put a salon process in a bedroom for the price of a small appliance.

The material change is the real story of the decade. Lightweight plastics replaced heavy metal and Bakelite housings, and by the 1950s, ABC News says, "it was a rare household that didn't possess one." AEG bought Sanitas in 1957 and with it the Fön mark, so rivals marketed Haartrockner.

What the money bought was sold as comfort rather than speed. GE advertised "72 tiny air jets," an interior roomy enough for rollers, quiet operation and adjustable settings. Sales through May 1961 were twice what they had been in 1960, according to Electrical Merchandising Week.

The salon's social role is the decade's best cultural evidence. The Atlantic describes the post-war salon as a "cherished second space" outside the home, and the row of women under dryer hoods as "a symbol of postwar prosperity and of women's new leisure time." Bonnet advertising borrowed the image, showing women chatting on the telephone while their hair dried.

## 1970 — the blow-dryer as cultural object

The layout that made the pistol grip possible arrived in 1954, when GEC moved the motor inside the casing — fan and heater in one body, the arrangement every later dryer inherited. Conair turned it into a business. Incorporated in 1959 as Continental Hair Products with $100 of capital, it sold pistol-grip styler-dryers to salons from 1968 and reached retail with the Conair Pro-Style in late 1971.

By 1977 Conair offered eight basic pistol-grip models and held the largest US share of that market. The growth curve is the argument: sales ran $1.1 million in 1968, $12.6 million in 1973, $24.7 million in 1975, $36.4 million in 1976 and $53 million in 1977 — then a $2.1 million loss in 1978.

Farrah Fawcett, of Charlie's Angels, fronted a Lady Schick Speed Styler commercial, working the bulky dryer with a comb attachment through soaking-wet hair to make "flippy curls"; the unit carried a built-in "styling mist." The hardware is pinned by a museum record: the Lady Schick Model 327, c.1972, designed by Mel Boldt and Associates, red and white plastic and metal, 7 by 8.25 inches.

The dryer also became a prop. In Hal Ashby's *Shampoo* (1975), Warren Beatty's Beverly Hills hairdresser rides around Los Angeles with a blow dryer tucked into his belt "like a sidearm"; the character was based on the stylist Gene Shacove. The haircut it served was the shag, credited to Paul McGregor around 1969 and worn by Jane Fonda in Klute (1971).

## 2001 — the ionic dryer

"The 1990s ionic dryer" is a category back-dated by its own advertising. No documented ionic hair dryer launched in that decade; "minus ion" was a late-1990s Japanese marketing phrase, a coinage, not a scientific term, and the hardware followed the word. Panasonic, then trading as Matsushita and National, released the first dryer with a negative-ion generator in 2001; the Nanocare series came in 2005.

The regulator's verdict is the strongest document here. Japan's National Consumer Affairs Center published its report on 5 September 2003: consultations had risen every year since fiscal 1997, reaching 1,425 over six years, with 714 in fiscal 2002 alone. In its survey of 190 products, only 43.2% of users reported any effect; 56.8% reported little, none, or did not know.

A 2003 revision of the Premiums and Representations Act required a reasonable basis for labelling claims. The science is narrower than the packaging: negative ions do reduce static electricity and help airborne particles settle, but there is no good evidence they moisturise hair, and a 2018 review found the evidence inconsistent.

## 2016 — the Dyson Supersonic

Dyson announced the Supersonic on 27 April 2016 at $399 — reported as $399.99 — with a $450 limited edition that sold out online within hours; US sales began in September, exclusively at Sephora. The pitch was heat control: a glass-bead thermistor measures air temperature twenty times a second, and a microprocessor holds output below 150 °C, against 200–230 °C for typical consumer dryers.

The motor is the V9, spinning up to 110,000 rpm with a 13-blade impeller instead of the usual 11 and mounted in the handle for balance. The research bill is reported three ways: $71 million by NBC News and Slate, "nearly $100 million" by Fortune in 2019, and "almost £50 million" by GadgetGuy. What stays constant is four years, 103 engineers, 600 prototypes, more than 100 patents and over 1,010 miles of human hair.

The claim and the evidence answer different questions. Dyson sells a materials threshold of about 150 °C, the point at which keratin denatures. Manuel Gamez-Garcia, in the Journal of Cosmetic Science in 1998, wet hair for ten seconds and blow-dried it for ten at 30–120 °C, and found longitudinal cuticle cracks; cracking peaked when the surface was held at 75–95 °C.

In that panel, about 40% of 100 individuals averaged 184 ± 15.5 cracks per mm, against about 7 ± 1.3 for the rest, and the high-crack group correlated with daily blow-drying. The mechanism is repeated wet–dry cycling and rapid evaporation, not steady exposure to 150 °C. A cooler dryer can still crack hair if it dries the surface fast and often.

Japan supplied the market evidence. Within a year Dyson held about 10% of the premium hair-dryer market at ¥40,000–50,000, roughly double the existing premium tier, while Panasonic held about 70% of the total; GfK's average Japanese unit price rose from ¥4,300 in 2014 to ¥6,200 in 2016. Dyson's 2018 revenue rose 28% to £4.4 billion.

Dyson's clinical framing came from a consultant. Professor Rod Sinclair, a Melbourne dermatologist, is quoted saying excessive heat "can irreversibly damage the hydrogen bonds within the inner cortex" — an expert comment in a Dyson-organised context, not an independent comparative trial. Fred Howe, Dyson's own engineer, supplied the better line: "This is the 'worst' dryer Dyson will ever make."

The folklore is tidier than the documents. Godefroy's 1888 patent has no crank; Kazanjian's 1911 patent precedes the 1920 market by nine years; the 1990s produced no ionic dryer; 150 °C measures denaturation, not damage. Each simplification survives because it is easier to repeat than the record.

The through-line is less glamorous. A 100-watt motor in a partly wired country, a handle that had to carry the motor after 1954, a ground-fault rule in 1991 that changed what a bathroom circuit could do, a 2,000-watt appliance in a fully electrified house — the motor improved, and the plug set the terms.
