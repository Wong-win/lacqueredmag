The statutes of the Paris gantiers-parfumeurs, confirmed in 1656, set out a ladder: four years' apprenticeship, three years as a journeyman, then a masterpiece — a *chef-d'œuvre* — before trading as a master. Masters' sons were exempt. Everyone else served seven years. Perfumery is usually described as becoming creative only after 1945. The record runs the other way.

## Grasse, seventeenth century — the first ateliers

Grasse's charter is earlier and narrower. In January 1614 its glove makers and perfumers were granted royal letters patent letting them "se nommer et se qualifier autant maître gantier que parfumeur" — two titles created in one stroke. That is a charter of corporate status, not a training record: it says who may call himself what, not how a perfumer was made.

The trade it protected was leather. Grasse had been a tanning centre since the Middle Ages, its workshops along the Foulon and the Siagne, and sixteenth-century glove makers adopted Florentine methods of scenting hide with herbal infusions and floral macerations. The fortune-maker was cruder: jasmine-scented grease rubbed into the leather, sold on as gloves, waistcoats, shoes and fans.

Under Louis XIV these stayed "modest workshops", though a specialist history calls them "a rich and powerful element of the Provençal economy". Supply widened in 1664, when Colbert's Compagnie des Indes brought musk, patchouli, vetiver and sandalwood to France directly rather than through Italy or Spain.

Cultivation followed the trade: jasmine, imported from India, appears around Grasse by about 1650, with some fifteen hectares planted near the town by the late seventeenth century; the common rose arrives at the same date; tuberose, from Italy, establishes itself around 1670.

What the century does not yield matters more. No apprenticeship contract, master-perfumer register or curriculum from seventeenth-century Grasse has been found; the patent regulated titles, not teaching. A 1697 engraving by Gerrit Valck shows a perfumer wearing a censer as a hat, surrounded by his products — the closest the record comes to a job description.

## The eighteenth century — the rise of the nez

The century's revolution was a lightening, not a creative surge. Eau de Cologne displaced the heavy animalics of the court. Giovanni Maria Farina, an Italian in Cologne, made *Aqua Mirabilis* around 1708 — bergamot, lemon, neroli, lavender and rosemary in dilute alcohol — sold as a tonic to drink and to splash on. The French court took it up after his death; the name changed.

Enfleurage, the cold-fat absorption that became Grasse's signature, dates from the 1750s: an eighteenth-century method, not a nineteenth-century one. The chemistry the century is often credited with belongs to the next — coumarin in 1868, vanillin in 1874, artificial musk in 1888, the ionones in 1893. The eighteenth century had the nose; the molecule arrived with the industrial house.

The firms that still trade were founded here. Galimard dates to 1747 and calls itself the oldest French perfumery; Chiris, later part of Givaudan, to 1768. A separate chronology puts France's first aroma-production company in Grasse at about 1730, and the sources disagree. Houbigant, founded in 1775, was perfumer to Marie Antoinette.

After 1789 that client base vanished. Several prominent perfumers were guillotined alongside noble customers; survivors repivoted to patriotic scents with names like "Parfum de la Liberté" and "Eau de la République". Houbigant re-positioned as supplier to the new political class, and in Paris the laboratory, not the bench, became the organising unit of a perfumer's career.

The vocabulary settled too, though nobody has dated the coinage. *Nez* is attested in period usage for a perfumer; the olfactive families by which materials are sorted came later. What is documented is the training's shape: the Paris corporation covered leatherwork and perfuming in one charter, so its apprentice learned two crafts at once. No curriculum, matriculation list or duration survives.

## The nineteenth century — the industrial perfume house

The legal break came in March 1791, when the Paris community of gantiers-parfumeurs was dissolved, and perfumers could set up on their own account. One thesis blames the d'Allarde law of March and the Le Chapelier law of June; the sources disagree. The consequence is not in dispute: "un développement massif des nouvelles maisons de parfumeurs au XIXe siècle".

What the guild required did not disappear with it. The 1656 statutes' four years, three as a journeyman and a masterpiece formed a legally binding ladder, and the nineteenth-century house inherited the shape while taking ownership of it. A guild monopoly ended in 1791; a house monopoly began.

Industrialisation changed the perfumer's standing as much as his equipment. The artisan became an "investisseur, voire notable"; the family establishment became a factory, and the workshop's specialised workers were joined by marketing experts. At Grasse the technical break came in 1864, when Léon Chiris took out the first patents for extracting plant material with volatile solvents, progressively displacing enfleurage.

Artisanal production persisted regardless. "Peaux de senteurs" gave way to "eaux de senteurs" only gradually, and Grasse still had roughly sixty firms, a number "constant depuis le xixe siècle". The houses that define the period are dated precisely — Houbigant 1775, Lubin 1798, Guerlain 1828, Penhaligon's 1870, Coty 1904, Caron 1904.

The apprentice became an employee, trained in a laboratory. Henri Robert, born in Grasse in 1899, learned "les rudiments du métier" in the family, then entered Établissements Chiris as an *apprenti parfumeur chimiste*, learning "la création, la fabrication et la production des parfums" — three operations the house had separated.

## The twentieth century — the perfumer as celebrity

The twentieth century is supposed to be when the perfumer became a public figure. It is not. In 1911 Paul Poiret, the first couturier-perfumer, had his perfumes composed by Maurice Schaller and Henri Alméras without crediting them, and industrial secrecy became the norm by the late twentieth century.

1921 is a perfume date, not a school date. Chanel No 5, composed by Ernest Beaux, launched on 5 May 1921 at the rue Cambon boutique and was "le parfum le plus vendu au monde" by 1929. AnOther calls it the first abstract fragrance and the first to use aldehydes — a magazine's superlative, not a settled fact.

The "Chiris school of 1921" does not survive checking. What 1921 connects is Beaux, the Rallet laboratory associated with Chiris at Cannes-La Bocca, and the young Henri Robert, who met Beaux there. The first documented formal school is Roure's, founded in Grasse in 1946 with Jean Carles as first director.

Carles codified the teaching as well as running the school. The Jean Carles Method arranges sixty odorants in a table, with contrasts between classes down the columns and comparisons within a class across the rows. His students included Monique Schlienger and Jacques Polge, and his articles "A Method of Creation in Perfumery" appeared in 1961 and 1962.

IFF opened its own school in 1968, continuing Ernest Shiftan's teaching, with a senior vice-president as mentor throughout. The trade's institutions date from the same years: the Fragrance Foundation in 1949, the FiFi Awards from 1973, an indie category in the 2010s. Celebrity fragrances of the 1980s and 1990s put the licensor on the bottle and left the perfumer anonymous.

## The twenty-first century — the indie apprenticeship

The credited perfumer, as a marketing device, is a 2000 invention. Frédéric Malle's Éditions de Parfums launched in Paris with "nine perfumes signed by their authors" — names on the box, names on the bottle, biographies published. Until then the default had been Poiret's: the house signs, the nose does not.

The niche house is the independent perfumer's vehicle, and its foundations are dated: L'Artisan Parfumeur in 1976, Annick Goutal in 1981, Serge Lutens in 1992, Frédéric Malle in 2000, Le Labo and Byredo in 2006, Maison Francis Kurkdjian in 2009. A house can be founded in a year; a nose cannot.

The self-taught route has documented cases. Andy Tauer founded Tauer Perfumes in Zurich in 2005 and released L'Air du Désert Marocain the same year, after several years of self-directed study. Liz Moores took the Fragrance Foundation's CFSS distance course in 2009 and a five-day natural-materials course with Alec Lawless in 2010 before co-founding Papillon Artisan Perfumes in 2011. The documented span is five to ten years from first formulation to first commercial release.

The institutional houses still produce most working perfumers. Givaudan, dsm-firmenich, IFF and Symrise run internal schools; Robertet, Mane and Takasago use selective mentorship, and Givaudan admits fewer than one in three thousand applicants. ISIPCA, founded by Jean-Jacques Guerlain in Versailles in 1970, runs a master's of two to three years and takes forty to sixty students a year from several hundred, with an eliminatory nose test regardless of academic record.

The Grasse Institute of Perfumery, created in 2002, offers an International Technical Degree in Fragrance Creation and Sensory Evaluation: eighteen months, twelve in centre plus a six-month internship, 1,200 hours, twelve students, taught in English, at €13,500 plus a €150 assessment fee, validated by the trade body Prodarom. Its two-week summer school cost €2,400 in 2026. The two figures, side by side, are the argument.

## The modern apprenticeship, briefly

The best-documented in-house apprenticeship is Robertet's: two years, taught by *tutorat*, master to pupil, averaging four students a year. Year one covers natural raw materials and simple accords; year two, evaluation and the student-teacher confrontation. Graduates may accede to the title of *parfumeur junior*. Two years is the shortest unit in the trade, and not the whole of anyone's training.

Givaudan runs four years, with two to four students per intake worldwide and intakes that are not annual. Year one is several hundred raw materials memorised under daily blind tests; year two, binary and ternary accords; year three, full-pyramid formulas with IFRA compliance; year four, live commercial briefs. The company puts the interval from admission to first signed release at six to eight years.

The Grasse intangible-heritage inventory gives a transformation technician "une dizaine d'années" and records transmission "de père en fils" alongside the schools. End to end, the institutional path is ten to fifteen years: school for two to four, evaluator placement for one to two, house school for four to seven, then creator status, with junior promotion taking another three to five. The figure usually quoted is six years. No programme of that length appears in the record.

The historical benchmark is seven, not six: four years plus three plus a masterpiece under the 1656 statutes, which makes the modern path longer than the guild's. The training buys roughly 1,500 raw materials memorised against a library of about 5,000, plus accords, full-pyramid construction and IFRA compliance. Gatekeeping is scarcity rather than curriculum: two to four at Givaudan, about four at Robertet, twelve at the Grasse degree. The gate is a chair count, not a fee.
