Perfumery is, in our view, the last beauty craft with a working apprenticeship. The apprenticeship is, in our view, also the longest training in the modern cosmetic industry. The apprentice, in a French house of the kind that has survived since the eighteenth century, spends three to five years at the bench before she is permitted to touch a formula on her own. The apprentice, in an indie house of the kind that has emerged in the last twenty, spends an even longer apprenticeship — without, in most cases, the formal title. The apprenticeship has, in our view, also become the part of perfumery that the contemporary market most wants to forget.

The forgetting is, in our view, a problem. The perfume that arrives on the counter is, in most cases, the work of an apprentice who is now a *maitre parfumeur*, and the route between the two is, in our view, the route worth describing. The route is, in our view, also the route that explains why some houses continue to make perfume that wears as though it has been *made*, and others make perfume that wears as though it has been *manufactured*. The difference is, in our view, mostly the apprenticeship.

## Grasse, seventeenth century — the first ateliers

The apprenticeship, in its modern form, begins in the seventeenth-century town of Grasse. Grasse is, by 1650, the tanning capital of Provence; the town is, in the same period, also beginning to take seriously the *cultivation* of aromatic plants — lavender, jasmine, rose, tuberose — and the *distillation* of them into what would, by the eighteenth century, be called *eaux*. The apprenticeship, in Grasse, is, in this period, a guild apprenticeship. The apprentice is bound to a master for a term of years — usually five to seven. The apprentice is taught, in those years, the entire chain of the trade: the planting, the harvest, the enfleurage, the distillation, the maceration, the compounding.

The apprentice, in Grasse, also learns the *language* of the trade — the names for the raw materials, the names for the parts of a formula, the names for the proportions. The language is, in this period, partly French, partly Occitan, partly Latin, and is, in our view, the first thing an apprentice loses when she leaves Grasse. The language survives, in fragments, in the modern perfume house. The language is, in our view, also the first thing a perfumer is taught when she arrives at a new house.

The compounding, in the seventeenth century, is done by hand. The apprentice learns to weigh, by hand, on a balance that is, in many houses, the same balance the master has used for thirty years. The apprentice learns to count drops, by eye, in a way that is, in our view, no longer taught — the modern apprentice learns to count drops with a pipette, and the pipette, in our view, is both a gain and a loss. The gain is precision. The loss is the *hand*, which is, in our view, the thing the apprenticeship is most trying to develop.

## The eighteenth century — the rise of the *nez*

The eighteenth century is, in our view, the moment the perfumer acquires a *title*. The title — *nez*, in French; *the nose*, in English — is, in this period, a guild title. The *nez* is, in the eighteenth century, a person who has been examined by her peers and found competent. The *nez* is, in this period, also a person who has, in most cases, completed an apprenticeship of at least seven years. The *nez* is, in this period, finally, a person who is allowed to sign a formula.

The title is, in our view, also the moment the perfume trade becomes a *profession*. Before the title, perfume was, in most cases, a *sideline* — a tanner's hobby, a glove-maker's curiosity. After the title, perfume was, in most cases, a *vocation*. The perfumer was paid to be a perfumer. The perfumer was examined as a perfumer.

The apprenticeship, in the eighteenth century, expanded to include *sensory training* — the apprentice was expected to recognise, from a single sniff, two hundred raw materials, to describe what she had sniffed, and, by the end of her term, to have a vocabulary that, in our view, is still being taught in modified form in the modern perfume house.

## The nineteenth century — the industrial perfume house

The nineteenth century is, in our view, the moment the perfume trade becomes an *industry*, organised around a small number of large houses — Guerlain, Houbigant, Roger & Gallet. The apprenticeship, in this period, becomes, in most cases, an *internal* apprenticeship, a person who has been hired by the house and taught by the house's *nez*. The apprentice is, in this period, finally, a person whose training is the property of the house.

The internal apprenticeship is, in our view, both more efficient and more conservative than the guild apprenticeship. The internal apprentice is taught the *house style*, the *house materials* — the particular rose, the particular jasmine, the particular moss — and the *house method*, the way of compounding that distinguishes the house from its peers.

The internal apprenticeship is, in our view, also the model that survives, in modified form, in the modern French luxury house. The apprentice at a modern house is, in most cases, hired from chemistry school, taught by the *nez en titre*, evaluated at the end in a ceremony that resembles, in our view, the eighteenth-century guild examination more than the modern job interview.

## The twentieth century — the perfumer as celebrity

The twentieth century is, in our view, the moment the perfumer becomes a *public figure* — Guerlain's Jacques, Chanel's Jacques (a different Jacques), the postwar independents, the celebrity noses of the 1980s. The perfumer's name, in this period, appears on the bottle. The name on the bottle is, in our view, both an elevation and a flattening. The elevation is that the perfumer is, finally, recognised as the *author*. The flattening is that the author is, in the modern market, expected to be a *brand*.

The apprenticeship, in the twentieth century, becomes, in most cases, a *private* apprenticeship. The apprentice is not mentioned by name. The apprentice is evaluated not by the guild but by the master. The evaluation is the bottleneck through which the apprentice must pass to become a *nez en titre*. The bottleneck is, in our view, also the bottleneck the contemporary French luxury has, in the last twenty years, been most reluctant to maintain.

## The twenty-first century — the indie apprenticeship

The twenty-first century is, in our view, the moment the apprenticeship is taken up, in a different form, by the indie houses. The indie house — of the kind that has emerged, since 2010, in Brooklyn, in Berlin, in Grasse itself — has, in most cases, no formal master, no guild, no *nez en titre* in the eighteenth-century sense.

The indie apprenticeship is, in our view, an *open* apprenticeship. The apprentice is a person hired because she has demonstrated, somewhere, a *nose*. The nose is, in this period, a more important credential than the chemistry degree. The nose is, finally, the thing the indie master is trying, in three to five years, to *develop*.

The indie apprenticeship is, in our view, also a *public* apprenticeship. The apprentice is, in many cases, named — on the bottle, in interviews, in the press. The naming is, in our view, both an act of generosity and an act of marketing. The marketing is that the apprentice is sold to the consumer as a kind of *novelty* — and the story of the apprentice is, in many cases, the story the indie house tells to differentiate itself from the heritage houses.

The indie apprenticeship is, in our view, also a *shorter* apprenticeship than the guild apprenticeship. The indie house cannot, in our view, afford a seven-year apprenticeship. The indie house can, in most cases, afford a three-year apprenticeship. The three years are, in our view, the minimum at which a person can be trusted with a formula of any complexity. The three years are, in our view, also the maximum at which the indie house can remain solvent.

## The modern apprenticeship, briefly

The modern apprenticeship is, in our view, a hybrid. The modern apprentice is, in most cases, trained partly in the house, partly in a school, partly on her own. The modern apprentice is, in most cases, evaluated partly by the master, partly by the market, partly by her own nose. The modern apprenticeship is, in our view, the most fragile of the three — and, in our view, the most important to defend.

The fragility is, in our view, mostly economic. The apprentice costs money. The apprentice is, in many cases, not, in the first three years, contributing to the bottom line. The apprentice is, in many cases, finally, a person the house has decided to invest in — and the investment is, in the modern market, the kind of investment the modern market is, in many cases, unwilling to make.

The importance is, in our view, editorial. The perfume that wears as though it has been *made* is, in our view, almost always the perfume that has been, at some point, in the hands of an apprentice. The apprentice is, in our view, the person who has done the slow work that makes a perfume *wear*. The apprentice is, in our view, also the person who, in the modern market, is, in many cases, the first to be cut. The cutting is, in our view, the loss the modern market has, in the last twenty years, been most unwilling to recognise.

---

The apprenticeship, in sum, is the story of perfumery. The story is, in our view, the story of a craft that has, in five centuries, refused to industrialise its training. The refusal is, in our view, also the reason the craft has, in five centuries, refused to industrialise its product. The refusal is, in our view, finally, the reason the perfume that arrives on the counter — when it arrives from a house that still trains — still arrives as a thing that wears as though a person made it.

The apprenticeship is, in our view, also the part of perfumery the modern market most needs to defend. The defence is, in our view, also the work of the consumer — the consumer who, on the counter, asks the brand about the brand, and who, in asking, gives the brand a reason to keep the apprentice. The asking is, in our view, finally, the work. The work is what the apprenticeship is for.
