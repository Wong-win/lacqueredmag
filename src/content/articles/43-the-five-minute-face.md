Start with the teaspoon. The American Academy of Dermatology asks for about one ounce of sunscreen for the body — roughly a shot glass — and at least one teaspoon for the face, applied fifteen minutes before you go outside and topped up every two hours. That is a measurement, not a mood, and the only number here you cannot argue with. Five minutes is ample time to dispense a teaspoon, and ample time to do four other things badly, which is how most five-minute routines end.

The assumption to dismantle is that a five-minute face is a compromised one — the version you make when you are tired. It is not a consolation prize. It is a decision, and like any decision it can be made badly: the same five minutes produce a face that reads as deliberate or as careless, and the difference is not the number of products but which two steps get the seconds. Nobody has published how long any of this takes, so the seconds here are arithmetic, not data: on our reckoning the brow takes about forty seconds and the lip about thirty.

## Step one — the tinted moisturiser

What a tinted moisturiser is, in numbers. Industry formulation guides put the pigment load at roughly 5–15% — one comparison guide says 10–25% — against 25–45% for a BB cream and 20–40% for a liquid foundation, so a tinted moisturiser delivers something like 15–20% of the coverage of a full-coverage foundation.

It does one job, evening the tone, at the price of a step. Hence the label matters more than the formula: most contain no SPF at all, and only a printed, tested number counts.

The label number, in turn, is not the number you get. The FDA's testing protocol applies the product at 2 mg/cm², a laboratory condition rather than anybody's bathroom.

Schalka and colleagues, in 2009, tested SPF 15 and SPF 30 products at 2, 1.5, 1 and 0.5 mg/cm² in forty volunteers and found protection fell significantly as the application thinned (P<0.001); Ouyang's 2018 paper in the *Journal of Drugs in Dermatology* found that at half the test dose a labelled SPF 15 delivered an in-vivo SPF of 7, a labelled SPF 70 delivered 26. Real-world application runs at 0.5–1.5 mg/cm², per the Skin Cancer Prevention Working Group consensus in *Skin* (2021).

Hence the teaspoon, and a floor of SPF 30, broad-spectrum and water-resistant. Reapplication is the weak point of every tinted SPF on the market: at 30 and above it covers incidental exposure and fails on a long day outdoors, and the fix is a powder or stick SPF.

A tinted SPF does not replace a separate sunscreen, and none is built to be left on for eight hours without a top-up. It is a floor, not a ceiling, and a routine that under-applies the sunscreen has not saved five minutes. It has saved five minutes of the wrong thing.

And the shade range, which is the real five-minute problem. Tinted sunscreens ship the narrowest range of any complexion product — often one to three *universal* tints that read grey or orange on deeper skin — while liquid and powder foundation offers the widest range of depths and undertones.

Newer launches have pushed the top end to 15–20 shades: MERIT's The Uniform, SPF 45, 100% mineral, zinc oxide 15.065%, $38 for 50 ml, launched in fifteen shades in May 2025 and now listed at twenty. A five-minute face has no time for a second match: a twenty-shade range is not an inclusion gesture but what makes a fast face possible.

## Step two — the cream blush

A cream blush is pigment suspended in an oil-and-water emulsion or a solid balm, and it carries far more pigment than a powder, since no talc or silica dilutes the colour, which is why it reads as a flush rather than a stripe.

It is also the step that goes first. No controlled measurement of cream blush wear time has been published, and reviewers report wear qualitatively, but the cheek is the first thing to leave the face — which makes it the step to cut if the minute count will not move.

The failure is a shade failure, and the flashback is a chemistry fact, not a scare: the iron oxides are the same molecules that flash orange over a white base — invisible on fair skin, lifting on deeper skin wherever the undertone is ashy. Flashback and the wrong undertone are one problem in a cream, which magnifies all three shade variables — depth, undertone, saturation.

Fingers are the method, not a workaround: fingertip warmth softens the cream so the tint builds in thin layers, and tapping rather than dragging keeps it off the under-eye. MERIT's Flush Balm is $30 in thirteen shades; Glossier's Cloud Paint is $22 to $24 for the 7.5 ml tube.

## Step three — the brow gel

There are three brow-gel technologies, plus a fourth that does one job. Soap — the original Pomade trick — sets the hairs with a film that washes out with water and usually needs a second application within a day. Wax lays down a hard-holding film that can go brittle.

Fibre is a coloured gel that adds apparent volume and blends the brow. A clear gel holds without adding colour, and it is the one most fast faces want. Glossier's Boy Brow, $22 for 0.13 oz, sits at the flexible end: it defines, thickens and shapes face-framing brows in seconds, gives a soft, flexible hold, and neither stiffens nor flakes.

The failure at the other end is the frozen face, and everybody recognises it. A hold strong enough to set the hairs rigidly and matte removes the movement that reads as a real brow, so the brow reads as a painted shape; the film flakes once it breaks, and flaking makes a gel look dirty.

Hold time is not printed on the tube and no published figure for it exists; the six- and twelve-hour numbers in the category are marketing claims. The film-forming polymer decides, and then your brow hair: coarse and straight behaves nothing like fine and wavy. The brand matters less than the hair, which is why the brow is one of the two steps that repay the seconds.

## Step four — the mascara

The wand is the mechanism, not the marketing. Maybelline's Lash Sensational, $9.99 to $11.99 for the washable 0.32 oz, uses a curved Flex Tower fanning brush with ten layers of bristles to separate and fan the lashes. L'Oréal's Voluminous Lash Paradise, $11.99 at MSRP for 0.28 oz, carries a densely packed, wavy hourglass boar-bristle wand with 200-plus bristles to load volume. Too Faced's Better Than Sex, which both are dupe-tiered against, runs $27 to $29.

The replacement rule is eye-care guidance, not beauty-industry invention: the American Academy of Ophthalmology advises replacing mascara every three months, Poison Control puts all other eye makeup at six months and says to discard everything after an eye infection, and one Tear Film & Ocular Surface Society expert advises replacing mascara every month and liquid eyeliner every three, with blepharitis patients told four to six weeks.

Waterproof cuts both ways. Luensmann and colleagues, in *Cosmetics* in 2025, tested nine cosmetic types against seven monthly-replacement contact lens types and found non-waterproof mascara produced the highest lens deposition, waterproof deposits were least removable by the standard Clear Care hydrogen-peroxide cycle, and waterproof caused the greatest deterioration in lens optical quality.

Some 65% of American women use mascara regularly, and preservatives such as benzalkonium chloride and formaldehyde releasers are cytotoxic to corneal and conjunctival epithelium. One caveat: no study has measured mascara flaking or smudging as an endpoint; the literature frames eye-makeup failure as lens deposition, tear-film migration and ocular-surface effects. It will not tell you which wand flakes, only what it leaves behind.

## Step five — the tinted lip balm

A tinted lip balm sits between a balm and a lipstick, carrying a translucent tint rather than a full pigment load, and because the pigment is iron-oxide based the tints run warm rather than true.

It is also the only step the day finishes for you — the only product that gets wiped off, by coffee, by a sandwich, by the dentist's mask — and any transfer moves the SPF out of position with the colour.

The reference product is MDSolarSciences' Hydrating Sheer Tinted Lip Balm SPF 30 at $26 for a 0.15 oz tube: avobenzone, octisalate and homosalate, shea butter, jojoba and avocado oils, vitamins C and E.

The field around it is cheap and mostly fine: Cay Skin's Isle Lip Balm SPF 30 at $16, Well People's Lip Butter Tinted SPF 15 at $15.99. And it is the one place dermatology asks for a number at all.

The AAD recommends SPF 30 or higher for lip products, partly as a margin: an SPF rating assumes a thick, even application, and people apply a thin swipe of balm, so real protection is lower than the tube's figure. How much tinted lip balm it takes to reach its labelled SPF has never been established — an inference from the general sunscreen literature, not a measurement.

What is documented is thinner skin, minimal melanin, disproportionate UV exposure to the lower lip, and a recognised progression from actinic cheilitis to squamous cell carcinoma. The best study is nearly thirty years old and observational: Pogoda and Preston-Martin published a population-based case-control study in *Cancer Causes & Control* in 1996, 74 women with lip cancer and 105 controls.

Risk rose steeply with lifetime sun exposure, an odds ratio of 13.5 in the highest quartile, and among the heavily exposed, those using lip protection once a day or less had roughly twice the risk of those using it more often. There are no randomised trials of SPF lip balm and lip cancer.

So the budget. The sunscreen is not where to economise: the teaspoon is the floor under everything else, and a tinted SPF doing two jobs is the cheapest way to pay. You economise on coverage by substitution: one tinted SPF instead of a foundation and a separate sunscreen.

The brow and the lip are what the seconds are for — cheap in time, legible in failure, and in the lip's case the only step the day edits for you. A five-minute face is not a reduced face but a face with a budget and an editor, and nobody will think about the length of your routine if the brow is right and the lip in place.
