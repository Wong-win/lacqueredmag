A jar candle is a burner in a glass jar, and the instructions that ship with it read like an equipment manual. Trim the wick to a quarter inch before every burn. Keep at least a foot between the flame and anything that can burn. Cap the session at four hours. Put it out when half an inch of wax is left. Never leave the room with one lit, and never take it to bed — the National Fire Protection Association advises against candles in bedrooms and sleeping areas. Those are the product's real instructions; the copy on the band is the marketing.

Meanwhile the National Candle Association, whose member companies make about 80 per cent of the candles made in the United States, counts more than ten thousand scents on the market, says three-quarters of buyers consider fragrance extremely or very important when choosing one, and puts roughly 35 per cent of sales in the holiday season.

The trade has organised itself around the scent. The object is a combustion device — and the distance between a candle sold as a mood and a candle that must be trimmed, monitored and extinguished on schedule is where the money and the risk sit.

What separates a jar that throws from a jar that smells pleasant in the shop is three things: a percentage of oil, a length of wick, and a type of wax.

## The wax

Paraffin is a by-product of petroleum refining and the most widely used candle wax in the world: odourless, inexpensive, easy to shape, melting between 46 and 68 degrees Celsius. Its usefulness is exactly its blankness — the paraffin smells of nothing, so everything detected in a paraffin jar was added after the wax was made.

Soy melts lower, at 46 to 54, burns slowly and evenly, and carries a fragrance-load ceiling of ten per cent. Beeswax, at 62 to 65, is animal-origin, naturally golden, scarce and expensive. Rapeseed melts at 57 to 61 and produces low soot.

The wax is also the oldest part of the story. In 1834 a Manchester pewterer, Joseph Morgan, patented a machine for continuous moulded candle production, making about 1,500 candles an hour; James Young developed a commercially viable way of distilling paraffin from coal and oil shales at Bathgate, Scotland, in the mid-1850s, though other accounts date the introduction of manufactured paraffin to 1850. Price's Candles, founded by William Wilson in 1830, was the world's largest candle maker by the late nineteenth century.

What the wax decides is the envelope: how hot the pool runs, how much oil the formula can hold before it weeps, how fast the surface gives way.

How much it can hold is a number — ten per cent of its own weight in oil for soy, six for beeswax — and how much it actually carries is the maker's choice. Which leaves the shopper asking the wrong question. *Is it soy?* tells you about the carrier and nothing about the one ingredient the candle is for.

## The wick

The wick is the part of the object a federal regulator once reached for. On 18 April 2003 the Consumer Product Safety Commission published a final rule, "Metal-Cored Candlewicks Containing Lead and Candles With Such Wicks," at 68 FR 19142, amending 16 CFR Part 1500 under the Federal Hazardous Substances Act.

It declared a metal-cored wick containing more than 0.06 per cent lead by weight of the core — and any candle containing one — a hazardous substance, banned from manufacture or import on or after 15 October 2003.

The threshold was not arbitrary. CPSC staff concluded that candles emitting 430 micrograms of lead an hour or more could raise blood lead above 10 micrograms per decilitre, and laboratory work found such candles emitting more than 3,000 micrograms an hour. A 1999 Washington, DC survey found about 30 per cent of sampled candles carried metal-cored wicks, and roughly 10 per cent of those — three per cent of everything sampled — had detectable lead.

The industry had promised in a voluntary 1974 agreement to replace lead-cored wicks, and the promise had decayed until manufacturers were producing them again. The sentence that matters most to a shopper is in the rule itself: *the presence of lead in a wick can be determined only by laboratory analysis.*

With the metal cores gone, the wick became a design choice again. Braided cotton is the default: a flat braid self-trims slightly, a square braid gives a larger flame, a round braid the strongest flame for large jars.

Wood wicks in maple or cherry give a wide, even melt pool and excellent scent throw, but crackle and soot more, and are trimmed to an eighth of an inch. Zinc cores add rigidity in soft or large waxes and burn cleanly, though they are not eco-friendly and are being phased out. Hemp is natural and slows the mushrooming that ruins a flame.

Wick length, meanwhile, is the mechanism behind most defects a buyer complains about. Too long and the flame runs larger, hotter and less controlled — it draws more wax, burns the candle faster and soots more. Too short and you risk self-extinguishing or *tunnelling*. Trim to a quarter inch, 6 mm, before every burn.

Burning too long, running a wick that is too large, or loading the wax heavily with fragrance and dye produces *mushrooming*, a carbon ball on the tip; the fix is to let the candle cool, pinch the cap off, trim again and cap the session at four hours.

All of this sits upstream of the scent, because the wick is the biggest single lever on how the candle smells: melt pool area drives throw more than load, and the target is a pool a quarter to half an inch across after two hours, reaching the wall by hour two or three.

## The burn

What happens in the jar is a fire, and it is counted like one. NFPA figures for 2015 to 2019 average about 7,400 home structure fires a year started by candles — two per cent of reported home fires, 90 deaths, 670 injuries and $291 million in direct property damage.

About twenty a day. The percentages depend on the denominator: another dataset puts candles at four per cent of residential fires and four per cent of fire deaths, while the standard reference on candles gives roughly ten per cent of civilian fire injuries and six per cent of fire fatalities. Any candle statistic should name the book it came from.

The safety card is where the object's real specification lives. Trim wicks to one-quarter inch. Use a stable, non-combustible holder large enough to catch drips. Extinguish tapers and pillars when the flame comes within two inches of the holder, and put out votives and container candles before only the last half-inch of wax begins to melt.

On clearance the authorities simply disagree: the NFPA and the National Candle Association say 12 inches, while Marion County Fire Rescue in Florida, Bushkill Township Fire Company in Pennsylvania, Winona in Minnesota and the Town of Normal, Illinois, all say three feet from anything that can burn.

Normal repeated its instruction after a fatal mobile-home fire. Every one of those departments says three feet for space heaters and fireplaces. The disagreement is specific to candles.

The session has rules of its own. The first burn should continue until the melt pool reaches the edge of the vessel — two to three hours for a standard eight-ounce, three-inch jar — and after that, two to four hours maximum, with at least two hours of cooling before relighting. Stop when half an inch of wax is left, two inches for tapers and pillars, and never leave it unattended.

The first burn matters for a physical reason rather than a ritual one: the pool diameter set on that burn becomes the *wax memory* for every later burn. Extinguish early and a hardened ring forms at the perimeter; the wick then burns a narrow tunnel down the centre of the jar, the wall never melts, and the tunnel can take 30 to 40 per cent of the wax with it.

Burn time is measured by weight, and the standard the industry cites, ASTM F2417, is a fire-safety specification that measures weight loss rather than hours of fragrance. For scale, a modern paraffin taper burns about 0.1 g a minute, releases roughly 77 watts, produces around 13 lumens and has a luminous intensity near one candela.

## The scent

Here is the number that explains the object. *Fragrance load* is the weight of the oil divided by the weight of the wax, multiplied by 100 — always by weight, never by volume, because fragrance density runs from 0.9 to 1.05 grams per millilitre and a pour measured in millilitres is measured wrongly.

Recommended and maximum loads run by wax: six to eight per cent for a soy container, with a ceiling of ten; six to nine for paraffin, ceiling ten to twelve; eight to ten for coconut and coconut-soy; three to six for beeswax; six for palm; six to nine for parasoy blends. Past twelve per cent come pooling, weeping and weak throw.

And here is the part that undoes the marketing. More oil is not more candle. At the ceiling you waste the most expensive ingredient in the formula, and loads of six to eight per cent can throw harder than loads of ten to twelve, with a cleaner burn, less soot, fewer wet spots and smoother tops — maxing at ten per cent does not always produce proportionally stronger scent.

The economics point the same way: fragrance oil is typically the highest-priced thing in the tub, so moving from eight per cent to twelve raises the cost of the oil per unit by about half. Eight per cent is the number most container candles should be poured at.

Even the arithmetic goes wrong in small ways — a "200 gram candle" is 200 g of wax plus 16 g of fragrance at eight per cent plus about 2 g of wick, roughly 218 g finished, so a maker who calculates load on the finished weight over-loads every candle by about 1.5 g.

The regulatory spine is the industry's own, and it was rewritten recently. IFRA issued the 49th Amendment on 10 January 2020, adding a quantitative risk assessment method — QRA2 — built on an aggregate exposure model that treats exposure to a single fragrance ingredient across many products at once.

The transition periods ran 13 months for new fragrance creations and 25 for existing ones, and the dates have two versions: the compliance firm Obelis gives 10 February 2021 and 10 February 2022, while IFRA's own communication and CosmeticOBS give 10 May 2021 and 10 May 2022.

Either way, the deadline governs the supply of the fragrance, not the finished candle on the shelf. The amendment took IFRA from eleven categories to twelve.

None of that machinery decides whether a candle smells good, though, because the performance lives in two very different places. *Cold throw* is oil evaporating off the surface of solid wax at room temperature — what a shopper smells when the lid comes off, and the single biggest driver of the initial sale. The opposite number, *hot throw*, is oil released from the melt pool while the candle burns, and it decides whether the customer comes back.

The two do not predict each other; a jar can disappoint unlit and be excellent lit, or the reverse. Cure is the other half of it: soy wants seven to fourteen days, coconut and coconut-soy blends three to seven and sometimes two weeks, paraffin one to three.

A fresh soy pour gives roughly 40 per cent of its eventual throw, 60 per cent at 48 hours and all of it at two weeks, which means a jar poured last week is not yet the candle it will be in a fortnight.

Scent family predicts behaviour. Citrus, mint and eucalyptus give a loud cold throw and evaporate quickly out of a hot pool. Vanilla, patchouli, resins, woods, sandalwood, leather and tobacco are quieter unlit and open up when warm, throwing more overall — though oxidisable oils such as vanillin also drive the tan that soy and coconut develop.

The market is priced on the same logic: the National Candle Association puts retail candles from about $1.99 for a votive to $35 for a large jar or pillar, with premium products at $200 and above. A two-hundred-dollar candle is not two hundred dollars of wax; the difference between the votive and the large jar is mostly the size of the oil bill.

Then there is the most interesting object in the category, which does not do what the others do. SC Johnson's Glade "scented oil" system never burns fragrance in the wax at all: heat passes from the flame through a wick clip to a warming plate that melts a large pool of scented oil, transferred by capillary action, giving markedly higher throw than a conventional candle — and the candle extinguishes itself once the oil is gone, which the patent treats as a safety feature.

The jars are sold as 3.4 oz and 12 oz soy blend. That is what happens when a fragrance business stops treating the object as a mood and starts treating it as a burner with a consumable inside it. The trade will go on selling the mood; the manual will go on describing the burner. The distance between them is measured in one percentage and one quarter inch of wick.
