Beauty photography is, in our view, the studio art that built the modern cosmetic counter. The counter, in 2026, looks the way it looks because beauty photography, in the hundred years before 2026, decided that it should. The studio, in our view, is the room in which that decision was made. The studio is, in our view, also the room in which the decision is, every season, renegotiated.

The studio is, in our view, the most under-photographed part of beauty photography. The studio is, in our view, also the most consequential. The studio is, in our view, finally, the room in which the picture is composed, lit, and shot — and the picture, in beauty photography, is the thing the consumer eventually pays for. The studio is, in our view, the part of the industry that, by remaining mostly unseen, has done the most to determine the part of the industry that is mostly seen.

## Horst P. Horst, 1930s — the Vogue era

The beauty photograph, in the modern sense, begins in the late 1920s and the early 1930s, in the studios of Condé Nast in New York and Paris. The principal photographer, in those years, is Horst P. Horst. The Horst studio is, in this period, a single large room in the Condé Nast building in New York, with a north-facing window, a white wall, a small library of props, and a single light — a large softbox, hand-built, that Horst and his assistant have been refining for years.

The Horst picture is, in this period, almost entirely a *still life*. The Horst picture shows, in most cases, an arrangement of cosmetic objects on a pale surface. The objects are, in this period, simple — a glass bottle, a powder compact, a tube of cream, a cake of rouge, all photographed against a pale, mostly empty background. The Horst picture is, in this period, the picture the cosmetic industry most wanted to project: a picture of *calm*, of *stillness*, of *order*. The cosmetic counter, in this period, looked the way the Horst picture looked.

The Horst studio is, in our view, the model for almost every beauty studio that comes after. The Horst studio is, in our view, also the model the cosmetic industry, in the twentieth century, was most successful in *exceeding*. The exceeding is, in our view, mostly a matter of *props*. The props — the surface, the background, the light — are, in the modern beauty studio, the things the studio is most invested in. The props are, in our view, also the things the modern consumer most identifies as *beauty photography*.

## Avedon, 1940s and 1950s — the contract

The second great moment in beauty photography is the 1940s and 1950s, and the principal photographer is Richard Avedon. The Avedon studio is, in this period, a different kind of room from Horst's. The Avedon studio is, in this period, a *white* room. A white floor, a white ceiling, a white background, lit by a small army of strobes, with the subject standing in the middle of the room on a small platform.

The Avedon picture is, in this period, almost entirely a *portrait*. The portrait shows, in most cases, a face — a single face, lit from the side, lit from below, lit from above, lit in the way that would, in the twentieth century, become the *standard* for the cosmetic advertisement. The face is, in this period, the subject. The cosmetic is, in this period, almost invisible. The lipstick is, in this period, a *trace* on the lip. The powder is, in this period, a *trace* on the cheek. The cosmetic is, in this period, finally, a *trace* of the photographer's eye.

The Avedon contract is, in this period, the contract the cosmetic industry most wanted. The contract is, in this period, also the contract the photographer most wanted to negotiate. The contract is, in this period, finally, a contract that pays the photographer by the *day* — a day rate that, in the postwar period, is high enough that Avedon can afford to spend a single day on a single picture, in which the picture will, in many cases, appear on the cover of a magazine. The day rate is, in our view, the most under-discussed part of the beauty photograph. The day rate is, in our view, also the part of the industry the modern market has been most unwilling to preserve.

## Lindbergh, 1970s and 1980s — the Dior era

The third great moment is the 1970s and 1980s, and the principal photographer is Peter Lindbergh. The Lindbergh studio is, in this period, again a different kind of room. The Lindbergh studio is, in this period, a *warehouse* — a large, unheated, north-lit room, with a single camera, a single light, a small library of props, and a *truck* of clothes and cosmetics parked just outside.

The Lindbergh picture is, in this period, almost entirely a *group*. The group shows, in most cases, three or four women, in black-and-white, lit from the side, photographed against the wall of the warehouse. The Lindbergh picture is, in this period, the picture the cosmetic industry most wanted to project in the late twentieth century: a picture of *kinship*, of *sisterhood*, of *the face as a collective thing*. The Lindbergh picture is, in this period, also the picture that would, in the 1990s, become the canonical way of selling a cosmetic.

The Lindbergh studio is, in our view, the model for the *editorial* beauty studio. The Lindbergh studio is, in our view, also the model the *advertising* beauty studio has, in the last twenty years, been trying to imitate. The imitation is, in our view, mostly a matter of *subjects*. The Lindbergh subjects are, in this period, almost always *real* women — women who have, in most cases, not been extensively photographed before. The advertising subjects are, in this period, almost always *known* women — women who have, in most cases, been photographed for years. The difference is, in our view, mostly editorial.

## Mert Alas and Marcus Piggott, 1990s and 2000s — the digital

The fourth great moment is the 1990s and 2000s, and the principal photographers are Mert Alas and Marcus Piggott. The Mert and Marcus studio is, in this period, again a different kind of room. The Mert and Marcus studio is, in this period, a *digital* studio — a room with three or four computers, a large format printer, a bank of hard drives, and, behind the computers, a small white cyclorama.

The Mert and Marcus picture is, in this period, almost entirely a *digital picture*. The digital picture shows, in most cases, a face that has been *retouched* — in some cases, lightly retouched, in some cases, extensively retouched. The retouching is, in this period, the technology that, for the first time in the history of beauty photography, allows the cosmetic industry to *correct* the face in the picture. The retouching is, in our view, also the technology that, for the first time in the history of beauty photography, allows the cosmetic industry to *manufacture* the face in the picture.

The digital studio is, in our view, the model for almost every beauty studio of the last twenty years. The digital studio is, in our view, also the model the cosmetic industry has, in the last twenty years, been most willing to invest in. The investment is, in our view, mostly a matter of *computers*. The computers are, in the modern beauty studio, the tools that, in most cases, decide what the picture looks like. The computers are, in our view, also the tools the modern consumer most identifies as *digital*.

## The studio, in 2026

The studio, in 2026, is, in our view, at a turning point. The turning point is, in our view, mostly a matter of *technology*. The technology is, in 2026, mostly a matter of *AI* — the AI that can, in 2026, generate a picture of a face that does not exist, in any real woman, and present the face as if it had been photographed. The AI is, in 2026, the technology the cosmetic industry has, in our view, been most willing to embrace.

The embrace is, in our view, mostly a matter of *cost*. The AI picture is, in 2026, cheaper than the photographed picture. The AI picture is, in our view, also *flatter* than the photographed picture — the AI picture has, in our view, the surface of a photograph without the depth of one. The flatness is, in our view, also the loss the modern market has, in 2026, been most unwilling to name.

The naming is, in our view, finally, the work of the studio. The studio is, in 2026, the room in which the honest calling — the calling that says *this picture was photographed, this picture was retouched, this picture was generated* — has, in our view, the most leverage. The leverage is, in our view, also the responsibility.

The beauty studio, in sum, is the room in which the cosmetic counter has, for a hundred years, been made. The room is, in our view, also the room in which the cosmetic counter, in the next hundred years, will be made. The room is, in our view, finally, the room in which the question of what a cosmetic counter should look like is, in our view, every season — most slowly and most carefully — answered.

The answer is, in our view, the answer the consumer is, in the end, paying for. The consumer's question — *was this picture photographed? was this face real?* — is, in our view, the question the studio is most slowly and most carefully answering. The answering is, in our view, finally, the work. The work is, in our view, finally, what the studio is for.
