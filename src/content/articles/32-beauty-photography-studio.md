On 11 August 1939 Horst P. Horst finished a sitting in Paris, left the studio at four in the morning and caught the seven o'clock train to Le Havre. He called the picture he had just made the last one he took in Paris before the war. The corset was by Detolle for Mainbocher; the model was Madame Bernon.

The photograph is *Mainbocher Corset*, a gelatin silver print of 347 by 275 millimetres, accession PH.222-1985 at the Victoria and Albert Museum. Where it ran is already disputed: the V&A record gives French Vogue, December 1939, its own gallery label says American Vogue, 1939, and Christie's dates a print to Paris, 11 August 1939. It was made on assignment for a magazine that sold advertising against it.

That is the argument here, and it is not a complaint. A couture corset, a Vogue page, a client who paid: beauty photography has always been commercial. The industry's mythology says its best practitioners were artists working from inner necessity; the paper trail says they were contractors who happened to be brilliant. The question this history keeps asking is narrow — who decided how the face was lit?

## Horst P. Horst, 1930s — the Vogue era

Start with the softbox, because nearly every retelling puts one in Horst's studio and none belonged there. The collapsible fabric softbox is a manufactured object of 1980, the year Chimera introduced the Lightbank and the year the company was founded. A 1930s room worked with Fresnels and other hard sources, broken up by scrims, silks, troughs and bounced tungsten.

Horst was explicit that the legend was tidier than the setup. "There appears to be only one source of light. But there were actually reflectors and other spotlights. I really don't know how I did it. I would not be able to repeat it." The single-source story is a fabrication the client approved.

The client had opinions of its own. Edna Woolman Chase, editor of American Vogue, put hers in writing: "I have been lecturing Horst about the lack of light in his photography. We have simply got to overcome this desire on the part of our photographers to shroud everything in the deepest mystery…" The house, not the photographer, was the aesthetic authority.

Condé Nast's pre-war house rules restricted its photographers to 10-by-8-inch large-format cameras and to studio work. Horst: "It took ten years, a world war, and a world-wide shortage of film for us to be allowed to use a Rolleiflex." The equipment was a permission, not a preference.

The face in that light was a Hollywood-derived, matte, high-coverage face. Max Factor's Pan-Cake, a solid cake applied with a damp sponge, had been developed for Technicolor; thin pencilled brows and pale ivory foundation were the period's standard. Mass-market cosmetics advertising was already a national magazine business.

## Avedon, 1940s and 1950s — the contract

Avedon's white background is usually dated to the war years, which is a decade early. He enlisted in the U.S. Maritime Service in 1942 and spent the war making crew ID portraits with a Rolleiflex — flat, frontal, plain background, the same format he later used for fashion. But the seamless white came later: the New York Times links his adoption of it to Brodovitch's use of white space on the page, not to the portrait commission.

He left the Merchant Marines in 1944, enrolled in Alexey Brodovitch's class at the New School for Social Research, showed his portfolio to Brodovitch and Carmel Snow, and was hired by Harper's Bazaar, first appearing in the October 1944 *Junior Bazaar*. The studio dates to 1945 or 1946, depending on which biography you trust.

The camera usually called his signature arrived late. The 8×10 Deardorff view camera is dated by several sources to about 1969, not to the Harper's Bazaar years. The Avedon look of the 1950s was made on other equipment, which is the first sign that a studio's tools get back-dated into its myths.

Revlon's *Fire & Ice* of 1952 put Dorian Leigh in a silver gown with red lips and nails under the tagline "Are you made for Fire & Ice?", with a questionnaire sorting women into Fire or Ice types. Four years later, Shirley Polykoff wrote "Does she… or doesn't she?" for Miss Clairol, a campaign credited with moving home hair colour from a minority practice to a majority one within a decade.

No primary Harper's Bazaar contract terms for Avedon have surfaced, so the commercial case has to be argued from the advertising. His ad work runs from a 1944 Milgrim department-store page to a 2004 Harry Winston page; clients include Revlon, Clairol, Chanel, Max Factor, L'Oréal, Pond's, Colgate, Dior and Versace. *Avedon Advertising* (Abrams, 2019) is the first survey of that archive.

The white is a construction, not an absence. The trade recipe is to light the background a stop or two above the subject, on a separate circuit, which makes the clean Avedon white a two-light system. Wikipedia dates his move to strobe, and the elimination of soft lights and props, to the late 1950s.

## Lindbergh, 1980s — the Dior era

There is no evidence of a Dior campaign by Peter Lindbergh in 1977 or in 1984, which is why this heading says the 1980s. La Galerie Dior's exhibition covers 1988 to 2018; Les Echos dates the collaboration from 1988; FashionNetwork places its start in the 1980s under Marc Bohan, whose tenure ran from 1960 to 1989.

His actual 1970s were German and unglamorous. Lindbergh moved to Düsseldorf in 1971, assisted Hans Lux for about two years, opened his own studio in 1973 and joined the Stern circle alongside Helmut Newton, Guy Bourdin and Hans Feurer. He did not move to Paris until 1978, which makes "the 1970s Dior era" a category error.

*Dior / Lindbergh* ran at 30 Avenue Montaigne until 4 May 2025, across ten spaces, with original Dior garments set among photographs, contact sheets, work prints and final proofs. Its source was the Peter Lindbergh Foundation archive, and the two-volume Taschen book *Peter Lindbergh. Dior* (2018) included a previously unseen shoot of more than 80 archive looks on New York streets, with Alek Wek, Karen Elson and Saskia de Brauw.

His method was black-and-white, minimal retouching, available light and grain — Wallpaper calls it a naturalist approach, models photographed "without artifice". Lindbergh said monochrome "helps communicate an impression of reality", and that colour could make beauty look like a cosmetics advertisement. The most artistic photographer in this lineage defined his art by refusing the commercial look, which concedes that the commercial look was the default.

The dated objects are firm, unlike the legends. In November 1988 he shot Anna Wintour's first American Vogue cover, Michaela Bercu outdoors in a Christian Lacroix T-shirt and jeans. The January 1990 British Vogue cover with Evangelista, Campbell, Patitz, Crawford and Turlington is the one credited with launching the supermodel era, and the only date worth citing.

## Mert Alas and Marcus Piggott, 1990s and 2000s — the digital

Mert Alaş and Marcus Piggott met at a party in England in 1994 and began making pictures together in 1995, shooting themselves and friends at home. They started working in fashion in 1997 from an East London loft turned studio; *Dazed & Confused* put their first photographs on the cover, and Katie Grand was the editor who published them.

The method is layered construction. Marcus said digital "just gives you so much freedom" after years in the darkroom; Mert said "It's fast." Grand's account is the clearest: "They change colours, play with backgrounds and put an image together in layers, much as a painter would do… controlled every step of the way."

The New York Times, in August 2004, framed them as the end of the 1990s natural-light, under-groomed look, with a "candid love of artifice"; the Louis Vuitton campaign with Diane Kruger was the breakout. W's Dennis Freedman called the result "ethereal, disorienting, sometimes unsettling" and asked whether the subject was flesh and blood or silicone. An art director, naming the skin problem out loud.

The part usually left out of the digital story is the payroll. Their studio runs an in-house team of digital artists and printers, a department that did not exist in the 1970s darkroom. What changed in the 1990s was not the camera; it was the staffing. Mert says the quiet part: "We are artists, we're not journalists, so we're meant to be selling dreams."

## The studio, in 2026

The same phrase — a beauty photographer's day rate — carries two numbers in 2026. At the top of the commercial market it is quoted at $10,000 to $20,000 per day including the photographer's team and licensing, and a worked example of two prep days and three shoot days comes to $40,000. At the volume end the benchmark is $500 to $3,000 per shoot day before crew, equipment and licensing, or $50 to $350 per final image.

Usage sets the difference, not equipment. The four axes are term — 3, 6, 12 or 24 months, or perpetual — territory from a single country to worldwide, media from organic social through print and OOH to packaging, and exclusivity from non-exclusive to full. "Buyout" may mean a copyright assignment or merely a perpetual licence, and the guidance is to ask which.

The 2026 kit is LED-heavy: 100 to 300 watts of continuous LED in a 60 by 90 centimetre softbox is quoted as covering about 95 per cent of portrait and product needs, adjustable from roughly 2700 to 6500 kelvin. The beauty dish survives because it is directional while still wrapping, sits on or near the lens axis and is usually gridded; it costs about $30 to $200.

The tent light is a lineage, not a metaphor. Chimera's founding product was a collapsible fabric light tent, the Lightbank of 1980, descended from mountain-tent design, and full-wrap tent lighting is still how shadow is removed from skin and reflective product. The correction about the 1930s is also a description of the 2026 room.

AI has arrived with a receipt. Guess ran an AI-generated model called Vivienne in Vogue's August 2025 print issue, made by the London agency Seraphinne Vallora; the backlash covered unrealistic beauty standards, lost work for models and photographers, and a barely visible disclaimer, and Condé Nast called it a commercial rather than editorial decision. Deals with AI social accounts fell about 30 per cent between January and August 2025 against 2024, and Dove has ruled out AI models.

Put the two rates side by side — $500 and $20,000 for the same three words — and the difference is almost entirely a licence to circulate a face: who may show it, where, and for how long. That is the argument, stated as a fee. The studio light was never only a decision about beauty. It was a decision about what the client could sell.
