The jasmine at Grasse is picked by hand, at night or at dawn, from July to October, and only once the flower is fully open. A report published on 15 September 2025 put the fresh crop at €87 to €107 a kilo; the same reporting found about 40 hectares of jasmine still cultivated in the district, on 26 farms and by 43 growers, and Grasse's flowers at 0.01 % of world jasmine production.

The story everyone knows is that the roses made Grasse. Half of it is true, and the half that is false is the interesting half. A kilo of Grasse centifolia absolute sells for €8,000 to €15,000 — but roses are no longer the crop the industry comes for. What Grasse offers now is technique: a school, extraction plants in the fields, and exclusive contracts that decide which hectares get planted at all.

## The arrival

Grasse stank, for six centuries, of tanning. From the twelfth to the sixteenth century hides were cured with urine, dung and limewater, and the finished leather went to Genoa and Pisa.

Florentine methods of perfuming leather were taken up by the local *gantiers*, the glovers, whose guild was recognised by royal edict in 1614, chartered again in 1656, and dissolved in 1791; by the end of the eighteenth century perfume had overtaken leather as the town's main business. Charabot, Molinard, Robertet and Fragonard descend from that trade, which began by hiding a smell.

What the town asked the world to protect was not the scenery but the skill. On 28 November 2018, at a session of UNESCO's Intergovernmental Committee sitting in the Republic of Mauritius, the element covering the cultivation of perfume plants, the processing of natural raw materials and the art of perfume composition went onto the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

The dossier was started in 2008 by the Association du Patrimoine Vivant du Pays de Grasse with the City and the growers. Four emblematic flowers are named: rose centifolia, jasmine, tuberose and violet.

The museum makes the argument better than the inscription does. The Musée International de la Parfumerie was founded in 1989 by the City of Grasse and the Agglomération du Pays de Grasse, is labelled a Musée de France, and occupies the historic Hôtel Pontevès complex; Frédéric Jung expanded it in 2008 to 3,500 m² and more than 50,000 objects.

What is being kept is a procedure — how to grow, how to extract, how to compose — and a procedure survives in a way a hillside of roses does not.

## The workbench

The plant does two things to a flower, in order. First, hexane — a non-polar hydrocarbon solvent, with benzene or petroleum ether in the older recipes — washes the petals and lifts the aromatics out with the waxes and pigments, leaving a semi-solid waxy paste: the *concrete*.

Then ethanol, typically 96 % at 35–40 °C, washes the concrete; it is chilled so the waxes precipitate, filtered, and the ethanol evaporated under vacuum, leaving the *absolute* at roughly 50 to 70 % of the concrete's weight. For Rosa centifolia the solvent route beats steam distillation, giving a softer, more honeyed result, while steam destroys heat-sensitive compounds such as phenethyl alcohol.

Scale is where the romance turns expensive. For damask rose, steam distillation needs 3,000 to 5,000 kg of petals — three to five million flowers — for one kilo of essential oil, and one published figure puts the absolute at 0.15 to 0.2 % of fresh flower weight, about 500 to 667 kg of flowers for a kilo.

Other published ratios run higher. Industrial extraction is unhurried too: hexane percolates a charge for five to six hours at 30 °C, the ethanol wash about eight hours at 35–40 °C, then eight hours of chill at −20 °C. Days of plant, for a kilo of concentrate.

The factory has moved into the field. The Mul family, Chanel's exclusive partner since 1987, runs extraction on its own land; Chanel owns an extraction plant in the fields as well; IFF-LMR's Laboratoire Monique Rémy converts concrete into absolute; Robertet, Mane, Firmenich, IFF and Givaudan have put money back into Grasse, where rose plantings have expanded since 2000. The teaching followed the plants: ISIPCA, founded in 1970 by Jean-Jacques Guerlain in Versailles and later relocated to the Grasse area, sits alongside the GIP and the École Supérieure Internationale du Parfum, which works with the University of Montpellier.

## The materials

None of that survives without the harvest. Jasmine comes in from July to November, picked at night or at dawn when the flower is fully open, and Grasse's crop is largely spoken for: the Mul family has held an exclusive contract with Chanel since 1987, and Dior runs exclusivity contracts of its own.

Chanel farms the Domaine de Manon; Dior holds the Château de la Colle Noire at Montauroux, bought in 1951; Lancôme has seven hectares at the Domaine de la Rose de Lancôme; Louis Vuitton has Les Fontaines Parfumées since 2016.

The rose runs on a tighter clock. Rose de Mai has a window of about three weeks — roughly 5 to 25 May, though other accounts open it earlier, in late April — and each flower is picked on the day it opens, at dawn, and must be processed within hours.

Weather arrives as arithmetic: the drought of 2022 cost Grasse producers up to 50 % of the jasmine harvest and 40 % of the tuberose. Forty hectares of jasmine and a three-week May window are the whole of the local crop now.

Which leaves the awkward question of why anyone farms it. The answers are cheaper elsewhere. Synthetic jasmine chemistry — methyl dihydrojasmonate, sold as *Hedione* — reproduces the radiance of the real flower, and linalool and geraniol stand in for rose and lavender; Grasse material runs 20 to 30 % dearer than Egyptian or Indian.

The land is the second problem: about €150,000 a hectare for Grasse farmland treated as buildable, against roughly €1,500 a hectare in Normandy. And the work has gone where the wages are: Egypt, Morocco, Tunisia, Turkey, Bulgaria and India take the market, with Egypt and India supplying about 95 % of the world's jasmine extract. What survives in Grasse is not a competitive crop but a contracted one.

## The organ

The bench itself is a piece of furniture. The perfumer's organ is a curved, stepped desk with a tiered, keyboard-shaped layout, carrying hundreds of vials of natural materials and synthesised molecules within immediate reach; every perfumer arranges it differently and no two organs match.

Vials cluster by family, citrus high for the opening and base materials low for the drydown, auditioned drop by drop on blotters. Jean Carles, who composed Miss Dior in 1947, ran Roure's Grasse house and taught generations of perfumers; his method is deliberately musical, built on reusable *accords* that smell as one odour.

What arrives on that desk is a brief, which may specify the brand, the mood, the market and the price. The perfumer converts it into top, heart and base, and the trials are assessed over months, sometimes years.

The narrowing is severe: from hundreds or thousands of materials down to roughly fifty, tested on paper and on skin since body chemistry changes the opening, then checked for stability, safety, regulatory limits and price. The visitor-facing version is smaller: at Galimard's Studio des Fragrances each participant works at a personal organ of 126 essences.

## The formula

The people who could still do this work were, for the most part, bought. During the 1960s and 1970s international firms — IFF, Givaudan, Firmenich, Quest — acquired local houses including Chiris and Lautier, severing factories from fields and turning to global sourcing; by the 1970s and 1980s French houses had largely abandoned French flowers.

Ernest Beaux's *Chanel No. 5*, made in 1921 in the Chiris laboratory, is the formula that changed what a perfume could be, an aldehyde construction laid over a floral heart. Robertet's Julien Maubert: "In the 1980s–1990s, it was far from a given." No single authority governs perfumery classification either, and a fragrance routinely belongs to several families at once.

Two narrowings run in parallel through this town — hundreds of materials reduced to roughly fifty by taste, a broad crop reduced to 40 hectares of jasmine by contract — and it is not obvious which is the more fragile.

## The finishing

What decides what may be sold is a discipline of its own. IFRA, the International Fragrance Association, was founded in 1973 and works from Geneva, drawing its safety data from RIFM, founded in 1966. Its 51st Standards Amendment, the latest published, carries guidance documents dated 3 July 2023; the 52nd is not yet in force, its consultation having closed on 12 June 2026, with formal notification pending. Lilial is banned in the EU as of 2022, and a finished absolute must carry less than 10 ppm of residual hexane.

The change that will matter most to a bottle bought next year is EU law. Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/1545, published in July 2023, added 56 fragrance allergens to mandatory on-pack disclosure, taking the number of individually labelled substances from the 25 or 26 labelled since 2003 to more than 80 and bringing substance mixtures and natural extracts into the declaration for the first time.

From 31 July 2026 only compliant products may be placed on the market; after 31 July 2028, goods placed on the market before that deadline without the expanded declarations may no longer be made available. The molecules now required on the pack are, in many cases, the very ones that stood in for Grasse's flowers.

## The leaving

France's cosmetics trade is not in doubt. The sector exported a record €22.5 bn in 2024, up 6.8 % on 2023 and a slowdown from 10.8 % the year before; perfume alone accounted for €8 bn, up 13.6 %, roughly 35 % of all cosmetics exports. The figures come from FEBEA on 7 February 2025, via the trade press.

The United States took €2.8 bn, up 17.6 %; China €1.78 bn, down 8.9 %. Perfumes were 52.4 % of French cosmetics exports to the US, and the sector's surplus of €17.6 bn makes it France's second trade-balance contributor after aeronautics. UN Comtrade, on a different classification, puts global perfume trade at $28.6 bn and France first at about $10.3 bn.

So France exports more finished fragrance than any other country and buys its flowers abroad, and the two sets of numbers point in opposite directions. Grasse's contribution to the first is a chemical system and a handful of contracts; its contribution to the second is 40 hectares, 43 growers and a three-week window in May.

The industry is not trying to solve the harvest with machines — no mechanical picker appears anywhere in the record — but at the molecular level, with Hedione and its relatives, which is what makes the flowers fragile: they live on an underwriting, not on a market. What remains in Grasse is not the romance but the compounding — a school, plants in the fields, and contracts that decide, before anything is picked, what will be planted at all.
