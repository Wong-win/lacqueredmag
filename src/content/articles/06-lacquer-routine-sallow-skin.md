We owe a debt of honesty here, before we begin: *sallow* is a difficult word. It has, in the last decade, become a word used mostly to warn women away from warm undertones. *"Don't wear orange, you'll look sallow."* *"Avoid yellow undertones, they'll make your skin sallow."* This is a misuse of the word. Sallow is a *colouring*, not a flaw. It is what warm, slightly olive, slightly muted skin looks like in the wrong light, or against the wrong shade of foundation, or under the wrong concealer. The skin itself has done nothing wrong.

What follows is not a corrective routine for a defect. It is a guide for warm, muted, slightly olive complexions — often called *sallow* by the industry — to assemble a small, deliberate *lacquered* routine. The aim is not to make the skin disappear. The aim is to make the colour you already have more legible to yourself and to the room.

We will move through the routine in the order you would actually apply it.

---

## 1. The cleanse — no surfactants in the morning

In the morning, you do not need a cleanser. You need water, or a cream that rinses off. The skin at this point in the day has done nothing wrong overnight — a dry, slightly warm face needs only to be cleared of the residue of whatever you applied at night.

We are partial to a plain cream cleanser at this stage — a hyaluronic acid face wash is fine; an emulsifying oil cleanser is fine — but the rule is that the morning cleanse should not contain a true surfactant. Save the foaming cleansers for the end of the day, when there is something to clean.

Pat the skin dry with a clean towel. Do not rub.

## 2. The hydrating layer — one serum, not four

There is no skin in the world that needs four serums in the morning. There is no skin that needs four serums at any time. The morning hydrating layer is a single product, applied to damp skin, and left to absorb for sixty seconds before anything else goes on top.

We are partial to a low-molecular-weight hyaluronic serum at this stage, but the choice is not the point. The point is that this layer is the canvas for the day. Anything you apply now will behave differently depending on what is beneath it; don't make the canvas complicated.

If the skin is reactive in the morning — pink across the cheeks or along the jaw — leave it alone for a week. There is no serum that fixes reactive skin; reactive skin resolves itself when you stop messing with it.

## 3. The moisturiser — emollient, not occlusive

The morning moisturiser is an *emollient*, not an *occlusive*. The distinction matters for our skin type. An emollient softens the skin; an occlusive seals it. Sealing, in the morning, beneath makeup, is a recipe for product pilling and the slightly greasy halo that haunts photographs from the late 2000s.

A light ceramide moisturiser is the right call here. Apply a thin layer — about a third of what you think you need — and let it settle. By the time you put the next product on, the skin should feel supported, not coated. If it feels coated, you have used too much.

## 4. The SPF — non-negotiable, and tinted is fine

You are using SPF in the morning. This is the rule. The brand and the format can vary; the rule cannot.

For our skin type, a tinted mineral SPF is often the right call, because it does the work of two products. The iron oxides in a tinted mineral SPF will, on a warm complexion, correct the slight ashy cast that a pure zinc cream can leave on olive skin. The tint is also — let's say it — a beginning; you can stop here and go to the office.

If you would rather use an untinted SPF, do so. But add a pigmented step before you leave the house. The next section explains why.

## 5. The pigment — saturation, not coverage

A sallow-looking complexion is, more often than not, a *desaturated* complexion. The skin has not lost its colour; it has, by some chain of cleansers and moisturisers and sunscreens and lights, had its colour *lifted* by other colours around it. The corrective move is not to add more pigments — it is to add *deeper* pigments, in the right places.

For our editors with warm, muted skin, this often means a cream blush in a warm rose or terracotta (something with a touch of brown in the base, not a cool pink), tapped onto the high cheekbone and blended with a finger. A small amount. A single layer. The cheek is the place where colour on a face is most legible; if you are going to put pigment anywhere, the cheek is the place.

The lip is the second place. Sallow skin, in our experience, carries a satin or semi-matte lip very well. Gloss is more difficult — it picks up the surrounding room light in ways that can wash the colour out — but a satin or cream finish will hold. Avoid the very pale, very nude lip; on warm-muted skin, it tends to read as *bare* in a way that reads as *tired*. A deeper nude, two or three shades deeper than your natural lip, will be your most flattering neutral.

## 6. The setting — a single, light pass

Set the centre of the face with the lightest possible pass of loose powder. The aim is not to flatten the skin; the aim is to take the shine off the centre of the forehead, the bridge of the nose, and the chin. The edges of the face — the perimeter, the cheeks, the jaw — should be left alone. The skin you see at the edge of the face is the skin you want to see; the powder is to remove the midday shine, not to blur the dew.

A small, soft brush. One dip into the powder. Tap off the excess. A single pass over the centre of the face. Stop.

## Closing notes

We have written this guide around an assumption that the reader is interested in building a *lacquered* routine, by which we mean a routine that lets the day — and the camera — see what was put on. If you would rather build a *vanishing* routine, by which we mean a routine that hides everything that was put on, this guide is not for you. There are many guides for that routine; the magazine industry has been writing them, almost exclusively, for fifteen years.

The routine above is small. It is five products. It takes about seven minutes. By the end of it, your face is not invisible — it is *legible*. The colour is yours. The lacquer is yours. The choice is yours.

That, finally, is what we are arguing for.
