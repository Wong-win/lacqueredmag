The fragrance industry has, for the last forty years, been selling *collections*. The fragrance collection is, in the industry's imagination, an *additive* thing — a bottle added, each birthday, each anniversary, each promotion, until the dressing table holds a small forest of bottles that the wearer, in many cases, can no longer remember wearing.

The fragrance industry has, in our view, been selling the wrong thing. The fragrance industry should, in our view, be selling a *wardrobe*. A wardrobe is, by definition, a small set of pieces that work together. A wardrobe is curated. A wardrobe is, in most cases, smaller than the collection. A wardrobe is, in most cases, what the wearer actually wears.

The six bottles below are, in our view, the wardrobe of the year. The six bottles are, in our view, the bottles that, in the right month, on the right skin, do the work the year is asking of them. The six bottles are, in sum, the architecture of a fragrance year.

The fragrance year has, in our view, six *months* of consequence. The summer months (June, July, August) are one season, and we have collapsed them into one bottle. The winter months (December, January, February) are another, collapsed into one bottle. The shoulder months (March, April, May and September, October, November) are the four that get the remaining four bottles. The wardrobe, in our view, is the bottles, not the months. The months are the *rhythm*. The rhythm is the *wardrobe*.

## January — the sharp green

The first bottle is for January. The first bottle is, in our view, the bottle that has to do the most work for the fewest people. The first bottle is, in our view, the bottle that is, in midwinter, on the wearer's skin, after the holidays, when nothing is yet in bloom.

The January bottle is, in our view, a sharp green. The green is not, in our view, the green of a leaf. The green is, in our view, the green of a *cut stem* — galbanum, fig leaf, the bitter top note of a freshly-broken twig. The green is, in our view, what the wearer needs in January, when the world is brown and the wearer is, often, brown with it.

The January bottle is, in our view, *not* a heavy bottle. The January bottle is, in our view, a bottle that wears close to the skin. The January bottle is, in our view, a bottle that, by February, you have stopped noticing and decided to wear again in February. The January bottle is, in sum, the bottle that *cuts through* the winter.

## March — the iris

The second bottle is for March. The second bottle is, in our view, the bottle that is, in early spring, when the first warmth has arrived but nothing has bloomed, the bottle that the wearer reaches for when she wants to feel that the year has, finally, turned.

The March bottle is, in our view, an iris. The iris is, in perfumery, one of the most expensive raw materials — the iris butter, extracted from the rhizome of the *Iris pallida*, can take three years to mature, and is, in 2026, more expensive per kilo than saffron. The iris is, in our view, what the wearer smells when she wants to feel that she is wearing something that has, in itself, *taken time*.

The March bottle is, in our view, the bottle that wears best on the *cool* skin. The iris is, on the warm skin, often too powdery. The iris is, on the cool skin, lifted by the skin's natural temperature, and produces, in most cases, a smell that is, in our view, more interesting than the smell of the iris alone.

The March bottle is, in our view, the bottle that is, by April, the bottle that the wearer is, often, still wearing. The March bottle is the bottle of the *long spring* — the bottle that does not, in most cases, get retired in early May.

## May — the cologne-grade citrus

The third bottle is for May. The third bottle is, in our view, the bottle that, in late spring, when the warmth is, finally, sustained, is the bottle that the wearer reaches for when she wants to feel that the year has, finally, opened.

The May bottle is, in our view, a *cologne-grade* citrus. The cologne-grade is the key. The cologne-grade is, in perfumery, a particular concentration — usually 4-6% perfume oils in alcohol, often with a higher water content than the eau de toilette. The cologne-grade is, in our view, the lightest of the four concentrations that the contemporary market recognises (after cologne, eau de cologne, eau de toilette, eau de parfum). The cologne-grade is, in our view, what the wearer wears in late spring, when the weather is, finally, warm enough to need a *light* fragrance.

The May bottle is, in our view, the bottle that is, by June, the bottle that the wearer is, often, no longer wearing. The cologne-grade is, in our view, a summer-grade, not a heat-grade. The May bottle is the bottle of the *transition*, not the bottle of the *full*.

## July — the white floral or the marine

The fourth bottle is for July. The fourth bottle is, in our view, the bottle that, in high summer, when the heat is, finally, at its peak, is the bottle that the wearer reaches for when she wants to feel that the year is, finally, alive.

The July bottle is, in our view, a *white floral* or a *marine*. The white floral is, in our view, the traditional answer. The white floral is the jasmine, the tuberose, the gardenia, the orange flower. The white floral is, in our view, the bottle that, on a hot evening, on the skin, smells, finally, *hot*. The white floral is, in our view, the bottle that the heritage fragrance houses have, for a hundred years, marketed as the summer fragrance.

The marine is, in our view, the contemporary answer. The marine is the synthetic ozonic note — the smell of *Calone*, the molecule developed in the 1990s that smells, on the skin, of *sea air*. The marine is, in our view, the bottle that the contemporary fragrance houses have, in the last thirty years, marketed as the *alternative* summer fragrance. The marine is, in our view, the bottle that, on a hot day, on the skin, smells, finally, *cool*.

The July bottle is, in our view, the bottle that is, in our view, the *one or the other*, not the *both*. The white floral and the marine do not, in most cases, mix well on the same skin. The choice is, in most cases, the wearer's.

## September — the chypre

The fifth bottle is for September. The fifth bottle is, in our view, the bottle that, in early autumn, when the light has, finally, gone golden, is the bottle that the wearer reaches for when she wants to feel that the year has, finally, matured.

The September bottle is, in our view, a *chypre*. The chypre is, in perfumery, a family — bergamot in the top, oakmoss and patchouli in the base, often with a floral heart. The chypre is, in our view, the most *constructed* of the fragrance families. The chypre is, in our view, what the wearer wears when she wants to feel that her fragrance is, finally, an *argument*.

The September bottle is, in our view, the bottle that, by October, the wearer is, often, still wearing. The chypre is, in our view, the bottle that, in our view, the year has been *building toward*. The chypre is, in our view, the bottle of the *long autumn* — the bottle that does not, in most cases, get retired in early November.

The chypre is, in our view, the bottle that, in 2026, has been, in some formulations, restricted by IFRA's oakmoss limitations. The contemporary chypre is, in many cases, the chypre that has been *re-formulated* to comply with the regulation. The re-formulated chypre is, in our view, a chypre that is, in many cases, still worth wearing. The chypre is, in our view, the bottle that is, finally, the bottle of the *year's argument*.

## November — the oud or the amber

The sixth bottle is for November. The sixth bottle is, in our view, the bottle that, in late autumn, when the light has, finally, gone thin, is the bottle that the wearer reaches for when she wants to feel that the year is, finally, ending well.

The November bottle is, in our view, an *oud* or an *amber*. The oud is, in perfumery, the resinous heartwood of the agar tree, infected by a particular mould, and distilled into a dark, animalic, often expensive oil. The oud is, in our view, the bottle of the *deep* — the bottle that the wearer wears when she wants to feel that her fragrance has, finally, *weight*.

The amber is, in perfumery, a *constructed* family — a base of labdanum, vanilla, benzoin, often with a touch of tonka or a touch of a synthetic amber molecule. The amber is, in our view, the bottle of the *warm* — the bottle that the wearer wears when she wants to feel that her fragrance is, finally, *holding her*.

The November bottle is, in our view, the bottle that, by December, the wearer is, often, still wearing. The November bottle is the bottle of the *late autumn* — the bottle that does not, in most cases, get retired in early December.

---

The six bottles, in sum, are: a sharp green, an iris, a cologne-grade citrus, a white floral or a marine, a chypre, and an oud or an amber. The six bottles are, in our view, the wardrobe of the year. The wardrobe is, by intention, *small* — six bottles, not sixty. The wardrobe is, by intention, *curated* — one bottle per month, not one bottle per occasion. The wardrobe is, by intention, *seasonal* — the bottle for the season, not the bottle for the mood.

The wardrobe is, in our view, also, by intention, *aspirational*. The wardrobe is, in our view, the wardrobe that the wearer builds *over time*, not the wardrobe that the wearer buys in a year. The wardrobe is, in our view, the wardrobe that the wearer *replaces* when a bottle runs out, not the wardrobe that the wearer *adds to* when a new launch arrives.

The wardrobe is, in sum, the architecture of the year. The architecture is, in our view, the architecture of slowness. The slowness is, in our view, what the fragrance has, in the last twenty years, lost. The slowness is, in our view, what the fragrance is, finally, for.
