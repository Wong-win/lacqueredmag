Chanel N°19 opens on galbanum, and galbanum does not smell like a lawn. Chanel's copy lists neroli and Persian galbanum at the top, Florentine iris in the middle, cedar and oakmoss underneath. Three materials, one January — and the month is the least interesting thing about it.

A fragrance wardrobe is usually sold as a calendar: something light for spring, something cold for summer, something dark when the light goes. No study, survey or trade report turned up that connects temperature or humidity to what a person sprays. The seasonal story is a merchandising convention, useful and unproven in equal measure.

What the six bottles below actually share is a set of solvents. Galbanum, orris butter, cologne-strength citrus, calone, oakmoss, agarwood — six answers to one question, which is how much plant or animal matter it takes to make a smell, and what that matter costs.

The costs run from €250 a kilogram to somewhere past €100,000, a spread of roughly four hundred times inside a single wardrobe. That gradient is the spine of the piece. It follows yield, and yield follows years in the ground.

## January — the sharp green

Chanel N°19 is the standard galbanum overdose of the family: an iris and vetiver heart written on a galbanum overdose, as Osmetheca puts it. Henri Robert composed it. The launch year is 1970 in one trade table and 1971 in another, a disagreement a fifty-year-old formula attracts.

Galbanum is an oleoresin from *Ferula gummosa*, an umbellifer grown mainly in Khorasan and Mazandaran, with smaller supply from Afghanistan and Turkey. The resin is cut by hand from the root collar and harvested in June and July. One plant yields a few grams a season.

In a formula it is dosed at 0.1 to 0.5 per cent. The green-pepper and crushed-leek facet comes from methoxypyrazines; alpha- and beta-pinene make up more than half the oil. European industrial prices for 2025–26 are €250 to €500 a kilogram for the essential oil, €200 to €400 for the resinoid.

Its golden age is dated 1947 to 1974: Bandit in 1944, Vent Vert in 1947, Miss Dior the same year, Chamade in 1969, N°19, Cristalle in 1974. Underneath all of them sits the chypre frame of bergamot, labdanum and oakmoss that Coty fixed in 1917. January's green never escapes it.

## March — the iris

Orris butter costs €40,000 to €100,000 a kilogram in 2026. Two independent sources give that range identically, which is rare enough in this trade to note. The number is load-bearing: the rest of this wardrobe can be explained by chemistry, and March can only be explained by patience.

The patience is six to eight years. *Iris pallida* spends about three years in the ground, then three to five more drying and ageing after the rhizomes are lifted and peeled. Only then does the material smell of anything worth the money.

Yield explains the rest. Dried rhizome gives roughly 0.1 to 0.3 per cent butter, about two kilograms per tonne. Italy produces ten to fifteen tonnes of dried root a year, which becomes a few hundred kilograms of finished orris products.

Irone content separates the grades: eight to twenty per cent in butter and concrete, fifty-five to eighty-five per cent in the refined absolute, the rest largely odourless myristic acid. The reference is *Iris pallida* from Tuscany, around San Polo in Chianti. This is why a powdery violet on a €60 bottle is not orris — alpha-isomethyl ionone, Irival and Orris Pure are the named replacements, and they are perfectly good materials. They are simply not six years in the ground.

## May — the cologne-grade citrus

Eau de cologne is a concentration before it is a place: 2 to 5 per cent aromatic concentrate in a 70 to 90 per cent ethanol base, one to two hours of clear presence, a faint trace at four. Giovanni Maria Farina, born in Piedmont in 1685, made his Eau de Cologne in Cologne in 1709; a letter describing it dates from 1708. The house still trades, and the formula is secret.

4711's dates arrive as a set rather than a year. The brand's own account begins on 8 October 1792, when Wilhelm Mülhens received an aqua mirabilis recipe; French occupying forces assigned the house number in 1794; the trademark came in 1875. Wikipedia's article body says "since at least 1799." Mäurer & Wirtz has owned the brand since 12 December 2006.

The structure is what the month buys: Calabrian bergamot, Sicilian lemon, bitter orange, neroli and petitgrain over lavender, rosemary and thyme, with musk, vetiver, cedar and oakmoss below. Guerlain's Impériale, commissioned by Empress Eugénie in 1853, carries 69 bees on its bottle; 4711 Original lists oakmoss, Tahitian vetiver, cedar, patchouli, sandalwood and musk in its base.

Cologne is the cheapest thing here per millilitre, and the reason is structural: classic bottles run 200 to 400 ml. Scento's index of 12 October 2026, covering 950 perfumes from 91 houses, puts the 30 ml median at €3.46/ml for niche and €2.36/ml for designer — a gap of roughly 48 per cent, which measures positioning more than juice.

## July — the white floral or the marine

Calone, CAS 28940-11-6, methylbenzodioxepinone, is the molecule that made the 1990s smell like a swimming pool, and its history is a date trap: Wikipedia dates the discovery to 1966 at Pfizer, while the trade name "Calone 1951" refers to the brand rather than the molecule. More surprising is the legal status. Calone carries no IFRA standard — unrestricted at typical use in all categories — and ScenTree and FragranceHelp both report no restriction under the 51st Amendment of 2023.

Dosing is practical rather than legal. Olfactorian's community medians are 0.9 per cent as an accent, 2.1 to modulate and 3.8 to build; FragranceHelp puts fine-fragrance use at or below 0.8 per cent. Overdosed, calone reads metallic and fishy rather than marine. Dihydromyrcenol, the accord's other half, boils at 194 to 197 °C and passed 1,000 metric tons a year worldwide by 2004.

Acqua di Giò is the benchmark: Giorgio Armani, 1996, Alberto Morillas, an aquatic composition that by 2015 was estimated at about 30 per cent of Armani's perfume sales, with the EDT and Profumo retailing between €280m and €300m.

The white floral answer is older and more expensive. Fracas, composed by Germaine Cellier for Robert Piguet in 1948, is built on Indian tuberose absolute, Tunisian orange blossom absolute, French jasmine and Italian iris root butter. Tuberose is *Polianthes tuberosa*, a Mexican native domesticated by the Aztecs.

## September — the chypre

Chypre is a structure, not a mood. Bergamot, orange, lemon or neroli on top; a heart built on cistus labdanum; oakmoss, patchouli and musk underneath. The effect depends on contrast, and on what the trade calls a certain bitterness in the drydown. François Coty's *Chypre* of 1917 is the first composition to use the bergamot–labdanum–oakmoss accord; it survives at the Osmothèque, and the name refers to Cyprus.

The canon that followed has firm dates: Mitsouko in 1919, Femme in 1944, Ma Griffe in 1946, Miss Dior in 1947, Cabochard in 1959, Calèche in 1961, Givenchy III in 1970. Aromatics Elixir is 1971 in one table and 1972 in another. Every one of them is built on a material that has since been capped.

Oakmoss is *Evernia prunastri*, a lichen — fungus and green alga in one — not a moss, and it yields 3 to 8 per cent absolute by dried weight. The regulation targets two molecules inside it, atranol and chloroatranol, both strong skin sensitisers. IFRA restricted them in 2003 and refined the rule through the 43rd amendment in 2008.

Trade summaries put the current ceiling at 0.1 per cent in finished fine fragrance; the IFRA PDF itself downloaded unreadable, so that figure carries a caveat. What matters is the dose. Low-atranol absolute goes in at 0.01 to 0.1 per cent, which means a 2020s chypre cannot be built the 1917 way even with compliant moss.

The substitute stack does the rest: Evernyl and Veramoss, both methyl atrarate from IFF, for the dry facet; patchouli fractions, Givaudan's Akigalawood, Iso E Super, ambroxan and labdanum for depth. Supply moved with the rule — Morocco's Atlas range has led low-atranol material since 2003, with Robertet, Mane and Biolandes named. The neo-chypre has years now: Masque Milano Tango in 2014, Tom Ford Vert des Bois in 2016.

## November — the oud or the amber

Agarwood is a wound, not a wood. *Aquilaria* has 17 species, nine of which produce the resin; A. malaccensis is the main commercial source, with A. crassna, A. sinensis and Gyrinops also yielding. Wounding — the ambrosia beetle Dinoplatypus chevrolati is one route — lets fungi in, notably Phaeoacremonium parasitica. Healthy heartwood is pale and scentless; infected wood turns dense, dark and resinous. Natural infection happens in about seven trees out of a hundred.

The yield is the reason for the price: about 70 kg of wood gives no more than 20 ml of oil. A. malaccensis has been on CITES Appendix II since 1995, and every Aquilaria species joined it in 2004. Wikipedia puts superior pure material near $290,000 a kilogram, an isolated claim best read as a ceiling, while plantation or underage-harvested material can be had for as little as $100.

The top of that range is not where most oud lives. Cashmeran, the documentable oud-adjacent captive, was developed by IFF in the 1970s, credited to John Hall, described as spicy-musk-woody-clean, used at about 2 per cent. Ambergris runs €15 to €50 a gram in 2026 for high-grade white and grey material; a tincture is made at 3 to 5 per cent in high-proof alcohol and dosed at 0.5 to 3 per cent of the finished perfume.

Legality is the part nobody puts on the card. Sperm whales have been on CITES Appendix I since 1981, but CITES treats naturally excreted, aged white ambergris as an excretion rather than a derivative. The United States bans it outright under the Marine Mammal Protection Act of 1972, with no beach-cast exemption, while the EU, UK, Switzerland, Australia and Japan accept beach-found material with provenance.

The split explains the short list of natural users — Roja Dove, Henry Jacques, a handful of artisans — and the industry's reliance on synthetics. Ambroxide, CAS 6790-58-5, is the key odour component of ambergris, an autoxidation product of ambrein. Labdanum, the tacky resin from Cistus ladanifer and C. creticus, does the rest. Six bottles, six solvents, and a cost gradient of four hundred times that tracks yield rather than weather.
