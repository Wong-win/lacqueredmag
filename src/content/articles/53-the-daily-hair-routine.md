There are five steps in the ordinary hair routine, bought in a single afternoon: a shampoo, a conditioner, a leave-in, a heat protectant, and a towel with a fabric specification on the box. Two have peer-reviewed support, and the American Academy of Dermatology's guidance covers a third. The other two are defended by brand content and explainer blogs.

The reader's assumption is that the routine is a matter of finding the right shampoo. The evidence here says otherwise. Frequency, the variable people argue about most, is close to settled: in the one controlled comparison of daily against weekly washing, daily won on every endpoint, with no objective harm detected. What is not settled is the thing nobody markets. The load-bearing variable is *time wet*, and the one controlled study of drying methods reached a conclusion that reverses the received advice.

## Step one — the wash

Frequency was settled in 2021, by a paper in *Skin Appendage Disorders*. Punyani, Tosti, Hordinsky, Yeomans and Schwartz ran two studies in Asian populations without scalp disease: one epidemiological, comparing natural washing habits, and one controlled, assigning frequencies. Assessments peaked at five to six washes a week in the first. In the second, daily washing beat weekly washing on every endpoint, with no objective detrimental effect on the hair. A Beijing biotech conducted the work; neither arm was a long-term randomised trial.

What the paper does not contain is a threshold. Secondary coverage turned the five-to-six figure into an optimum and invented a cliff beyond which washing becomes harmful. The real guidance is texture-conditional. Dr. Oyetewa Oyerinde, at Baylor College of Medicine, wrote in 2024 that straight hair wants three to four washes a week, daily for what she calls super pin straight; loose curls and waves, two to three; tightly curled or coily hair, once a week, because curl stops sebum travelling down the shaft.

Sebum is the other half of the wash. It is a protective film, not dirt. Daily washing does not trigger rebound oiliness — that belongs to harsh alkaline or clarifying shampoos with very hot water, a product-and-temperature effect, not a frequency one. And shedding is not a washing problem; it runs on genetics, hormones, nutrition, telogen effluvium and androgenetic alopecia.

The bottle is next. D'Souza and Rathi, in the Indian Journal of Dermatology in 2015, give the clearest account: two to four surfactants, the first listed the primary cleanser. Lauryl sulfates foam well and strip sebum hard enough to leave hair rough and dull; cationic surfactants cleanse poorly but improve softness, so they belong to the conditioner; plant-derived saponins lather beautifully while cleansing poorly — an inclusion the authors call *largely promotional*. That is the shortest answer to the sulfate-free argument. Warm water for the wash, cool water for the rinse, fingertips rather than nails.

## Step two — the condition

A conditioner is, chemically, a cationic system built on quaternary ammonium compounds — cetyltrimethylammonium chloride and behentrimonium chloride. Being positively charged, they adhere electrostatically to the negatively charged fibre, preferentially where it is damaged or weathered. The documented effects are six, all of them surface effects: less static, flatter cuticle scales, lower friction between fibres, more light reflected back, easier combing wet and dry, and the temporary sealing of split ends.

The chemistry explains why wet hair tangles. Keratin's *isoelectric point* is pH 3.67, so at the pH of wash water the cuticle carries a net negative charge; alkaline shampoos strip the protective 18-MEA lipid layer and expose cysteic acid groups, and damaged hair therefore carries a stronger negative charge than intact hair.

That is why it tangles and frizzes most, and why the quat cancels those charges. Conditioners are formulated acidic for the same reason, commonly pH 3.5 to 5.0, so water-swollen scales contract and a cool rinse closes them further.

None of this reaches inside the fibre. A conditioner cannot repair fractured disulphide bonds or reverse cortex damage, and it has no effect on follicle biology. Placement follows: mid-lengths to ends, not the scalp, because the ends are the oldest, most weathered part of the fibre. Nor does conditioner cause hair loss — shed hairs slide out and are noticed. The rule inverts by texture: the Academy tells curly-haired readers to condition all their hair, not just the ends.

The ingredient list is the specification. Fatty alcohols — cetyl, stearyl, cetearyl — give slip and body without the dryness their name suggests: behentrimonium at twenty-two carbons gives maximum slip, cetrimonium is lighter for fine hair. Amodimethicone is a cationic silicone, so it targets damaged, high-porosity areas selectively, and D'Souza and Rathi put the useful silicone range at three to five per cent for Asian hair against roughly half a per cent for Caucasian hair.

## Step three — the towel

The American Academy of Dermatology's own instruction is to wrap rather than rub: dry your hair by wrapping it in a towel after a shower. Its reason for handling wet hair as little as possible: wet hair breaks more easily when combed or brushed — with one exception, because people with tightly curled or textured hair should brush it when wet.

Its healthy-hair tips recommend a towel or a t-shirt, the t-shirt being a low-friction alternative to *terry*. Water disrupts keratin's internal hydrogen bonds and swells the cortex, so wet hair stretches further before it yields.

The percentages circulating for wet-hair fragility are a warning about the genre: the same number appears as a probability of breakage and a loss of tensile strength, credited to journals that cannot be found. Only the direction of the effect is supportable, and what supports it is the Academy's sentence. Friction runs the other way: terry loops are abrasive, their irregularities catching and lifting the cuticle scales rather than merely absorbing water, so terry generates significantly more friction against hair than a smoother fabric.

And here the industry's habit reappears: the microfibre-versus-terry case is built on supplier laboratories and brand blogs. Nantong Patni Textile Technology states that a 400 GSM microfibre towel absorbs seven to eight times its own weight against four to five for cotton, and that ambient drying takes sixty to ninety minutes against ninety to a hundred and fifty — while conceding that the figures are indicative only.

The multiplier most often quoted in the argument has no traceable source; the DOI attached to it resolves to a 2021 paper about radiofrequency microneedling in porcine skin. What survives is the function: a towel shortens the wet period.

## Step four — the leave-in

Here the evidence thins. The Academy treats a leave-in as a distinct third step after the rinse-off conditioner: apply oil or leave-in conditioner after washing and conditioning, and, for detangling, wet the hair, apply the leave-in, and work through it in sections with a wide-toothed comb, never brushing dry hair.

The label cue for curly-haired readers — argan oil, glycerin, or a fatty alcohol such as cetyl or stearyl alcohol — is the same three classes as the conditioner's backbone. Mechanically it is that system at a different dose with no rinse.

What the record does not contain is any controlled trial of leave-in conditioner: no dosing data, no evidence that the step is necessary for straight hair that is not heat-styled. Nothing shows a leave-in providing thermal protection — a separate product class — and nothing shows it repairing damage inside the fibre.

Treat the step as optional and texture-dependent, applied to damp rather than soaking hair, and watch for silicone buildup: dimethicone is water-insoluble and accumulates, while water-soluble silicones such as dimethicone copolyol rinse out cleanly.

## Step five — the dry

The drying evidence is where the received wisdom is most confidently wrong, and the source is a 2011 paper in the *Annals of Dermatology* — Lee, Kim, Hyun, Pi, Jin and Lee, on hair shaft damage from heat and drying time. Hair tresses, thirty treatments per group, five conditions.

The conditions: an untreated control; natural drying at 20 °C; a dryer for sixty seconds at fifteen centimetres, the hair at 47 °C; thirty seconds at ten centimetres and 61 °C; fifteen seconds at five centimetres and 95 °C. Surface damage increased with temperature, and no cortex damage appeared in any group.

The third result should reorganise a routine. Damage to the *cell membrane complex* — the internal layer between cuticle and cortex — occurred only in the naturally dried group, attributed to prolonged swelling of the wet shaft; the tress took more than two hours to dry at 20 °C, a figure from secondary coverage rather than the abstract.

The authors conclude that dryer use caused more surface damage than natural drying, but that a dryer held at fifteen centimetres and moved continuously caused less. The limits: tresses rather than heads, fixed temperatures and times, shaft changes only.

Which makes the advice odd but specific. The Academy's guidance: let the hair air-dry partially before styling or combing it, and keep heat moderate — flat irons on dry hair, low or medium heat, no more often than every other day, a curling iron held in place for a second or two.

Practical settings keep tools below 185 °C where the hair allows, 180 °C for fine or bleached hair and ideally 160 °C, in single passes. Heat styling breaks fibres; it does not cause shedding from the follicle or a telogen effluvium.

Thermal protectants stand in the same muddy ground. The record names two 2003 papers in the Journal of Cosmetic Science: one an electron-microscopy study of thermal damage at graduated temperatures, the other showing that silicone protectants reduced tensile strength loss in heat-treated hair. Neither was opened for this guide, and the percentages circulating for thermal protection come from commercial explainers.

They help, partially. The 230 °C on the front of the bottle is a marketing threshold rather than a finding: Garnier and Phyto both sell a Réparation 230 °C, claiming an 8.6% reduction in hair surface temperature after one application.

Pulled together, this routine is shorter and rests on firmer ground. Wash at the frequency the scalp and texture require. Condition the lengths, not the roots. Wrap rather than rub, and treat the leave-in as optional. Dry at distance and in motion rather than letting hair sit wet for two hours. The industry's version adds a leave-in, a heat protectant and a microfibre turban to a routine the dermatology literature already calls safe. The load-bearing variable was never the product. It was the clock.
