Poppaea Sabina bathed in donkey's milk, and Pliny the Elder recorded it: a herd of about fifty she-donkeys supplying a daily bath, the milk kept for wrinkles and for keeping the skin smooth and white.

The name on the bottle is Cleopatra's, and no ancient source says so. Frank Ursin's paper in thersites traces the substitution to 1930s cinema, where Claudette Colbert played both queens, then to the 1963 Cleopatra and Carry On Cleo a year later.

What kept the mistake alive is the chemistry, which is real. Unrefrigerated milk sours into lactic acid, an alpha-hydroxy acid, and an alpha-hydroxy acid exfoliates — a link made plainly in a JAMA Dermatology paper called 'Beauty Tips From Ancient Queens'. A true mechanism hung on a casting accident, repeated for ninety years.

That is how the spa bathroom was sold: a plausible mechanism under an image, and a room assembled from a mood. The working version is a specification, and each object is governed by a number with nothing to do with how it photographs — IPX4 and 2.25 metres, Ra 90 and 3000 K, 655 kilograms per cubic metre, 1,008 grams, 150 watts per square metre. The five below are chosen for where they put your hands.

## One — the dedicated bathing mirror with task light

The mirror above the basin is not the bathing mirror. One is set for a face leaning forward over a sink; the other has to serve a face at the waterline and a hand reaching across a shoulder. No standard defines the second height, which is why it is usually wrong.

What the regulation covers is the water. BS 7671 Section 701, 18th Edition, puts Zone 0 inside the bath or tray at IPX7 with SELV limited to 12 V, and Zone 1 above it to 2.25 m at IPX4. Outside the zones, IP44 is a practical minimum for steam, not a legal one. Retail copy calls IP65 the Zone 1 minimum; the rule says IPX4, rising to IPX5 only where jets are likely. Bathroom circuits need 30 mA RCD protection.

Colour rendering matters more than brightness. CRI scores how faithfully a lamp renders object colour against a reference of the same colour temperature. Ra, the common version, dates from 1974 and averages eight samples, R1 to R8. It excludes R9, saturated red, deliberately: a lamp at Ra 90 can still render skin and red lipstick badly.

Trade guidance converges on Ra 90 at a mirror, with 80 treated as acceptable for ambient light. Colour temperature splits the room: 3000 K reads warm and flatters timber and stone, while 4000 K reads crisp and suits small or windowless bathrooms.

Placement is about the face rather than the fixture. Light from the sides, not from a downlight overhead, which drops shadows under the brow, the nose and the chin. Sconces go at roughly 150–170 cm from the floor, 90–100 cm apart, with a bar spanning 65–75% of the mirror width. Those figures are retail rules of thumb.

A demister is a thin electric pad bonded behind the silvering, warming the glass a few degrees so condensation cannot form — a car's heated rear screen, in miniature. Pads draw 20–60 W and clear in two to three minutes, at roughly 150 W/m²: a 600 × 800 mm mirror takes 75 W.

Two constructions are sold. The cheaper heats a rectangular centre zone and leaves the corners fogged; the hotel specification is a film over the full glass. A fog-free mirror starts near £119, and adding a demister costs £30–80. The extractor fan is still required: the pad clears glass, not air.

## Two — the single tray for in-shower oils

A shower tray is where the decorative and the operational versions of this room split. Teak — Tectona grandis — has heartwood the Wood Database rates "very durable" and calls "the gold standard for decay resistance", averaging 655 kg/m³ dried. The natural oils behind that rating leave an unfinished surface faintly greasy to the hand.

The design logic is drainage by construction: slats let water through and air circulate, and adjustable rubber feet lift the legs off a sloping wet-room floor and out of standing water. It is design rationale rather than tested performance, and no standard sets load, drainage or slip resistance for a bath tray.

The material has costs. Teak carries up to 1.4% silica, which dulls a blade, and its density makes a 60 cm shelf a solid object rather than a folded one. Alternatives fail differently: stone is inert and heavy, needing a fixing rated for its load; stainless steel drains and cleans but records every water spot and soap film.

Oil is the older technology, not an addition. Roman bathers oiled themselves and scraped the excess off with a strigil; slaves called *unctores* and *aliptae* did the anointing, and a capsarius carried and guarded his master's towels, oils and strigils. Some baths kept a room for it, the destrictarium or unctorium.

The honest objection is physical. Oil on a wet slatted surface is a slip hazard underfoot; residue mixed with soap leaves a film water alone will not shift; a tray inside the spray turns its drainage gaps into traps for hair and scum unless they are wide and reachable. That is reasoning from materials rather than a cited finding.

## Three — the hook for the bath sheet

A bath sheet is a weight before it is a luxury. GSM means grams per square metre; retail towels sit mostly at 400–600, with 700 sold as premium heavyweight. A 90 × 160 cm sheet at 700 GSM weighs about 1,008 g dry, a 70 × 140 cm bath towel about 686 g. That arithmetic is what a hook has to hold.

The bath sheet is not a large bath towel by accident. One comparison gives 0.98 m² for a bath towel against 1.44 m² for a sheet — about 47% more fabric and about 47% more water to dry. Sizes are not standardised: a US sheet is commonly 40 × 70 in, a UK one 90 × 150 to 100 × 200 cm.

Here the luxury number turns against itself. Kemet Cotton states that it stepped its Reserve line down from 800 GSM to 700 because 800-gram pieces dried too slowly in home bathrooms. The hotel weight assumes hotel laundry. A 700 GSM sheet is a domestic object carrying a specification written for a machine you do not own.

A hook holds the sheet bunched, so the inner folds stay wet longest and the load concentrates at one point of the fabric; a bar spreads it. No published capacity exists for a domestic robe hook — they are sold by finish, not rating — and the fixing, plasterboard against a stud or masonry, is the weak link.

Trade guidance on wet-room metal is consistent: solid brass, AISI 304 stainless, 316 for coastal or chlorinated settings, and powder-coated steel, with chrome-plated zinc alloy the grade that pits and flakes first. The heated rail arrived in the 1920s as a "bath radiator" for grand hotels before reaching ordinary bathrooms mid-century. A hook dries by air around a hanging edge, a rail by contact heat, and only the rail needs wiring and an RCD.

## Four — the stool for the in-bath treatments

The Japanese bathing stool is *koshikake* — literally a low seat. In a sento or onsen the pool is for soaking, not washing: bathers wash and rinse seated at a low tap before entering, so the shared water stays clean. The stool exists because of the order of operations, not because sitting is comfortable.

Sitting also produces a smaller splash zone than standing, the stated rationale for Wataru Kumano's WK Bath Stool, made by Karimoku, whose seat is inclined eight degrees so water runs off and which is built from six parts. Traditional versions are hinoki, Japanese cypress; the Oke-ei workshop in Tokyo, founded in 1888, still uses cypress up to 300 years old.

The Roman bath had no such furniture. Seating was built: the apodyterium carried stone seats along three walls with holes above them, probably for clothing pegs, and three bronze benches were found in the tepidarium at Pompeii beside a brazier. The sequence ran apodyterium, tepidarium, caldarium, frigidarium, with oil and strigil instead of soap. In that tradition, something to sit on is architecture.

An outsider confirms the object by 1906: a Salt Lake Tribune piece of 14 September describes a Japanese bathroom with "a stool to sit upon when washing". The reason a modern bath wants one is task-specific — foot care, shaving legs, hair masks are two-handed jobs below the waterline, where balance on wet enamel is the limit. No rated capacity exists for teak or hinoki stools; the joint and the floor are the constraint.

## Five — the stack of three towels

A stack of three towels is a rotation, not a display. GSM is the number the trade sells weight with: 400–600 is the ordinary band, 700 is premium heavyweight, and hotel towels are commonly specified at 400–700. Heavier means more cotton per square metre, which is not the same as more absorbency.

The size ladder is three objects with three jobs. US: bath sheet 40 × 70 in, bath towel 27 × 54, hand towel 16 × 30. UK: sheet 90–100 × 150–180 cm, bath towel 70 × 130–140, hand towel 50 × 85–90. Face cloths are 30–33 cm square. At 700 GSM the three weigh 1,008 g, 686 g and 315 g — roughly 2 kg of dry cotton, before water.

The rotation works only if one towel dries while another is in use and a third is in the wash, which means drying time has to be shorter than the interval between uses. That is precisely what 700 GSM undermines, for the reason the manufacturer gave when it stepped its Reserve line down.

Pile construction matters as much as weight: terry loop, low-twist — softer, bulkier, slower to dry — and waffle, thin, fast-drying, less absorbent. GSM cannot tell them apart. Lint is the other laundering fact: loosely bound surface fibres shed for the first several washes, and heavier or low-twist towels shed more. Wash at 60 °C, skip softener, do not overload, dry promptly.

The Roman bath put the body on architecture, and the Japanese bath put it on a stool. Poppaea had fifty donkeys and a rumour; you have a zone line at 2.25 metres, a sheet that weighs 1,008 grams dry, and five objects whose heights were set by an arm rather than an eye. A photograph keeps the mood. The skin keeps the numbers.
