The Victoria and Albert Museum's toilet mirror, English, 1700–1710, is walnut and burr-walnut veneer over oak and pine, thirty-two and a half inches tall and eighteen wide, with brass fittings and an ivory escutcheon. The catalogue calls it furniture. It is easier to read as a lighting instrument with a frame around it.

No mirror has ever made light. It is the light's auditor, and the honesty of its report has always belonged to the fuel rather than the glass. Three received dates fail the record: an 1830s patent for the triple mirror, a 1930s mass-market bulb ring, a 1990s fluorescent boom.

## 1700s — the candle and the hand mirror

The V&A's record supplies the vocabulary. In the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries a small freestanding dressing-table mirror was a *toilet glass* — "toilet" meaning the dressing table and its accessories. From the late eighteenth century it was a *dressing glass*. Hepplewhite's Cabinet-Maker and Upholsterer's Guide, published in 1788 and reissued in 1789 and 1794, showed several.

Such a glass rarely travelled alone. A toilet service ran to twenty or thirty pieces: a large table mirror on an easel back, hand mirrors, covered bowls and jars, brushes, a pin cushion, in silver or silver-gilt. The Met holds a set of 1687–88; the Ashmolean's Acton service, London, 1699–1700, survives in fourteen pieces.

Benjamin Pyne's thirty-four-piece silver-gilt service of 1708, made for Maria Howard, Duchess of Norfolk, is the grandest survivor. It sold for £1,380,000, an auction figure rather than a period price, and its export was deferred in 2012 under a V&A-backed licence.

The glass was the expensive part. Versailles shows the scale: the Galerie des Glaces, 73 metres long and 357 mirrors across 17 arches, was glazed between 1678 and 1684 by a manufacture Colbert founded in 1665 to break the Venetian monopoly. English plates stayed small, cut from cylinders that left parallel waves; the improved method split the cooled cylinder, reheated it and opened it on a polished slab called a *lagre*.

What the sitter saw by was a candle at roughly 1,800 K, with almost no blue in it. The eye white-balances the flame; skin and white powder do not, and read amber. Tallow burned badly and smelled rancid; beeswax was reserved for church and the upper classes; a household got through 300 to 400 candles a year. Only the richest lit whole rooms.

## 1830s — the triple mirror

The form this section is supposed to patent is a triptych: a central plate flanked by two hinged wings. Dealers trace it to religious altarpieces adapted into domestic furniture across the Regency, Victorian and Edwardian periods. The period trade term was *toilet glass* or *dressing glass*; "triple mirror" is a later label.

No patent for a triple or folding dressing mirror in the 1830s exists in any record consulted. British patent specifications were not numbered until 1852, and the Old Law patents before that are unnumbered and sparsely indexed. An 1830s patent claim should be treated as unsupported, not as a lost document.

What 1835 did produce was Justus von Liebig's observation that heating aldehyde with an ammoniacal solution of silver nitrate deposits a thin metallic film on glass. It was not a patent and was not widely adopted in his lifetime. Thomas Drayton's 1843 process using oil of cloves failed commercially; mercury amalgam stayed standard until safety legislation prohibited it.

The documentary record for the year is Loudon's Encyclopaedia of Cottage, Farm and Villa Architecture and Furniture, 1833. It illustrates dressing glasses at page 343, figure 714, a "plateau glass", and at page 1085, figure 1992, a villa dressing-glass — both Grecian-style reeded scroll-arm toilet glasses.

The economics explain the shape. Early plates were deliberately small because mirror glass was expensive, so three small plates were a cheaper route to a wide view than one large one. A Victorian mahogany toilet mirror with three hinged compartments measures 68 by 32 cm and stands 85 cm high; an Edwardian triple-plate example ran 69 by 90 cm.

## 1930s — the bulb-lit Hollywood mirror

Edison's carbon filament first burned for about 40 to 45 hours on 21 October 1879. He applied for the patent on 4 November and received it on 27 January 1880, U.S. patent 223,898. The advantage was the high vacuum and a 110-volt parallel distribution system, which turned the lamp into a product.

Household incandescent bulbs run near 2,800 K, against 1,800 K for candle and 5,600 K for daylight. The bulb did not fix colour; it moved the reference point.

The earliest documented lit dressing-table mirror is in Screenland, in the November 1928–April 1929 issue run. It credits Corinne Griffith, in the dressing room of her Burbank studio bungalow, with a mirror lit from underneath by incandescent bulbs of the kind used on studio sets, so she could tell how her makeup would photograph. The index survives; the page text was not opened, so treat the detail as attributed.

A 1936–37 Motion Picture article describes the standard portable studio dressing room as furnished with "a couch, a dressing table and a mirror". The baseline was unlit. No period source gives a standard 1930s bulb count; the numbers on modern replicas are product specifications, not documentation. Claims that stars demanded lit mirrors in contracts, or that studios installed them as standard, appear only in unsourced retailer copy.

The ring survives because of geometry, not glamour. A head tapers below the cheekbones, so light has to arrive at face level from both sides; a single source above the mirror lights forehead and crown and drops shadow under the eyes, nose and chin. A circle of bulbs approximates that arrangement. A lighting designer reaches the same place with two sconces at eye height.

## 1968–1990s — the fluorescent-lit vanity

Clairol's True-To-Light, introduced around 1968 and sold through the 1970s and 1980s, is the ancestor the decade label hides. Four settings — Day, Office, Evening, Home — came from coloured plastic slats and filters over linear fluorescent tubes. A consumer mirror had made "which light are you dressing for?" a physical control fifty years before the LED colour-temperature button.

The hardware was ordinary. Models LM-7, LM-8/VIII and V took replaceable linear T5 tubes — the F6T5TTL, 6 W, G5 bi-pin — behind a swivel mirror, plain one side and magnified the other. Clairol's design patent USD261817, for a make-up mirror by Horst J. Kretschmer, was filed on 12 July 1978, granted on 10 November 1981, and cites a Merchandising Week advertisement of 13 January 1975.

The light was the problem, and only sometimes. Standard halophosphate tubes, the cool-white workhorse since 1938, score roughly CRI 50 to 60, with R9 negative: the phosphor blends blue and orange and is short of red, which is why skin was said to look unhealthy. Rare-earth triphosphor tubes, from Philips in the late 1970s, reached Ra 80 to 88 — so by the 1990s a good fluorescent vanity had them, and the bad ones were a procurement decision.

Placement did the rest. A single fluorescent strip above a mirror lights the forehead and crown and drops shadow under the eyes, nose and chin; the lighting designer Randall Whitehead is quoted saying it can visually age you by 10 years. JLC's "Bathroom Lighting Basics" ranks it the worst arrangement. The correction is two sconces at eye level either side of the mirror, 36 to 40 inches apart, slightly off its edge.

The fluorescent mirror also had a commercial life away from the bedroom. Jerdon dates to 1977, not 1964, and built its business on halo-lighted two-sided swivel mirrors in 1x/5x and higher magnifications for Marriott, Hilton and Ritz-Carlton. That is the fluorescent mirror as hotel fixture: wall-mounted, immovable, and specified by someone who was not the user.

The bulb ring returned later than the decade story allows. Vanity Girl Hollywood was founded in 2008 by Maxine Tatlonghari in West Hollywood, manufactured in Los Angeles through Versa Products, supplied the 2010 Olympics and sold through celebrity placement. The commercial category that sells bulb rings today is younger than the fluorescent vanity it supposedly replaced.

## 2010s — the backlit LED

Saint-Gobain Glass France filed priority FR1057351 on 15 September 2010 for an illuminating mirror panel comprising LEDs, published as CN103209619B. The design couples LEDs into the edge, or thickness, of a glass sheet, with a reflecting layer in the mirror zone and a scattering surround. It is the foundational edge-lit architecture, from a glass company rather than a cosmetics one.

Séura's US9,765,958B2, filed in 2015, took the other branch: backlighting through a translucent portion of silvered mirror, with a reflective element to boost output. The company markets patented copper-free mirror coatings against black-edge de-silvering, and "5 mm copper-free silver mirror" is now standard in Chinese OEM listings, alongside touch dimmer, defogger and IP44 rating.

simplehuman, founded in 2000 by Frank Yang, arrived from the beauty-accessory side. Its original Sensor Mirror, around 2012–13, used the *tru-lux* light system at roughly 550–600 lux and CRI around 90, activated by sensor, cordless and USB-rechargeable. The full-size model cost $200; the Mini cost $130, offered 10x magnification and ran about eight weeks per charge.

By CES 2019 the mirror had a colour-temperature menu: the Sensor Mirror Hi-Fi at $350 and the Hi-Fi Assist with Google Assistant at $400, with night-shift and candlelight modes. The specification that matters is narrower than the brochure. ANSI C78.377 sets colour-temperature bins and no CRI minimum; CIE 13.3 defines colour rendering separately. A workable mirror is Ra 90 or better with R9 listed, adjustable across 2,700 to 4,000 K, at 3 to 4 SDCM.

The market followed. On Tmall, LED makeup mirror sales doubled for three consecutive years to 2020, with about seven million buyers against 300 million beauty consumers in China. Electra Mirror's commercial sheet reads "Signature LEDs 90+ CRI, 600+ lumens/ft, 3000 K standard". Magnification is a property of curvature, not lighting — 1x/5x and 1x/10x sit beside the light specification, not inside it.

The frame is the least documentary part of the object. What a buyer is actually choosing is a claim about how truly a face will be shown: CRI, R9, colour temperature, SDCM, lumen output. Those numbers mean nothing outside that claim, which is why the mirror rewards the same reading as a light fitting.

Two lineages met in the 2010s — the lighting industry's edge-lit panel, dated from Saint-Gobain's 2010 priority, and the beauty-accessory industry's magnification, USB port and $200 price tag. The result is a lighting fixture re-categorised as a beauty product. It is still not the light source. It is still the auditor, and it is now sold with a menu of what it should audit.
