The sheet mask is, in our view, a technology. The sheet mask is, in our view, also the technology the modern skin care market most often mistakes for a *category*. The category mistake is, in our view, mostly a matter of the brand's marketing — the brand wants the consumer to believe the sheet mask is, in formulation terms, a unique kind of product, a product that requires, in our view, a sheet-mask-specific routine, a sheet-mask-specific shelf, a sheet-mask-specific *addiction*. The technology is, in formulation terms, finally, just a delivery system.

The delivery system is, in our view, also a technology with a long history. The history is, in our view, mostly the history of *wound care*. The technology that became, in the late twentieth century, the K-beauty sheet mask was, in the early twentieth century, the technology of the *occlusive dressing* — the dressing that, in medical terms, keeps a wound moist, keeps a wound protected, keeps a wound in the conditions the wound needs to heal. The sheet mask is, in formulation terms, finally, an occlusive dressing for the face. The face is, in our view, finally, the wound.

This essay is, in part, a history of the technology. It is also, in part, an argument that the sheet mask, in 2026, has, in our view, become the skin care product most over-marketed and most under-understood. The under-understanding is, in our view, also the reason the modern consumer buys the sheet mask for the *experience* and not, in most cases, for the *work* the mask is, in formulation terms, doing.

## 1973 — Shiseido and the first sheet mask

The sheet mask, in the modern sense, begins in Japan in 1973. The mask is, in this period, a product of Shiseido's research labs. The mask is, in formulation terms, a sheet of non-woven fabric, pre-soaked in a serum, packaged in a single-use sachet. The mask is, in this period, sold through Japanese department stores, as a *single-use treatment* — a treatment the consumer was, in most cases, expected to use after a long flight, or before a meeting, or as a one-off indulgence.

The Shiseido mask is, in our view, also a product of its time. The product of its time is, in most cases, a product of the *masculine professional* — the Japanese businessman, the Japanese salaryman, the man who, in 1973, was the consumer the department stores most wanted to reach. The sheet mask was, in this period, marketed as a product that would, in formulation terms, *restore* the face the long flight had, in formulation terms, dehydrated. The restoration is, in our view, finally, the work the sheet mask was designed for.

The Shiseido mask is, in our view, also a product that did not, in 1973, become a global hit. The Shiseido mask was, in most cases, sold only in Japan. The Shiseido mask was, in most cases, available only through department stores. The Shiseido mask was, in most cases, finally, a product the rest of the world did not, in 1973, know about.

## 1990s — the Korean adoption

The Korean adoption of the sheet mask begins, in our view, in the late 1990s. The Korean skin care industry, in the late 1990s, was, in our view, going through a period of *technological maturity*. The Korean industry was, in this period, building on the *Shiseido playbook* — the playbook of the single-use treatment, the pre-soaked sheet, the packaging that, in our view, allowed the consumer to use the product without, in most cases, having to measure, or to mix, or to wait.

The Korean sheet mask is, in our view, also a product of the *Korean beauty routine*. The Korean routine, in this period, is, in our view, the routine that *layers* — the routine that has, in most cases, ten steps, ten products, ten minutes. The sheet mask, in the Korean routine, is, in our view, the step that comes *between* the essence and the cream. The mask is, in this period, the step that delivers, in formulation terms, a *single concentrated dose* of active ingredients to the skin, in a vehicle the skin is, in most cases, ready to absorb.

The Korean sheet mask is, in our view, also a product of the *Korean retail*. The Korean retail, in this period, was, in our view, the retail that was, in most cases, full of *single-use sachets*. The sachet was, in our view, the format that allowed the consumer to *try* the product without, in most cases, committing to a full-size jar. The trying is, in our view, finally, the work the sachet was doing for the Korean market.

## 2010s — the K-beauty export

The K-beauty export of the sheet mask begins, in our view, in the early 2010s. The export was, in our view, mostly a function of *social media*. The social media, in this period, was, in our view, mostly Instagram. The Instagram was, in our view, mostly the platform that allowed the consumer to *see* the mask on the face. The seeing is, in our view, also the moment the sheet mask became, in the Western market, an *object* — an object to be photographed, an object to be posted, an object to be, in most cases, identified by.

The export was, in our view, also a function of *celebrity*. The celebrities, in this period, began posting photos of themselves with sheet masks on. The posting was, in our view, mostly a function of the *flight*. The celebrities, in this period, were, in most cases, flying long-haul. The long-haul flight is, in our view, the moment the skin is, in formulation terms, most dehydrated. The mask is, in this period, the product the celebrities were, in most cases, photographed wearing on the plane.

The export was, in our view, also a function of *price*. The Korean sheet mask was, in this period, cheap. The cheapness was, in formulation terms, a function of the *Korean manufacturing* — the manufacturing that, in our view, allowed the Korean brand to produce the mask at a price the Western consumer was, in most cases, willing to pay. The paying is, in our view, finally, the work the export was doing for the Korean market.

## 2015 — the sheet mask boom

The sheet mask boom, in the Western market, peaks, in our view, in 2015. The boom is, in our view, mostly a function of *variety*. The variety was, in this period, the variety of *brands* — Korean, Japanese, American, French, British, every brand launching a sheet mask, every brand launching a *sheet-mask flavour*. The flavours were, in this period, mostly ingredients — the green tea mask, the cucumber mask, the honey mask, the snail mask, the bird's nest mask, the gold mask, the charcoal mask.

The boom is, in our view, also a function of *subscription*. The subscription services — the Birchboxes, the Ipsy bags, the Glossyboxes — were, in this period, sending the consumer a sheet mask every month. The monthly sheet mask was, in our view, finally, the format that, in most cases, normalised the sheet mask as part of the Western routine. The normalisation is, in our view, finally, the work the subscription was doing for the sheet mask.

The boom is, in our view, finally, a function of *Instagram*. The Instagram was, in this period, the platform the consumer used to *show* the sheet mask. The showing was, in our view, mostly a function of the *look* — the look of the mask on the face, the look of the white cotton on the cheek, the look of the sheet, in most cases, *dripping*. The dripping was, in our view, finally, the visual signifier the Instagram consumer most wanted to project.

## 2020s — the pared-back mask

The sheet mask, in 2026, has, in our view, settled into a more modest place on the skin care shelf. The modest place is, in our view, mostly a function of *scepticism*. The consumer has, in the last five years, become sceptical of the boom — the scepticism is, in our view, mostly a function of the *ingredient*. The consumer has, in most cases, realised that the active ingredients in the bird's nest mask are, in formulation terms, not, in any meaningful sense, *bird's nest*. The consumer has, in most cases, realised that the active ingredients in the gold mask are, in formulation terms, not, in any meaningful sense, *gold*.

The pared-back mask is, in our view, also a function of *sustainability*. The consumer has, in most cases, become conscious of the *waste* — the waste of the single-use sachet, the waste of the fabric sheet, the waste of the packaging that, in most cases, cannot be recycled. The waste is, in our view, finally, the loss the modern consumer most often does not realise she is incurring.

The pared-back mask is, in our view, also a function of *formulation*. The formulation of the modern sheet mask is, in our view, mostly a function of the *serum* — the serum that, in formulation terms, the mask is, in most cases, mostly delivering. The serum, in the modern mask, has, in our view, become the work. The fabric has, in our view, become, mostly, the *delivery*.

---

The sheet mask, in sum, is a technology the modern market has, in our view, finally, stopped over-marketing. The technology is, in our view, also the technology the modern consumer has, in our view, finally, stopped buying for the *experience*. The consumer is, in 2026, buying the sheet mask, in our view, mostly for the *work* the mask is doing. The work is, in formulation terms, finally, the work of the *serum*.

The serum, in the modern mask, has, in our view, become the work. The fabric has, in our view, become the *vehicle*. The vehicle is, in our view, finally, a vehicle that, in 2026, the consumer has, in our view, mostly learned to *evaluate* — to ask what the serum is, to ask what the serum does, to ask what the serum is, in formulation terms, worth.

The asking is, in our view, finally, the work. The work is, in our view, finally, what the sheet mask is for. The sheet mask is, in our view, finally, also the technology the modern consumer has, in our view, mostly learned to *use* — to apply the mask on damp skin, to leave the mask for fifteen minutes, to remove the mask without, in most cases, rinsing, to follow the mask with the moisturiser the consumer has, in our view, mostly already decided to use.

The using is, in our view, finally, the work. The work is, in our view, finally, what the sheet mask is for.
