The object arrives in a foil pouch smaller than a paperback, folded twice, wet. Inside is a sheet of non-woven fabric cut into the approximate shape of a face, and behind it a film that has to be peeled away before the thing will lie flat.

It is the only skincare product that is also a photograph. That is not a sneer. A serum can be described and a cream can be swatched; only the mask puts a face inside a frame, white and eyeless, and asks to be looked at for the fifteen or twenty minutes it stays on.

The story usually told about it is Korean and recent — a K-beauty export that reached Western bathrooms around 2012 and became a category by 2015. The sequence is roughly right. The geography is not.

The modern format is Japanese. That is the part the export story skips: the sheet was a Japanese product through the 1990s, and the Korean contribution came later, from the road shop rather than the laboratory. Behind the format sit a medical patent from 1875 and an industrial fabric from the 1960s.

## 1875–1993 — from medical dressing to the first modern sheet

Start with the claim that will not die: that Shiseido invented the sheet mask in 1973. It survives in English summaries and in brand copy. Shiseido's own corporate history begins in 1872, with Japan's first Western-style pharmacy, and its brand timeline runs 1959, 1977, 1980, 1985 — beauty academy, Paris Six sponsorship, Serge Lutens, the FIT exhibition — with no 1973 entry and no mask of any kind.

Japanese Wikipedia is no help to the myth either. Its Shiseido timeline jumps from the 1972 centenary, when the men's brand RODOS launched, to the 1980 French joint venture with Pierre Fabre. The word for sheet mask does not appear at all.

What does exist is older and less glamorous. US patent 165,955, "Improvement in masks for medical purposes," was issued to Helen Rowley of Ohio on 27 July 1875. It covers a device for the face, and it is a medical one. The format also needed a fabric that did not yet exist: spunlace, the process behind a soft, wet-strength non-woven, was commercialised in the 1960s and 1970s, while bonded non-wovens had scaled in the 1940s and 1950s for other industries.

Trade histories put the earliest pre-cut, face-shaped sheet — soaked in essence, sold in a sealed pouch — in 1980s Japan, among prestige brands, though the same accounts concede that no inventor is documented. The first version that can be pinned to a year is SK-II's, in 1993. Pinguan, the Chinese beauty-industry outlet, calls it the progenitor of the modern sheet mask; SK-II brought it to mainland China in 1998, and Olay followed around 2002.

A 1970s SK-II mask would be chronologically impossible in any case. The brand dates to 1980 at the earliest, and Procter & Gamble acquired it only through the 1991 purchase of Max Factor. The real 1970s Japanese story attached to SK-II is an ingredient: sake-brewery workers with unusually youthful hands, and the yeast extract sold as *Pitera*.

## 1990s — the Korean adoption

Korea came to the format later, and lower, than the export story implies. Through the 1990s the Korean market ran on gauze-type packs and peel-off packs; the essence-soaked sheet was not yet the standard object. What masks existed were poor: serum-coated non-woven, associated with actresses, handed out free on Seoul streets — the South China Morning Post recorded the detail in June 2019.

The category stayed niche into the early 2000s. Korean companies then introduced the essence-soaked sheet properly, creating the peel-and-apply product the rest of the world would later recognise. Mass adoption ran through road shops and through arithmetic: Nature Republic and Innisfree used 1+1 and 10+10 promotions to make a mask an everyday purchase. Innisfree, launched by Amorepacific in 2000, supplied the volume.

The retail layer thickened quickly. The Face Shop opened its first store in Myeongdong in December 2003 and expanded by franchising. Laneige had put the Water Sleeping Pack on shelf in 2002, an overnight leave-on step that made the routine feel manageable. Jenic, founded after the 1997 currency crisis by Yoo Hyun-oh, developed Korea's first water-soluble hydrogel; its Ha Yumi Pack made the company known.

Hydrogel was adopted to fix what the sheet did badly — fit, dryness, convenience — and it shifted consumer attitudes to the category. By 2010 Jenic held about 30% of the Korean mask-pack market, with 81.8 billion won in sales and 12.2 billion won in operating profit, roughly double and quadruple the year before. None of this was export-led: South Korea became a net exporter of beauty products only in 2014.

## 2010s — the K-beauty export

The export, when it arrived, was an American retail story before it was a Korean one. In April 2011 two BB creams from Dr. Jart+ reached Sephora, the first products of their kind sold in the United States. 2014 was the first year South Korea exported more beauty than it imported — US$130 million of it to the United States alone — and in 2015 that American figure grew 59%, to US$207 million.

A specialist e-commerce layer carried the masks. Wishtrend launched in 2011, export-only, and sold about 200,000 units in the US by 2014; Soko Glam and Peach & Lily followed in 2012, Glow Recipe in 2014. Urban Outfitters stocked Tony Moly, which put a mask beside a hoodie.

This wave was mediated by bloggers more than by advertising. Euromonitor cites YouTube, blogs and Instagram, and singles out Fifty Shades of Snail — started in 2012, just over 6,000 Instagram followers as of March 2016 — as the sort of account that moved product. K-pop supplied the bridge from fandom to beauty: Super Junior modelled for Tony Moly, BTS for MediHeal.

China was the largest market of the period. Face-mask sales there reached US$2.85 billion in 2016, with double-digit growth since 2012, and Mintel forecast US$5.91 billion by 2021. L'Oréal paid US$845 million for the Chinese mask brand Magic Holdings in 2014; Estée Lauder bought GlamGlow the same year.

## 2015 — the sheet mask boom

The category's own numbers are smaller than the noise around it. The global sheet-mask market was valued at US$160.4 million in 2015 and forecast to reach US$336.7 million by the end of 2024, with Asia-Pacific accounting for 37.9% of sales.

It was fragmented rather than consolidated. Sephora Collection, Starskin and Estée Lauder held a combined 36.5% share in 2015, and one of the three was a retailer's own label. In the US, face masks grew 11% in current value that year, against 2% for skincare overall.

Euromonitor attributes the acceleration to the *sheet mask selfie* on Instagram, Facebook and Twitter, and names Khloe Kardashian as a significant populariser. Google searches for the term more than doubled between January 2015 and January 2016. Note what is absent from the explanation: a formulation breakthrough.

The boom was visible a year early, too. US prestige mask sales rose 60% from 2013 to 2014, to US$65.5 million. What changed after 2015 was the material — hydrogel mid-decade, bio-cellulose forecast at about 8.8% CAGR, foil-backed and two-layer formats later — not the serum. One mask cost roughly US$1 in a Myeongdong multi-pack bin in the 2000s, and roughly US$1 at entry level by 2019.

## 2020s — the pared-back mask

The retreat the 2020s are supposed to represent is a discourse, not a market. Grand View Research puts the global sheet mask market at US$363.7 million in 2024, US$429.6 million in 2026 and US$605.7 million by 2030, an 8.9% compound annual rate for 2025 to 2030.

The geography has been rewritten more than reduced. North America held the largest regional share in 2024, at 37.2% of revenue, while Asia-Pacific is forecast to grow fastest, at 9.9%. The 2015 estimate had Asia-Pacific leading at 37.9% — different methodologies, so read the shift as directional. Substrate competition, meanwhile, has moved to sustainability: cotton was the largest product segment in 2024 at 39.4%, and bio-cellulose the fastest-growing at 10.6%, cited for biodegradability.

The dermatological verdict is narrower than either camp would like. A mask is an *occlusive delivery system* — it slows evaporation and produces measurable hydration and surface smoothness for several hours, not structural change. Fifteen to twenty minutes, Dr. Jane Yoo writes, is not long enough, and the active concentrations are not high enough, to stimulate collagen, improve pigmentation or rebuild the barrier.

There is a ceiling, and it sits close to the recommended time. Normal stratum corneum water content runs 10% to 30%; above 35% the barrier begins to suffer, and occlusion for as little as 30 minutes can push healthy skin into that range. Wear beyond 25 minutes is associated with hydration injury.

Misuse is now a clinical presentation. Wang et al., writing in the Journal of Tissue Viability in 2024, link extended, social-media-driven wear to rosacea, irritant reactions and allergic contact dermatitis, because prolonged occlusion also drives fragrance, essential oils, alcohol and acids into the skin. The counterweight is real: Yang et al., in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology in 2025, found a 28-day regimen produced significant immediate TEWL reduction and improved hydration, elasticity and firmness at days 14 and 28.

Which leaves the cheaper substitute. Slugging — petrolatum-style occlusion — reduces TEWL at a fraction of the cost, and reframes the sheet mask as slugging in fancier packaging. The environmental case cannot be recycled away either. A typical mask is three components: the mask, an aluminium-plastic laminate pouch that cannot be recycled, and sometimes an inner plastic backing. The fabric blends nylon, polyester and plastic microfibers that will not compost.

Biodegradable claims are fragile. Biocellulose or bamboo coated in a silicone-heavy serum may not break down, silicone film is described as bioaccumulative, and discarded masks can release methane as they degrade. Meanwhile the innovation axis has moved off the sheet: IOPE showed an app-measured Tailored 3D Mask at CES 2020, and a Korean regulatory change effective 14 March 2020 permitted conditional in-store custom manufacturing.

Capacity is expanding, not contracting. The ODM Lee Miin went from 60.5 billion won in 2021 to 121.7 billion won in 2024, and is building a second plant to raise output from 230 million to 500 million units a year by 2027.

That gap is the thing to hold. Dermatology columns and sustainability pages describe a retreat the sales data does not show. A device calibrated to be photographed and removed, in a window too short to do what the copy claims, keeps selling more each year — which is what a photograph does, and what a treatment rarely manages.
