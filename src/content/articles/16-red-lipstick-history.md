The Guinness World Records entry on the most toxic makeup in history is short and unglamorous. Venetian ceruse, it explains, was white lead carbonate mixed with vinegar, sold from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century as "spirits of Saturn". Then comes the sentence that undoes a century of retellings: no evidence exists that Elizabeth I used it; it appears nowhere in the royal account books.

The legend is not the history, but it shows how this history gets written. The red lip is better documented as a sign than as a substance: the poisoning story has no paper trail, while the colour itself has purchase orders, patents and case reports.

The container is where the modern red begins. Before 1915, lip colour was a paste in a pot, applied with a brush or carried on impregnated paper. Maurice Levy patented a metal tube that year; James Bruce Mason Jr. patented the swivel-up in 1923. Within eight years, a dressing-table ritual became an object you could check in a handbag.

## Elizabethan — cochineal and white lead

Ceruse was not the portraits' plaster mask. Guinness calls its effect "more akin to a modern concealer" than to heavy whiteface. The lead was real, though: a median lethal dose of around 5 grams per kilogram of bodyweight, with 350 grams described as deadly for a typical adult.

Chronic exposure was slower and uglier — loss of hair and teeth, early cognitive decline. But the substance at the centre of the red lip was not lead. It was an insect. Cochineal is the dried, crushed female of *Dactylopius coccus*, a scale insect farmed on prickly pear in Mexico.

It reached Europe through the Spanish conquest of the Aztec empire. By the 1570s cochineal was Spain's second most valuable export from New Spain after silver. Viceroy Enríquez wrote to the King on 22 September 1572 placing grana second only to silver, and Mexico produced roughly 175,000 pounds a year by 1575, a figure that doubled over the next twenty-five years.

Vermilion, mercury sulfide made by melting and subliming mercury with sulphur, was the other red: a moderately priced apothecary commodity by the sixteenth century, used as rouge and lip colour. Recipe books give red fucus from madder, cochineal and ochre, with vermilion the popular and toxic one.

The National Portrait Gallery hedges the famous salve: Elizabeth "possibly" used one of cochineal, gum arabic, egg white and fig milk. Note the adverb, and note that rosewater, added in retellings, is absent from the standard version. Sumptuary law kept scarlet for aristocrats and forbade peasants to wear the colour.

## 1920 — the modern lipstick and the flapper

The tube is often said to have been turned from First World War shell casings; it wasn't. Levy's manufacturer also made ammunition cases, which is where the story comes from. The tube moved colour out of the dressing table and into the handbag. One history calls lipstick "an emblem of the New Woman" and a "badge" worn "in a deliberate and successful attempt to shock their elders".

The same scholarship traces the colour to the suffrage campaign, where red lipstick was a solidarity symbol through the passage of the Nineteenth Amendment. A red mouth was a position before it was a look. In October 1926 the New York Times reported Americans spent "millions every day" on cosmetics, making beauty a billion-dollar industry and the fourth largest in the country, after cars, movies and bootleg liquor.

By 27 August 1929 the paper was calculating annual lipstick output against 20 million "women of lipstick age", defined as fourteen to sixty-four. Then came the chemistry that made the red stay on. "Indelible" lipsticks were built on bromo acid dyes — halogenated fluoresceins of the eosin family, notably D&C Red 21 and D&C Red 27.

Their staining power depended on castor oil, whose ricinoleic acid uniquely solvates them. The same dyes left a medical record: Zakon, Goldberg and Kahn reported 32 cases of lipstick cheilitis in 1947, in women aged seventeen to fifty-four; symptoms cleared in about two weeks on non-indelible dyes.

## 1940 — wartime red

The Women Marines' lipstick was a uniform item. The Marine Corps Women's Reserve was established in 1942; almost 22,000 women served by the war's end, under dress standards sometimes stricter than the men's — a woman was five times more likely to receive a non-judicial punishment for a dress-code violation. Lt. Col. Ruth Streeter secured a $3,000 budget from Elizabeth Arden with help from Eleanor Roosevelt.

Arden's "Vibrant Red" was formulated to match the red piping and chevrons of the spruce-green winter uniform. Renamed "Montezuma Red" in 1942, it became the standard-issue lipstick in the Women Marines' make-up kit, with a matching cream rouge and nail polish.

The kit came with instructions. Recruits were expected to spend no more than two minutes applying make-up, and a replacement cost $2.27 at the PX. Failure to apply it "efficiently and adequately" carried punishment: Private Bernice Frankel, later known as Bea Arthur, received two non-judicial punishments in 1943.

Helena Rubinstein made "Regimental Red" in 1941, then "Fighting Red", "Commando" and "Jeep Red"; Arden released "Victory Red", and advertising supplied the line "beauty is your duty". The War Production Board called cosmetic materials "nonessential" in 1942 but let existing products continue; Arden's kit was an exception. Metal went to war production, so Tangee moved to plastic cases in 1943 and back to metal in 1946.

## 1960 — the nude

The 1960s nude was a technique before it was a shade. The mod look of about 1966, associated with Twiggy, paired heavy black eyeliner and painted lower lashes with a pale lip so the eyes carried the face. Women got there by applying foundation or pancake make-up over the lips, or by using a white lipstick.

"Chalk white" lipstick and eyeshadow originated in Italy in early 1959, according to the Fashion and Merchandising Fads reference work. Blended with conventional colours, it made a pale highlight, lost its novelty by 1960 and resurged later in the decade. In Britain, Yardley's "The London Look", fronted by Jean Shrimpton, was the commercial face of the chalky pale lip.

The finishes were materials: frosted and pearlised lipsticks used mica, silica and bismuth oxychloride. The shade itself has no inventor. No single patent or named creator for "the nude lipstick" could be found; the record shows a trend in shade ranges and application technique, not an invention. The absence of a date is the finding.

## 1980 — the power red

Begin with a correction. Revlon's "Fire and Ice" was first distributed in 1952, and the campaign was a two-page spread photographed by Richard Avedon, with Dorian Leigh in a silver-sequin dress and a scarlet Balenciaga-cape copy, headlined "Are You Made for Fire and Ice?"

Its centrepiece was a 15-question quiz; at least eight yeses out of fifteen made a woman "made of Fire and Ice". The questions included "Have you ever danced with your shoes off?", "Do sables excite you, even on other women?" and "If tourist flights were running, would you take a trip to Mars?" Kay Daly wrote the script and the questionnaire, and later Chanel No. 5's "Every woman alive loves Chanel No. 5".

The red actually worn in the 1980s is better documented in advertising than in sales. A 1985 Chanel campaign under Karl Lagerfeld ran as "New from Paris. Turbulent, seductive colours for lips and nails", pairing deep red lipstick and nail polish with the decade's shoulder-padded suit. Claude Montana and Thierry Mugler supplied the shoulder pads; Dynasty and Dallas the mood. Madonna's MAC "Russian Red" belongs to 1990.

## 2010 — the your-lips-but-red backlash

The phrase "your lips but better" was not coined by Clinique. The New York Times, in June 2021, credited the Makeup Alley forum and pre-YouTube beauty bloggers with popularising "my lips but better" and, less often, "your lips but better". The trademark record sides with the forum: Edward Bess registered "my lips but better" in 2013, It Cosmetics registered "your lips but better" in 2015, and L'Oréal previously owned both marks.

Clinique's connection is a product, not a slogan: Almost Lipstick in Black Honey, sold since the 1971 launch era, is the shade most often cited as the perfect version of the look. Make-up artists trace the aesthetic to eighteenth- and nineteenth-century ideals of rosy, "nubile and vital" lips, reincarnated as Bobbi Brown's "no-makeup makeup" in the 1990s and revived in the 2010s through K-beauty and Glossier.

The commercial engine of the decade was a format, not a colour. Kylie Jenner launched the Kylie Lip Kit on 30 November 2015 at $29 for a matte liquid lipstick and matching pencil in three shades. The initial run was 5,000 units per shade; it sold out within minutes and the site crashed within the hour. By September 2017 CNBC reported a $420 million business built from a $29 product.

## 2020 — the return of the committed red

The natural experiment arrived with the mask. UK lipstick sales fell about 49% between August and October 2020, a fall NPD Group linked to mask mandates; UK spend had previously exceeded £100 million a year. Lip make-up's share of prestige cosmetics fell from 14% before lockdown to 12% after, while eye make-up rose from 22% to 25% and brow products from 4% to 5%.

A Poshly survey found 60% wore lipstick most of the time before the pandemic, falling to 23% in summer 2020. Amazon's lip care and colour sales dropped 15% in the four weeks to 11 April 2020, with prices down 28%. McKinsey projected a 20–30% decline for a global beauty market, and L'Oréal UK suggested the shift might be "permanent".

By 2024 Trendalytics recorded dark red lipstick up 30% year on year, cherry red up 45% and lip stains up 53%. A Perspectus Global poll of 2,000 British women aged eighteen to thirty-five, reported in 2025, found 88% had recently worn red lipstick, with Taylor Swift the most-cited current icon at 29%. Fresha ranks "red lips" as the most-searched make-up trend at 134,180 monthly searches.

The reds the revival rests on have contested dates. Dior's Rouge Dior 999 is dated by sources to 1949, when it was numbered 9, or to 1953, when it appeared on models at a Dior show; MAC's Ruby Woo dates to 1999 and began as a limited edition in the Retro Matte range.

The 2020 numbers describe not a fashion cycle but a visibility cycle. The red lip fell when half the face was covered and returned when faces came back. The industry's retreats into the nude have never lasted, because a nude cannot do the job red was invented for: announcing that someone has chosen to be looked at.
