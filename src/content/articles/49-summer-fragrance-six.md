Summer fragrance has a reputation before it has a smell. It is supposed to be light, supposed to be citrus, supposed to last the length of a lunch — a thinner, brighter version of what you wear in November. The six people who answered do not describe it that way. The answers do not overlap.

The evidence sits with the professionals. In 2025 a study in the *International Journal of Cosmetic Science* measured the evaporation of eight fragrance molecules on the forearms of ten volunteers, and found that skin hydration mattered most: hydrated skin slowed the loss, roughness released the volatile molecules faster. Skin pH, skin temperature and sebum — the variables the trade treats as decisive — barely mattered. Summer is a solvent problem, not a temperature problem.

History agrees, in a way the industry does not enjoy. When Coco Chanel met Ernest Beaux in the summer of 1920 she asked for a fragrance like freshly washed linen, and Beaux, who had spent five years in Arkhangelsk, built her a composition with aldehydes at an estimated 0.7 to 1.2 per cent, several times the era's level. The result was Chanel No. 5, launched on 5 May 1921 — the profession's first great modern fragrance was a laundry scent.

What follows is a transcription of those conversations, edited for length and ordered by the shape of the disagreement rather than by chronology.

---

d-Limonene boils at 176 °C, linalyl acetate at 220 °C, geraniol at 229 — and limonene is roughly two to three times more volatile than the monoterpene alcohols, which is why it carries a citrus top note. Skin sits near 37 °C; a blotter sits at room temperature.

## 1. Olivia Mercier — Perfumer, Grasse

**Olivia Mercier — Perfumer, Grasse**

**What is the summer fragrance, in your practice?**

A construction problem, and the construction does not begin with the citrus. A top note has the least time to work in any formula — it leaves the bottle first and the skin first — so I am not making a lighter scent for July but a heavier base around a top note that will be gone before lunch. Hedione, Ambroxan, white musks, ambrette, Iso E Super decide what is still on a shoulder at four o'clock.

My June briefs trouble me. A client asks for freshness and sunshine, gets a day of citrus erased by one warm morning, and decides the house made a bad perfume. I use vacuum-distilled furocoumarin-free bergamot, because the cold-pressed oil carries a phototoxic load; I buy lime distilled rather than expressed; and I hold Calone below the point where it turns to wet metal and supermarket shampoo.

---

The second problem is verbal. A solar accord is not a standardised accord; it is a marketing category with no fixed referent. What a house sells as solar is one of three things — an ozonic note built on petrichor, a marine note built on Calone, or a solar musk built on skin warmth and ambrette.

## 2. Inès Bonnet — Beauty Editor, Paris

**Inès Bonnet — Beauty Editor, Paris**

**What does a summer fragrance promise?**

A mood, and the mood does the work. When a brand tells me a fragrance is solar, I do not ask what is in it; I ask which of the three they have meant, because the answer decides whether the bottle smells like a wet street, a swimming pool or a hot arm. A reader who buys the first and receives the second concludes the perfume was cheap, when the two were discussing different objects.

What I object to is the word *cologne*, which I would retire, because it now carries two incompatible meanings: a heritage recipe that happens to be citrus-based and precisely built, and a synonym for anything thin. Put those two in one sentence and the assumption about summer is rebuilt every time a shop window refreshes.

---

The history starts in Cologne. Giovanni Maria Farina launched his *Eau de Cologne* in 1709 from a shop opposite the Jülichplatz, describing an Italian spring morning of daffodils and orange blossom after rain. Free trade in Cologne from 1797 flooded the market with imitations, which is how the name became generic — and survives in the Ottoman Empire as Kolonya.

## 3. Pierre Dubois — Nose, Paris

**Pierre Dubois — Nose, Paris**

**What is the summer fragrance, historically?**

A formula of six materials with a green spine, which the industry has largely forgotten. Farina's blueprint was bergamot, lemon, sweet orange, neroli, rosemary and lavender — not a fruit spritz, but a built aromatic with herbs holding the citrus in place. Neroli and lavender do not announce themselves; they stop the whole thing collapsing into a novelty.

I would also correct the received history, because perfumers repeat it and it is wrong. Calone was not invented in 1951; that number is an internal compound reference, not a date. The molecule was synthesised at Pfizer's New York laboratories in the mid-1960s and took its name from a Grasse house. The marine note now sold as summer is a 1990s idea in a costume.

---

The law is silent. Regulation 1223/2009 names eau de cologne, eau de toilette and extrait de parfum only in a recital; a petition asking for the thresholds was told none are set. Convention puts extrait at fifteen to thirty per cent and cologne at two to five, and the bands overlap: a sixteen per cent parfum and a sixteen per cent extrait are the same liquid under two names.

## 4. Camille Lambert — Beauty Director, London

**Camille Lambert — Beauty Director, London**

**What is the summer fragrance, in your buying?**

The summer fragrance, as a category on my planogram, does not exist. What arrives in March is a recasting: the same juice in a different bottle and liquid colour, a summer name, a summer price. What a buyer does in the spring is adjust a concentration and shrink a size, not have a new idea about summer.

And the names on the box tell the customer almost nothing. When two labels describe the same liquid, the names are a price signal — and the woman choosing between two concentrations is choosing between two prices. What I would like to sell is a fragrance that behaves well when the wearer is hot.

---

The buyer's problem is arithmetic. Spain is mid-priced, not cheap. An October 2026 survey of official perfume-house stores priced Lancôme's La Vie Est Belle in 100ml at €139 in Germany, €156 in Spain and €177.78 in Hungary — a spread of nearly twenty-eight per cent that VAT does not explain. The mechanics: a pump delivers about 0.1 ml, so 100ml is roughly a thousand sprays and twenty to forty per cent cheaper per millilitre than 50ml.

## 5. Sara Beltran — Fragrance Buyer, Madrid

**Sara Beltran — Fragrance Buyer, Madrid**

**What should a customer buy for July?**

A packaging decision with a fragrance attached. The summer buyer wants something for July without committing to a full bottle of a full scent, so the industry offers a smaller volume at the same price per millilitre or worse, and calls it a season. I would rather sell one large bottle of the right juice than three small ones of the wrong: the unit economics punish the small bottle.

I part company with my colleagues over the word. The customer in Madrid in July is not after a different smell but one that does not make her feel hot. So the summer fragrance, on my floor, is whatever performs best when the wearer is perspiring — and if that answer is a heavier concentration, the honest packaging is the big bottle of the eau de parfum, with the summer on the copy and not in the liquid.

---

Then the part of summer nobody bills. IFRA's 51st Amendment, notified in June 2023, caps expressed bergamot at 0.4 per cent of a leave-on product, and a separate standard caps total furocoumarins at fifteen parts per million in the finished product. Since July 2026 the EU has also required bergamot oil and linalyl acetate on the label above a tenth of a per cent in a thousand, sunscreens included; sun products get no exemption.

## 6. Mathilde Vidal — Esthetician, Paris

**Mathilde Vidal — Esthetician, Paris**

**What does the summer fragrance do to a client?**

In my treatment room it is a clinical variable, and July is when it does most damage. My clients come back from a fortnight of sun, exfoliation and swimming with a barrier thinned twice over, then put a strong fragrance on it, under an occlusive product, on skin just treated. Occlusion plus a sensitising load is the worst combination available. The reaction does not arrive that afternoon but twenty-four to seventy-two hours later, by which point nobody connects it to the perfume.

So the summer fragrance I defend is the one that is not there, or not on freshly treated skin. I read the INCI list for fragrance, parfum, aroma, and then for the allergens the EU now names individually: linalool, limonene, geraniol, cinnamal, eugenol. *Fragrance-free* is not *unscented*, and *natural* is not safe — the essential oils called gentler are among the most reliable sensitisers on the shelf. I cannot tell a client whether a rash is allergy or irritation, so I document and refer.

---

Put the six side by side and the disagreement turns out not to be about taste. The perfumer treats summer as an anchoring problem, the editor as a problem of language, the nose as a problem of inheritance, the buyer as a margin question, the director as a question nobody asked, the esthetician as a question of barrier.

Two findings settle the rest. Humidity, not heat, is the better test of longevity: damp air slows evaporation, so a heat-humid formula outlasts a dry-heat one, the opposite of the folk advice that muggy weather shortens wear. And what makes a citrus scent smell wrong is air, not heat.

Limonene and linalool are not themselves allergenic, but they autoxidise into hydroperoxides several times more potent as sensitisers — which is why a Swedish series of 5,773 dermatitis patients found oxidised linalool in 7.0 per cent of reactions and oxidised limonene 5.1.

Which leaves the cheap-looking answer. The reader reaches for the light citrus cologne because it is brief, cheap and unchallenging, and on this evidence it is wrong twice over: it is the formulation least able to survive a hot day, and its top notes turn into the sensitiser an esthetician will spend August treating.

Farina's cologne of 1709 was built to outlast the day. Two or three sprays, on cooler skin, over an unscented balm, never rubbed. The instinct to buy something heavier in July is not an affectation; it is the one piece of folk advice the evidence keeps.
